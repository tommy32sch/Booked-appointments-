Patient assessment overview — detailed

This recap is the map you run on every call: from the doorway to the hospital bed.

The order you actually work NREMT

Scene size-up. Primary assessment to find and treat life threats. History with OPQRST and SAMPLE. Secondary exam plus a full set of vitals. Field impression and the treatments that match it. Reassessment until you hand them off.

Skip a block and you skip a job. Primary is universal. History waits until ABCs are handled and they can answer.

Scene — you have not touched them yet NREMT

BSI and PPE first. Is the scene safe for you, your partner, the patient, and bystanders? Trauma MOI or medical NOI? How many patients? Do you need fire, police, ALS, extra units?

Call law early if you suspect abuse or violence, or if the scene feels unsafe or is heating up.

Primary — life threats, then priority NREMT

General impression: sick or not, major bleeding, obvious distress, trauma vs medical, age, how they are sitting or lying.

Level of consciousness is AVPU. If they are Alert, check orientation: person, place, time, event. Alert and oriented ×4 usually means decision-making capacity and expressed consent. Confused, verbal, pain, or unresponsive usually means implied consent.

Airway: open and clear? Upper-airway sounds you hear without a stethoscope: snoring (tongue), gurgling (fluid — suction), stridor (upper swelling, think anaphylaxis). Fix it: open, suction, adjuncts per protocol.

Breathing is rate, rhythm, quality. Adult normal 12–20. Watch chest rise, accessory muscles, nasal flaring, retractions. Lower-airway sounds need the chest piece: wheezing is bronchoconstriction (asthma), high-pitched, often on the way out; crackles are fluid in alveoli (pulmonary edema), wet bubbling; rhonchi are mucus/congestion (pneumonia), low rattling. Then you decide oxygen, ventilation support, and how fast you leave.

Circulation: pulse rate, rhythm, quality. Adult 60–100. Skin color, temperature, condition. Cap refill especially in kids. Major bleeding: stop it the moment you see it.

Critical trauma gets a rapid head-to-toe scan for other life threats. Then you decide load-and-go versus more time on scene.

History NREMT

OPQRST for the present illness, especially pain or breathing: when and what they were doing; what makes it better or worse; what it feels like; where it is and if it travels; 0–10; how long and whether it is changing.

SAMPLE is the wider history: signs and symptoms, allergies, medications, past medical history, last oral intake, events leading up.

Ask about alcohol and drugs calmly. You are not law enforcement; you need the truth to treat them. If they deny and you still suspect it, you may ask once more, then drop it and write “Patient denies alcohol or drug use.” At EMT level most treatments do not swing on undisclosed use. If they lie and the outcome is bad, you are not liable for reasonable decisions based on what you had.

Suspected abuse (child, elder, domestic) or a serious safety worry is a mandatory report. Reporting a suspicion is not defamation even if it later is untrue. Defamation is you stating a false fact (“That guy IS a child abuser”). Document observations and quotes, then report the suspicion.

Charts must be objective, verifiable, and true. You can write what you see and what someone said in quotes. You do not write who is guilty.

Any female of childbearing age with lower abdominal pain is possibly pregnant. Ask if there is any chance and when the last period was. Document the denial and the date they give. With abdominal complaints also ask nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and bowel or bladder changes. Sexual history and STIs only if they are clinically relevant (fever plus belly pain, GU symptoms).

Secondary NREMT

Hands-on, on scene or in the box once primary and history are moving.

Focused exam for a responsive medical patient who can localize the problem. Detailed head-to-toe for the unconscious (medical or trauma), significant MOI even if they are awake (ejection, high speed, big fall), or when you think injuries are multiple or hidden.

Inspect, palpate, auscultate. DCAP-BTLS on each region: deformities, contusions, abrasions, punctures/penetrations, burns, tenderness, lacerations, swelling. Compare sides — lungs, limbs, grips.

Vitals and neuro NREMT

Adult respirations 12–20; younger kids run faster. Adult pulse 60–100; kids higher. Rate, rhythm, quality.

Adult systolic 90–120. Under 90 is hypotension and a shock concern. 120–130 is the hypertension range they gave. In emergencies you care most about systolic.

Under 10 years, minimum systolic is 2 × age in years + 70. A 4-year-old is 78. At 10 the formula hits 90 and you use the adult cutoff.

GCS: eyes 1–4, verbal 1–5, motor 1–6. Total 3–15. Fifteen is fully alert and oriented. Three is no eye, verbal, or motor. Memorize the table.

Pupils: PERRL — equal, round, reactive to light. Both constrict together. Note unequal, fixed and dilated, sluggish. Pinpoint often opioids/downers. Dilated often stimulants or brain injury.

SpO₂ is hemoglobin saturation. Want 94–99%. Carbon monoxide can look falsely high because the machine treats CO as occupied hemoglobin. It does not tell you how many red cells exist.

Glucose 80–120 mg/dL. Under 80 is low. Check the confused, altered, or unconscious, known diabetics, and anyone who could be stroke, intoxication, or hypoglycemia — they mimic each other.

Impression, treatments, reassess, handoff NREMT

After scene, primary, history, and secondary plus vitals, you say what you think is going on: suspected ACS/MI, hypoglycemia, asthma flare, stroke, shock from bleeding. That is a field impression, not a formal diagnosis.

Treat ABCs first — position, oxygen, ventilation, bleeding. Then the EMT-scope meds your protocols allow. On a test, if one option is assist ventilations and another is give a drug, and breathing is inadequate, you ventilate first.

Reassess mental status/GCS, airway, breathing, circulation, vitals, and whether treatments worked. Unstable about every 5 minutes. Stable about every 15.

Handoff is short: scene and chief complaint, primary findings and life threats, OPQRST and SAMPLE highlights, exam and vitals with trends, GCS and pupils, what you gave and what it did.

Practice

  • Do this: Recite the six-step flow without looking. You did it right if you said scene, primary, history, secondary, impression/interventions, reassessment — in that order.
  • Do this: Name the five scene tasks plus when you call law. You did it right if you have BSI, safety, MOI/NOI, number, resources, and law for abuse or an escalating scene.
  • Do this: Pair upper-airway sounds you hear without a stethoscope. You did it right if snoring is tongue, gurgling is fluid and suction, stridor is swelling.
  • Do this: Give adult RR, pulse, systolic band, and the peds hypotension formula. You did it right if 12–20, 60–100, 90–120, and 2×age+70 under age 10.
  • Do this: Score a fully alert patient and a no-response patient on GCS. You did it right if 15 (4+5+6) and 3.
  • Do this: Pick focused vs detailed for a talking belly-pain patient vs an ejection. You did it right if focused for the first and detailed for the second.
  • Do this: Write one chart line for a bruise and one for a quote. You did it right if the bruise is what you see and the quote is in their words, not “he is an abuser.”
  • Do this: Choose ventilate vs give a drug when breathing is inadequate. You did it right if you ventilate first.

Reassessment — detailed

Once the first assessment and the first treatments are in and you are rolling, the job is not over. Ongoing reassessment is the fifth piece of patient assessment.

The clocks NREMT

Unstable or critical patients get a full reassess about every 5 minutes. Practically, when you finish one pass it is almost time for the next. Stable patients get about every 15 minutes.

Unstable in this recap includes shock, severe trauma, chest pain with concerning signs, altered mental status. Stable means normal vitals, no bad trends, improving.

What you actually reassess NREMT

Airway: still open, still protected? Breathing: rate, depth, quality, work of breathing, and breath sounds if they apply. Circulation: pulse rate/rhythm/quality, skin, new or ongoing major bleeding. Mental status: AVPU, orientation to person/place/time/event, new confusion, agitation, lethargy, unresponsiveness.

Then a new full vital set: blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, SpO₂ if you have it, glucose if indicated, pain 0–10. Compare to the last set. Better, worse, or the same?

One set is a snapshot. Two or more sets are a trend. Falling blood pressure with rising heart rate and respiratory rate can mean they are heading toward shock. Numbers that barely move mean the condition is at least not clearly getting worse or better.

When the picture flips NREMT

Any significant change — better or worse — sends you back to the beginning of primary assessment: mental status, then airway, then breathing, then circulation.

Example: you were talking; they go unresponsive. You do not keep chatting through SAMPLE. You restart MS → A → B → C.

Example: you were doing CPR and you get a pulse back. Stop compressions. Then reassess mental status, airway, breathing, and circulation. ROSC is not automatic good breathing. The heart can restart while they still need you to ventilate.

Logic: the biggest life threats first, every time the patient changes.

Recheck everything you already did NREMT

If you did it, you re-check it. Is it still effective? Is it still safe?

Tourniquet: bleeding still held, still sitting right, not too loose or too tight. Dressings: soaking through?

Splints: swelling can turn a correct splint into a tourniquet. Distal pulse, motor, sensation. Cap refill and color past the splint. Can they wiggle fingers or toes? New numbness, tingling, or severe compression pain? Loosen or adjust per protocol if it is too tight.

Medications: for chest pain (nitro, aspirin where appropriate) ask the new pain number against the old one. Eight to five is progress. Watch for adverse effects. After glucose, recheck the sugar. After oxygen or a breathing med, recheck SpO₂, distress, breath sounds, work of breathing.

Restraints: still on, not injuring them. Distal circulation, sensation, movement. Warm, pink, normal cap refill. They can still feel and move fingers and toes.

Test-world meds NREMT

This recap splits book/National Registry world from the street.

On exams, assume you need an online order unless the question already says you have orders or your protocol authorizes the drug. The safe pick is contact medical control for permission before you give it.

If the stem already gave you the order, do not pick the “call first” answer.

On the street many meds are standing offline orders. That is not the default you use on the test.

Sequence NREMT

The order inside an answer choice matters. Administer → contact medical control → reassess has the right chores in the wrong order. On the test that is wrong.

Correct default sequence: contact medical control, administer if approved, reassess the patient.

Do not ask “are these steps roughly right?” Ask “is this the correct order?”

The point of the program NREMT

Later chapters will walk anatomy, specific illnesses, and a tailored assessment. The core goal stays: make a field impression and do the right treatments. Reassessment, ABC checks, and vital trends feed that question: what is going on with my patient, and what am I going to do?

Practice

  • Do this: Say the two clocks out loud. You did it right if unstable is 5 minutes and stable is 15.
  • Do this: List what one full reassess pass includes. You did it right if you have mental status, ABCs, a new full vital set, and every intervention you already did.
  • Do this: Read two BP/HR/RR sets that are sliding. You did it right if you called the second set a trend toward shock, not a snapshot.
  • Do this: Act ROSC after CPR. You did it right if you stopped compressions and rechecked mental status, airway, breathing, and circulation — and you said they may still need ventilations.
  • Do this: Recheck a splint after swelling. You did it right if you named distal pulse, motor, sensation, cap refill, and new numbness or severe pain.
  • Do this: Ask a chest-pain pain score after nitro. You did it right if you compared now to the first number (8 to 5 is improvement).
  • Do this: Pick the exam med sequence when the stem does not give orders. You did it right if you contact medical control, then give the drug, then reassess.
  • Do this: Cross out an option that has the right three steps in the wrong order. You did it right if you treated that option as wrong.

Respiratory physiology and assessment — detailed

This recap is how air moves, how gas trades, and how you decide “they are breathing well enough” versus “you have to bag.”

After the scene is safe, after a massive bleed is handled, and after you know they are alive, airway is next. If you see a life threat — a huge bleed or a wrecked airway — you treat it now.

Three words you keep in this order NREMT

Ventilation is the mechanical part: is air going in and out? You look at rate, rhythm, quality, and chest rise.

Respiration is the chemistry at the membrane: oxygen and carbon dioxide swapping. First at the alveoli and the lung capillaries, later between blood and cells.

Oxygenation is oxygen parking on hemoglobin on red cells. Pulse oximetry is an indirect look at that.

If they look terrible but air is actually moving, you do not stop at “ventilation is fine.” You go deeper. First suspect a gas-exchange problem (fluid, pneumonia, COPD). Then an oxygenation problem (the sat, the hemoglobin). Then circulation (shock, heart).

Upper and lower airway NREMT

Upper is everything above the larynx: nasal cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, epiglottis, larynx and vocal cords. That stretch warms, filters, and humidifies. What goes wrong here is tongue, swelling from allergy or infection, trauma, blood, or vomit.

Lower is below the larynx: trachea, then bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli. The job down there is gas exchange — oxygen in, carbon dioxide out.

Phrenic nerve NREMT

The line to remember is “C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive.” A high neck injury above that level can paralyze the diaphragm so they cannot breathe. A lower neck injury (below C5) can still leave them paralyzed in the body, but they may still breathe because the phrenic nerve is intact.

How a normal breath works versus a bag NREMT

A normal inhale is work. Diaphragm and intercostals contract. The chest cavity gets bigger. Pressure inside the chest drops below the outside air. Air is sucked in. Exhale is usually free: the muscles let go, the cavity shrinks, air flows out.

When you ventilate them with a BVM you reverse that. You push air in — positive pressure. That can shove air into the stomach and make them vomit. Squeeze too hard and you can overinflate and pop a lung.

Volumes at EMT level NREMT

Tidal volume is one ordinary breath. You do not need a milliliter number. You judge it by watching the chest rise and fall.

Inspiratory and expiratory reserve are the extra you could force in or out if you tried.

Residual volume is what stays in the alveoli after a full exhale. That leftover air keeps the sacs from collapsing between breaths.

Why they breathe NREMT

The main drive is carbon dioxide building up. Central sensors feel the high CO₂. The brainstem tells the diaphragm to contract through the phrenic nerve.

The backup is hypoxic drive — low oxygen telling them to breathe. That becomes important later in severe COPD, when CO₂ lives high all the time and stops being a trustworthy signal.

Adequate versus inadequate (adult) NREMT

Adequate breathing: rate 12–20 a minute, regular rhythm, good tidal volume (you see the chest move), clear equal breath sounds on both sides.

A little “off” can still be adequate. Example from this recap: 22 a minute but good volume, clear sounds, they are alert.

Inadequate: rate under 12 or over 20 with poor volume or an irregular rhythm; almost no chest rise; heavy retractions and accessory muscles; agonal gasps, no breathing, or a chaotic pattern; cyanosis, a bad mental status, a bad sat.

Patterns you must name NREMT

Agonal gasps are rare, gasping, spastic breaths in cardiac arrest. That is not effective breathing. You do CPR and you ventilate.

Cheyne–Stokes is a cycle: slow and shallow, then deeper and faster, then it slows, then a pause, then it repeats. Often brain injury. A bad sign.

Biot’s / ataxic is completely chaotic — no pattern. Think severe brainstem.

Kussmaul is deep and rapid. You see it with metabolic acidosis (DKA, aspirin overdose). The body is trying to blow off acid. Do not reflexively slow it by over-ventilating them.

Chest problems to recognize NREMT

Pneumothorax: air in the thorax but not inside the lung — a collapsed lung. Trauma, a broken rib, a puncture, or you bagged too hard.

Hemothorax: blood in the chest cavity.

Hemopneumothorax: both.

A severe one-sided problem can leave one lung doing all the work.

Oxygen and the numbers NREMT

Pulse ox reports the percent of hemoglobin that is saturated. Aim 94–99%. Reasons to put oxygen on: sat under 94, they are having trouble breathing, or they look like shock.

The probe can lie: anemia, hypovolemia, cold fingers, nail polish, strong room light. Carbon monoxide is the special trap — the sat can read normal or 100% because hemoglobin is full, but it is full of CO, not oxygen.

Capnography (end-tidal CO₂) is more of a medic tool, but the idea matters. A good ETCO₂ means ventilation is happening, gas is exchanging, blood is circulating, and cells are actually using oxygen and making CO₂. A shark-fin waveform is the classic “hard to get air out” picture — asthma and COPD.

How you actually check them NREMT

On every primary: look for the obvious massive bleed, severe distress, cyanosis, altered mind, trauma to face, neck, or chest.

Airway: talking clearly usually means the path is open. Listen for snoring, gurgling, stridor, or silence. Look for blood, vomit, secretions, a foreign body. If it is not open: jaw thrust if you worry about trauma, head-tilt chin-lift if you do not. Suction gurgling and fluids. Use an OPA or NPA when it fits.

Breathing: count 30 seconds and double it (adult goal 12–20). Note regular vs irregular and the named patterns. Watch rise, shallow vs normal, accessory muscles, retractions, tripod, nasal flaring, pursed lips. Listen if you have time: clear and equal, or diminished, crackles, wheezes, absent.

Then oxygenation and perfusion: pulse ox if you have it and it does not delay a killer. Sat under 94 or distress/shock means treat. Skin color (pink vs pale, blue, mottled, flushed), warm vs cool, dry vs clammy.

Fix the airway (position, suction, adjunct). Then choose oxygen versus ventilations. Expose the chest fast for an open wound, flail, or a hole.

The oxygen-versus-bag fork NREMT

Are they breathing?

No, or only agonal gasps: call for more help and an AED. If they have no pulse, start CPR and bag with oxygen.

Yes, but inadequate: get ready to assist with a BVM and oxygen. Open the airway, add an adjunct if it belongs. Good seal, right-size mask. Slow, gentle breaths — just enough to see the chest rise. Do not crush the bag. That is how you get a pneumothorax, a stomach full of air, and vomit. Rate follows your protocol; this recap’s common adult-with-a-pulse number is about 10–12 a minute. Recheck rise, skin, mind, sat.

Yes, and the mechanics are adequate, but they are still sick or hypoxic — short of breath, sat under 94, shock, chest pain, distress: hold the airway open and give supplemental oxygen. Which mask or cannula is local protocol. Watch rate, rhythm, quality, the sat trend, and mental status.

When you do not linger NREMT

Red flags for rapid transport / ALS: you think pneumothorax or hemothorax (dyspnea plus one silent side, with or without trauma); severe distress that basics did not fix; significant head or neck trauma with a weird breathing pattern; Kussmaul with DKA or poisoning signs; anyone who needs continuous assisted ventilations.

Difficulty-breathing dispatch NREMT

Primary first. Then focused questions: sudden vs gradual; what makes it better or worse (lying flat, exertion); tight, burning, cannot get air in versus cannot get air out; chest pain, cough, fever, wheeze, swelling, trauma; asthma, COPD, CHF, recent illness, diabetes; inhalers, home oxygen, diuretics, insulin, blood thinners.

Look at position (tripod? full sentences?), chest wall symmetry and trauma, lung sounds, swelling in the legs, JVD, cyanosis. Give oxygen or a bag as above. Help with their prescribed inhaler if protocol allows. Move if any red flag is up.

Practice

  • Do this: Name the order after safety. You did it right if massive bleed and “are they alive” come before airway, and a catastrophic airway is still “see it, treat it.”
  • Do this: Say ventilation vs respiration vs oxygenation in one breath each. You did it right if ventilation is air moving, respiration is gas exchange, oxygenation is O₂ on hemoglobin (pulse ox).
  • Do this: They look terrible but chest rise is good. You did it right if you work respiration first (fluid / pneumonia / COPD), then sat/hemoglobin, then shock/heart — not “bag harder.”
  • Do this: Point C3–4–5. You did it right if above that level means the diaphragm is out, and below C5 they may still breathe.
  • Do this: Mime a normal breath, then a bag squeeze. You did it right if inhale is negative (cavity bigger, air pulled in) and a bag is positive (you push; stomach air and popped lung if you over-squeeze).
  • Do this: Judge tidal volume without a number. You did it right if you used visible chest rise, and residual volume is the leftover that keeps alveoli open.
  • Do this: Recite the adult adequate box. You did it right if 12–20, regular, good rise, clear equal sounds — and 22 with good volume and an alert patient can still be adequate.
  • Do this: Name agonal vs Cheyne–Stokes vs Biot’s vs Kussmaul. You did it right if agonal is arrest (CPR + bag), Cheyne–Stokes is the wax-wane-pause (brain), Biot’s is chaos (brainstem), Kussmaul is deep-fast (acidosis — do not slam it).
  • Do this: Read a sat of 100% on a house-fire patient. You did it right if you did not trust it as proof of oxygen — CO can fill the seats.
  • Do this: Decide bag vs mask O₂. You did it right if no breath / agonal / poor volume gets a bag, and a breathing-but-hypoxic or shocked patient gets supplemental O₂ with the airway held.
  • Do this: Bag an adult who still has a pulse. You did it right if the squeeze only makes visible rise, you did not blast the stomach, and the rate is in the local ~10–12 range.

Airway and suction — detailed

This recap is unconscious-airway work and suction. The mindset is simple: always fix the airway first. You cannot assess or fix breathing if air cannot reach the lungs.

Talking vs down NREMT

If they are conscious and talking or crying, treat the airway as patent. Say “airway check” and move on to breathing.

If they are unconscious, assume they are not protecting the airway. The muscles relax. The tongue falls back and can fully obstruct. Do not grade breathing until you have opened and cleared, or you will miss a simple blockage.

Where you stand NREMT

Preferred: patient supine, you at the head of the bed. That is the control position.

Awake people often sit themselves up. That is them protecting their own airway.

If you cannot lay them flat — trapped in a car — you may need an urgent or extrication move. You can give up a perfect spine package to treat an immediate airway or breathing threat.

Which opener NREMT

Head-tilt–chin-lift when you do not suspect a spinal cord injury. Forehead and bony chin. Tilt so the tongue comes off the posterior pharynx. Easier, more effective, easier to keep. Use it for the elderly found unconscious in bed, or a witnessed collapse on a couch with no fall or trauma.

Jaw thrust when spine injury is suspected, possible, or you just are not sure — err on the side of caution. Fingers behind the angle of the jaw, thumbs on the cheekbones, jaw forward into an underbite, head and neck in line. It protects C-spine while it opens the airway. It is hard to hold. Let go and the jaw and tongue fall back and the obstruction is back.

Four steps in this lesson NREMT

Open. Assess (look in the mouth for blood, vomit, secretions, foreign material, teeth; listen for gurgling). Clear with suction if — and only if — you see that fluid or you hear gurgling. Maintain so the tongue does not re-block (adjuncts, position, more suction, ventilation). Maintain was slated for the next segment; the idea still belongs here.

Do not invent fluid on a test. If the stem never says fluid or gurgling, do not pick suction.

Opening the mouth does not need a ritual. Cross-finger technique is mostly a textbook move.

Units and catheters NREMT

Battery-powered portable unit is what you use most on scene. Wall-mounted unit in the ambulance or hospital. Hand-powered is the backup. It wears you out and material can drop back when you pause.

Rigid Yankauer is the oral default — larger opening for vomit, blood, chunky stuff. Flexible French / whistle-tip is thinner and clogs. Use it for a tracheostomy or down an endotracheal tube, not a mouth full of vomit.

Check the machine at the start of the shift: power, battery, charging, tubing and canister seated, vacuum present. The book number for adequate vacuum is greater than 300 mmHg. Some units have a gauge or light; others you occlude with a gloved finger.

Tubing, catheter, and canister are single-patient and disposable. They go in biohazard trash. You disinfect the machine body and restock sterile replacements. Plug it back in.

How you suction NREMT

Open and control the airway first with the correct maneuver. Stay at the head. Look and listen.

If indicated, turn the unit on and prove it works.

Measure corner of mouth to earlobe, or corner of mouth to the angle of the jaw. That measured length is the deepest you go.

Insert with the vent hole uncovered so you are not sucking on the way in. Only as far as you can see / that measured depth. Not into the trachea.

Cover the vent with a finger to start suction. Gentle circular motion while you withdraw. Never suction while you advance.

Suction until the airway is clear, up to 10 seconds. That ceiling is now for all ages — adult, child, infant.

If you will pass again, flush sterile water through the tubing so clots do not sit in the line.

Aspiration math and the least-bad choice NREMT

Aspiration means blood, vomit, or secretions reached the lungs. It raises mortality about 30–70 percent on top of whatever started the call.

If suction is not in your hand, roll them onto their side immediately. Gravity is the first tool. Sit a dyspneic patient up. Those position moves are often faster than digging for a machine.

If the catheter keeps clogging on thick vomit, stop repeating a failed pass. Log roll, drain, suction, scoop with the catheter — not a finger.

If they are still wet after a maxed pass: suction up to 10 seconds, ventilate about 2 minutes, repeat. Yes, bagging can shove leftover material down. That is aspiration risk. No oxygen is 100 percent mortality, which is worse. After you have used the suction window, you oxygenate.

When it is clear, say “airway check.” Then breathing: rate, rhythm, quality, oxygen or ventilations as indicated.

Practice

  • Do this: Point at a talking patient, then an unconscious one. You did it right if talking is “airway check, go to breathing,” and unconscious is assume not protected, open and clear first.
  • Do this: Pick tilt-chin vs jaw thrust for bed-found medical vs a wreck. You did it right if no-spine medical is tilt-chin and possible spine (or unsure) is jaw thrust.
  • Do this: Name the only two suction triggers. You did it right if you see fluid/blood/vomit/secretions or you hear gurgling — and you refused suction when the stem has neither.
  • Do this: Measure a rigid catheter on your own face. You did it right if it is corner of mouth to earlobe or angle of jaw, and you do not bury it in the trachea.
  • Do this: Mime insert vs withdraw. You did it right if the vent is open on the way in and you cover it and circle only on the way out, 10 seconds max for every age.
  • Do this: Choose portable vs wall vs hand, and rigid vs flexible. You did it right if scene is battery portable, oral vomit is rigid, trach/ET is flexible.
  • Do this: Vomit and no suction in your hand. You did it right if you log-roll to the side for gravity while a partner grabs the unit.
  • Do this: Still dirty after 10 seconds. You did it right if you ventilate about 2 minutes next, because no oxygen is 100% mortality.

OPA, NPA, and oxygen — detailed

This recap is how you keep the tongue out of the way and when you put oxygen on someone. Primary assessment is the immediate killers: airway, do they need CPR, is there major bleeding. That block plus treat and transport is about two-thirds of the test.

If they are unconscious and you have no bystanders, no medical ID, and no med list, you skip SAMPLE and OPQRST. There is no history to take. You go to the secondary assessment — the physical exam.

What the adjuncts do NREMT

This is the “maintain” step after you have opened and cleared. The tongue is the usual blocker. Two tools hold it off: an OPA (rigid, through the mouth) and an NPA (soft tube through the nose). Both sit just past the base of the tongue so air can still move even if the tongue is floppy.

OPA NREMT

You only put an OPA in someone who is fully out — AVPU = U. Not awake. No verbal. No pain. No gag.

If they gag when it goes in, they still have a gag reflex. Come out immediately and get ready for vomit.

Conscious or semi-conscious, or anyone with a gag: no OPA.

When they are truly out and have no gag, you like the OPA first. It holds the tongue off the back of the throat better than an NPA.

Size it from the corner of the mouth to the earlobe, or from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw.

Open the airway first with tilt-chin or jaw thrust. Then insert. Two methods both count as correct now: start upside down and rotate 180°, or start sideways and rotate 90°. Take it out along the curve. Do not push it straight in from the front — that drives the tongue down into the airway.

If they are in arrest and you are doing CPR, the muscles (and the tongue) are very relaxed. An OPA is appropriate if they are unresponsive and have no gag.

NPA NREMT

You use an NPA when they still need a path but they still have a gag — semi-conscious or even alert. You can also use it in an unresponsive patient who still gags. It usually does not poke the gag reflex the way an OPA does.

The hard contraindication: any head or facial trauma, especially a suspected basilar skull fracture. The tube can pass through a broken skull base into the brain. If you then bag them, you can force air into the cranial vault.

Rule of thumb: trauma to the head or face — OPA through the mouth is OK if there is no gag. NPA through the nose is no.

Decision: unresponsive, try OPA. If they gag, pull it. Think NPA only if there is no head or face trauma. If they have a gag and head trauma, you have no adjunct. You manage the airway with your hands and fight the tongue until a paramedic can place something more advanced.

Size: nose to earlobe, or nose to the angle of the jaw. Water-based medical lubricant. Bevel toward the septum (the middle wall). Insert gently, straight in; it follows the nasal curve and ends just past the tongue. Usually single-use, then discarded.

Recovery position NREMT

Unconscious, not injured (you do not suspect a spine injury), and breathing adequately on their own: roll them onto their side. That helps keep the airway open and lets secretions or vomit drain.

You see this less in EMS when they are deeply out, because those patients often need assisted ventilations, so you keep them on their back.

Oxygen — when and why NREMT

Give oxygen if the sat is under 94%, or they show respiratory distress (short of breath, increased work), or they show shock.

Most emergency patients: aim 94–99%.

If the sat looks “normal” but they are clearly in distress, treat the patient, not the number. If they might benefit and they look sick, do not withhold oxygen.

Too much oxygen over time can cause vasoconstriction — vessels narrow — and that can worsen a stroke or a heart attack. Oxygen toxicity is free oxygen with no hemoglobin left to bind it. It wanders and irritates tissue and adds to that vessel-tightening. COPD patients are more sensitive to high O₂; that gets a full chapter later. If they are clearly compromised, you still do not withhold.

Picture hemoglobin as seats in a stadium. Once every seat is full, extra ticket-holders have nowhere to sit and start trouble. That is free-floating oxygen.

Tanks, pins, flow NREMT

D tank is the small portable one you take to the patient or park on the gurney. H tank is the large, about five-foot tank mounted in the ambulance for the ride.

These cylinders sit around 2,000 psi. A car tire is around 30. Always strap them, lay them down, or mount them. Never let one fall or tip. If the valve shears, the tank is a missile that can punch through a wall.

Pin indexing: each gas has its own hole pattern on the tank. Oxygen tanks (green/silver) have their own. The regulator’s pins only fit that pattern, so an oxygen regulator will not go on the wrong tank and you do not fill a tank with nitrous, CO₂, or helium by accident.

The regulator’s job is to drop that ~2,000 psi down to about 40–70 psi. Then the flow meter sets liters per minute.

Bourdon gauge works in any position, which is why it is good on portable tanks you lay down. A pressure-compensated meter is the vertical-tube style. It has to stay upright, so it is usually on the wall in the ambulance or hospital.

Non-rebreather NREMT

At EMT level, the NRB is the preferred high-concentration device.

They have to be breathing adequately — good rate and tidal volume — but they are hypoxic (sat under 94%), blue, or short of breath / in distress. Their ventilation is fine. They just need more oxygen than 21% room air.

Set 10–15 L/min. The book and the test use that range. People often run 12–15. For the test, if they need oxygen and they are breathing well, think NRB at 10–15 unless the question clearly wants another device.

The tank is 100% oxygen. They actually get about 90–95% because the mask never seals perfectly and room air leaks in. That is still a huge jump from 21%.

Confirm it is an oxygen tank (green/silver). Secure it. Pins match. Crack it, read the gauge (full is about 2,000). Set the liters. Put the mask on. Watch sat trend, color, effort, and mental status. Aim 94–99%. If they improve and stay stable you can ease the flow down carefully. If they worsen, recheck the airway, whether they now need a BVM or a higher-level airway, and how fast you need to leave.

Practice

  • Do this: Recite when you skip SAMPLE. You did it right if unconscious plus no bystanders / ID / med list means skip history and go to the physical exam.
  • Do this: Say who gets an OPA. You did it right if only AVPU = U with no gag, and gag on insert means pull it now and expect vomit.
  • Do this: Measure an OPA and an NPA on your face. You did it right if OPA is mouth-corner to ear or jaw angle, and NPA is nose to ear or jaw angle.
  • Do this: Mime both OPA inserts. You did it right if you open the airway first, then 180° from upside-down or 90° from the side — never a straight shove that drives the tongue down.
  • Do this: Say the NPA hard stop. You did it right if any head or face trauma (especially basilar skull) is no NPA, even if they need an adjunct.
  • Do this: Gag plus facial trauma — pick your tools. You did it right if you use neither adjunct and you hold the jaw/tongue by hand.
  • Do this: Name three reasons to give O₂. You did it right if sat under 94, distress (even with a “good” number), or shock — target 94–99%.
  • Do this: Set up an NRB on a breathing, hypoxic patient. You did it right if 10–15 L/min and you did not pick a cannula just because they look “pretty okay.”
  • Do this: Point at D vs H, Bourdon vs upright tube, and a loose tank. You did it right if D is portable, H is the big mounted tank, Bourdon works lying down, compensated must stand, and an unsecured tank is a missile at ~2,000 psi.

Oxygen devices, BVM, CPAP, choking — detailed

Almost everything in this recap is two questions you answer before you pick a toy.

Is the airway open and clear? If not, you open it (head tilt–chin lift or jaw thrust), you suction, you use an OPA or NPA, and you manage whatever is blocking it.

Are they breathing adequately — rate, rhythm, and quality? If yes, you give supplemental oxygen, and on the test that is usually a non-rebreather. If no, or they are not breathing at all, you give positive pressure with a bag. Special case: they are still breathing on their own but they are in severe distress with fluid in the lungs — that is when you consider CPAP, and only if they meet the rules.

Cannula vs NRB vs CPAP vs BVM is never a first question. It is the device that matches those two answers.

Non-rebreather NREMT

You run it at 10–15 L/min. They get about 90–95% oxygen. The side flaps are one-way: exhaled air leaves, they do not suck it back, and they pull almost pure oxygen from the bag on the mask.

You can put it on someone who is awake or someone who is out, as long as they are still taking adequate breaths on their own.

Why start high: too little oxygen is an immediate killer. Too much (toxicity) is a longer-term problem.

National Registry world: if they are breathing well enough and they have respiratory distress or hypoxia, NRB at 10–15 L/min is almost always the answer over a cannula.

You use it when rate, rhythm, and quality are adequate, and they have difficulty breathing, or SpO₂ under 94%, or signs of shock.

Nasal cannula NREMT

1–6 L/min. About 24–44% oxygen. One liter is about 24% — barely above 21% room air. Six liters is about 44%.

On the street you use it for a mild bump. On the test it is often not even a choice, or it is rarely the correct one.

Above 6 L/min is very dry and irritating — “nose burning.” What you actually set most of the time is 2–4 L/min.

Real-world examples: sat 92% but they look comfortable, minor shortness of breath, or they already live on home oxygen and do not need high flow right now.

Partial rebreather NREMT

Think of a non-rebreather with the side flaps taken off. Once the mask is on the face you still run 10–15 L/min. They get about 80–90% because more room air leaks in. Rare. Sometimes talked about for hyperventilation / panic when they do not truly need high oxygen but they get some.

Venturi, trach mask, humidified oxygen NREMT

A Venturi mask uses color-coded adapters. Each adapter is a set flow plus a precise percent (24%, 28%, 32%, and so on). That is for long-term, tightly controlled oxygen in chronic respiratory patients. EMS rarely carries it. You may walk into a facility and find someone already on one.

A tracheostomy mask sits over a trach tube. Treat it like a face mask: 10–15 L/min if you are using it as high-flow.

Humidified oxygen is bubbled through sterile water so it is more comfortable and less drying. That is mostly long-term therapy. Portable tanks on the truck often do not have it.

Bag-valve mask NREMT

You bag when they are apneic, or when breathing is inadequate — too slow, too fast, shallow, poor chest rise. Rate, rhythm, or quality is not enough.

Hook the bag to the tank at 10–15 L/min. A tight seal on the face gets you close to 100% oxygen.

There are adult, child, and infant bags. The oxygen flow is the same 10–15 L/min on all of them. Only how often you squeeze changes.

Adults: one breath every 6 seconds, which is 10 breaths a minute.

Children and infants: one breath every 2–3 seconds, which is 20–30 a minute.

Squeeze gently for about one second, just until you see the chest rise. Count out loud so you do not go too fast: “Breathe… 1-Mississippi, 2-Mississippi… 6-Mississippi… breathe…”

If they are still taking their own inadequate breaths, try to time your squeeze with their inhale.

Two complications you are trying not to cause: air in the stomach (gastric distension) that leads to vomit and aspiration, and extra pressure in the chest that squeezes the heart and the big vessels and drops blood pressure. Too-fast, too-hard bags can push them into hypotension and even cardiac arrest.

CPAP NREMT

Continuous positive airway pressure is a constant high-pressure stream they have to breathe against. That back-pressure in the alveoli pushes fluid out of the way. That is why it is so useful in pulmonary edema.

The picture from class: head out a car window at 70 mph. Huge rush of air in. Then you have to breathe out against that pressure.

At EMT level you need them alert, able to follow commands, and able to protect their own airway. Typical patient: severe respiratory distress, fluid in the lungs, very labored breathing, but they are still breathing. You often reach for it when an NRB is not enough and they are still working hard.

Hard nos: they are apneic or hypoventilating (respiratory arrest/failure) — they need a bag, not CPAP. They cannot follow commands or they are unconscious. They cannot protect the airway. Systolic blood pressure under 90 — CPAP will drop the pressure more. Trauma, especially chest or face, or you suspect a pneumothorax. Facial trauma, poor mask fit, or they cannot stand the mask. Vomiting, GI bleeding, or nausea — aspiration risk. Shock states with low pressure, including cardiogenic shock.

The key mechanic: CPAP raises pressure inside the chest, and that can drop blood pressure. Great for fluid-overloaded lungs. Bad for someone who is already hypotensive.

When you run the checklist in your head: they follow commands, they can sit up and tolerate the mask, systolic is at least 90, no trauma, no vomiting, no pneumothorax. Any one of those fails and you do not use CPAP. You go back to NRB or BVM depending on how they are breathing.

Mild vs severe obstruction NREMT

Mild: they can cough, speak, make noise, move air. You stay hands-off and you encourage them to cough. You may put a non-rebreather on so whatever air they are moving has more oxygen. Goal is time — let them clear it.

Severe and still conscious: they cannot cough, cannot speak, they are turning blue, they grab the throat (universal choking sign).

For adults and children, updated AHA: five back blows, then five abdominal thrusts (Heimlich), and you keep repeating that 5-and-5 until the object comes out or they become unresponsive.

Infants were already in the book and did not change: five back blows with them face down on your forearm and the head supported, then five chest thrusts in the same spot you would compress for infant CPR. Alternate 5 and 5 until it is out or they go unresponsive.

When they become unresponsive you lay them on their back and start CPR. Compressions keep circulation going and they also generate pressure that can help pop the object. That is true whether they still have a pulse or they are in arrest. If they collapsed from choking, you start compressions.

Dentures, braces, facial bleeding NREMT

Stable dentures stay in. They help keep the face shaped so your mask seals. Loose dentures, plates, or devices come out so they cannot fall back and obstruct.

Braces, expanders, wires: if they are not blocking, leave them. If they get in the way of the airway or of an OPA/NPA, you have to find a way to manage or adjust so the path is clear.

The face is very vascular. You get a lot of bleeding and a lot of swelling. Blood in the airway means you suction often. Swelling of the face or neck can close the airway quickly.

Do not put an NPA through the nose if you suspect facial trauma. Do not use CPAP in trauma. For bleeding you can see on the outside of the face, direct pressure is first-line.

Why you put oxygen on at all NREMT

If they are breathing adequately, you still give oxygen for sat under 94%, any difficulty breathing or labored respirations (even with a normal sat), or signs of shock — cool, pale, clammy, altered mental status, weak or rapid pulse, and so on.

No difficulty breathing, no shock, normal sat: you probably do not need oxygen.

The checklist you run NREMT

Initial airway: is it open? If not, tilt-chin if there is no trauma, jaw thrust if you suspect spinal trauma. Look inside for a visible object, blood, vomit, secretions. Suction up to 10 seconds at a time, then re-oxygenate.

Adjuncts: OPA if they are unresponsive with no gag. NPA if they need support, they have a gag, and there is no facial or cranial trauma. Stable dentures stay. Loose come out.

Breathing: rate (adults 12–20, kids and infants higher), quality (good chest rise?), rhythm (very irregular like Cheyne-Stokes or ataxic is often inadequate). Sat if you have a probe. Work of breathing: accessory muscles, nasal flaring, retractions, tripod, cannot speak full sentences.

Then you decide: nothing, NRB or NC, BVM, or CPAP, using the rules above.

Choking branch is the mild/severe/infant/unresponsive sequence.

You keep reassessing airway patency, how well they are breathing, sat, mental status, and blood pressure.

Practice

  • Do this: Before you name a device, answer the two questions. You did it right if “open/clear?” and “adequate breathing?” came first, and the device was second.
  • Do this: Walk a hypoxic, talking patient through test-world oxygen. You did it right if you put an NRB at 10–15 L/min instead of a cannula.
  • Do this: Recite cannula math. You did it right if 1 L is about 24%, 6 L is about 44%, and you refused more than 6 because it burns.
  • Do this: Count bag rates with a clock. You did it right if the adult is 10 a minute and the child/infant is 20–30, same 10–15 L/min on the tank.
  • Do this: Explain why you do not empty the bag. You did it right if you named stomach air (vomit/aspiration) and extra chest pressure that drops blood pressure.
  • Do this: Run a CPAP yes/no on a wet-lung patient with a BP of 82. You did it right if you said no because systolic under 90, and you bagged or used an NRB instead of forcing the mask.
  • Do this: Coach a coughing choker, then a silent blue adult. You did it right if the first stays hands-off (maybe NRB) and the second gets 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts.
  • Do this: Switch an infant from conscious choke to limp. You did it right if backs and chest thrusts stop and you start supine CPR.
  • Do this: Face smash with blood in the mouth. You did it right if you suction often, skip NPA, skip CPAP, and hold pressure on external bleeding.
