[Patient Treatment and Transport] Online vs offline medical orders

Offline (standing orders)

  • Pre-approved protocols from the medical director
  • Follow without calling a doctor
  • Used for time-critical, standard care
  • Examples in the notes: CPR/AED, oxygen, epinephrine for anaphylaxis
  • Use for most routine emergencies

Online orders

  • Real-time direction from a physician (radio/phone)
  • Needed when the situation is outside protocols or needs specific approval
  • Examples in the notes: certain pain meds, advanced procedures, protocol deviations
  • An online order applies to that one situation only — make another call for each new situation
  • Use when unsure, the case is unusual, or you need extra authorization

Full protocol list and which pain meds need online orders: not in his notes / don’t invent.

[Operations] Error types (patient safety)

Skills-based failure (slip/lapse): you know the correct action but execute it wrong. Example: set O2 to 2 LPM instead of 15 LPM.

Rules-based failure: you apply a known rule/protocol that is wrong for this situation (misjudgment or misdiagnosis). Example: treat stroke as low blood sugar.

Knowledge-based failure: new/unfamiliar situation plus incomplete knowledge → wrong plan. Example: rare overdose → give the wrong antidote.

[Operations] Primary vs secondary prevention

Both are backed by evidence-based medicine (research).

  • Primary: stop the problem before it starts. Examples: vaccines, seatbelts, public education.
  • Secondary: detect early and reduce impact. Examples: screenings; early treatment of high blood pressure or cholesterol.

Tertiary prevention: not in his notes / don’t invent.

[Scene Size-up and Safety] Personal safety first

Protect yourself first so you can help the patient. Includes scene safety, PPE, body substance isolation, safe lifting, and awareness. You cannot help anyone if you become a patient.

Situational awareness: front-door survey before entering — look, listen, smell, assess the mood. Continuously scan the whole scene, not just the patient. After a task, pause and re-check the environment. Watch for changes in people, noise, or atmosphere. Trust your gut. Maintain peripheral awareness while working.

When to call for help

  • Police: violence, weapons, threats, crime scenes, aggressive patients/bystanders, suspected abuse.
  • Hazmat: chemical spills, unknown substances, strong chemical odors/fumes, toxic exposure, industrial accidents with hazardous materials.

[Primary Assessment] Fight-or-flight (why patients look “shocky”)

Automatic body reaction to perceived danger (sympathetic nervous system + stress hormones).

Physical reactions in the notes: HR and BP increase; breathing faster; pupils dilate; blood redirected away from digestion and skin toward large muscles, heart, lungs, and brain; digestion slows; muscles tense; sweating increases; pain perception decreases; senses heightened.

Why it matters: explains patient signs (fast HR, pale/cool/sweaty skin, nausea, anxiety) that may not be from injury; helps distinguish normal stress from true shock or critical illness; your own stress can impair decision-making.

Hormone names beyond “stress hormones”: not in his notes / don’t invent.

[Scene Size-up and Safety] Infection control

  • Pathogen: disease-causing organism (bacteria, virus, fungus, parasite). Examples: HIV, MRSA, influenza.
  • Contamination: pathogen present on a person, object, or surface (blood on stretcher, droplets on doorknob).
  • Exposure: pathogen enters or contacts your body in a way that can cause infection (needlestick, blood splash in eye, inhaling droplets).
  • Transmission: how the pathogen moves.

Routes: direct contact; indirect contact; droplet (large, short distance); airborne (tiny particles that stay in air — TB, measles); vector-borne (mosquitoes, ticks).

Standard (universal) precautions: assume every patient may be infectious. Protect against blood, body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes on every call.

Used every time: hand hygiene; gloves for patient contact. Added as needed: eye protection / face shield; mask; gown; N95 (airborne risk).

Hand hygiene: before and after every patient, after removing gloves, after touching contaminated items. Alcohol-based sanitizer (≥60%) when hands are not visibly dirty. Soap and water when soiled or after diarrhea cases (norovirus, C. diff).

Donning: 1 hand hygiene 2 gown 3 mask/respirator 4 eye protection 5 gloves. Doffing: 1 gloves 2 gown 3 eye protection 4 mask/respirator 5 hand hygiene.

Eye/face: goggles or face shield for splash, spray, or airway procedures. Gowns: fluid-resistant; significant soiling likely (heavy bleeding, childbirth, vomiting). Surgical mask → droplets and most respiratory patients. N95 → known or suspected airborne disease (must be fit-tested). Barrier devices: pocket mask or BVM with one-way valve; always preferred over mouth-to-mouth.

Sharps: never recap, bend, or break needles. Immediate puncture-resistant container. Do not overfill. Never reach in.

Infection control plan components: exposure determination; methods of compliance (PPE, precautions, engineering controls); Hepatitis B vaccination; post-exposure evaluation and follow-up; hazard communication and training; recordkeeping and sharps injury log; cleaning and disinfection.

Routine

  • Before: check PPE stock and sharps container; sanitizer and cleaning supplies available.
  • During: standard precautions (gloves + hand hygiene minimum); add PPE by risk; match the PPE level other providers on scene are wearing; avoid unnecessary surface contact; dispose of sharps immediately.
  • After / between: doff correctly; hand hygiene; clean stretcher, equipment, high-touch surfaces; change contaminated linens or uniforms.
  • End of shift: thorough ambulance cleaning; restock; report exposures immediately.
  • Core habit: treat every patient as potentially infectious; clean everything that was touched.

[Operations] Post-exposure

Immediate: (1) turn patient care over to another EMS provider (2) wash with soap and water (needlestick or skin) (3) flush eyes or mucous membranes with water or saline (4) do not squeeze, suck, or apply bleach.

Next: report immediately to supervisor or designated infection control officer; seek medical evaluation right away (ideally within hours); source may be tested for HIV, Hep B, Hep C; you cannot force a new blood sample in most cases; some states allow testing existing blood or testing under specific legal conditions; you may get baseline tests and PEP if indicated (especially HIV or Hep B).

Follow-up: exposure report; follow-up testing and counseling; evaluation and treatment at no cost to you.

Key: time matters; PEP is most effective when started quickly.

[Scene Size-up and Safety] Scene safety (fires, crashes, hazmat)

Priority: (1) your safety — gloves on every time (2) partner/crew (3) patient care.

Size up from a distance; listen to dispatch for weapons/violence/hazmat; wait for police or specialists if not secure; control hazards (traffic, fire, electricity, chemicals, unstable structures, violent people, animals); always have an exit route; reassess continuously.

Fires: stay clear of smoke and fire; watch collapse, explosion, toxic fumes; do not enter until fire personnel say it is safe; park at least 100 feet away.

Vehicle crashes: park at least 100 feet away. First on scene → park before the crash (upstream) to protect the scene. Second or later → park past the crash (downstream). Use ambulance as a barrier when possible. Watch traffic, leaking fuel, fire, unstable vehicles, downed lines, airbags.

Hazmat: stay uphill and upwind; park well away (100 feet or more). NREMT answer: use the Emergency Response Guidebook (ERG) to identify the substance and protective actions.

Unsafe examples: active violence/weapons; chemical spill or strong fumes; downed power lines; unstable vehicle or structure; large aggressive crowd or animals; active fire or heavy smoke.

Rule: if not safe, do not enter until made safe.

[Patient Treatment and Transport] Communicating with the critical patient

Introduce yourself and explain what you are doing. Avoid appearing sad or grim. Orient the patient (where they are, what happened, what is going on). Be honest without being blunt or cruel. Address initial refusal calmly. Allow for hope — do not take away all hope; do not give false hope. Locate and notify family as soon as practical.

Examples in the notes:

  • “Hi, my name is Alex. I’m an EMT with the fire department. I’m going to take care of you.”
  • “You’re in an ambulance right now. You were in a car crash. We’re taking you to the hospital.”
  • “You’re seriously hurt, but we’re doing everything we can and getting you to the hospital quickly.”
  • “We’ve stopped the bleeding and your vital signs are stable right now.”
  • “I understand you don’t want to go, but you’re badly hurt. Let me explain the risks so you can make an informed decision.”

Key: stay calm, honest, keep the patient oriented. Do not completely recommend an option.

[Patient Treatment and Transport] Death and dying

Kübler-Ross stages: (1) Denial (2) Anger (3) Bargaining (4) Depression (5) Acceptance.

EMT role: respect and empathy; honest but compassionate; allow family with the patient when possible; avoid false hope or empty platitudes (“Everything will be fine”); support family even after care ends; maintain professionalism.

People do not always go through stages in order; they may skip or move back and forth.

Chapter 3: Medical, Legal, and Ethical Issues — NREMT detailed (HIS notes only)

[Operations] Good faith + standard of care

A healthcare provider usually avoids legal exposure if they act in good faith and according to an appropriate standard of care.

Standard of care: what a reasonably prudent EMT with similar training and experience would do in the same or similar situation.

Negligence: failure to meet that standard that results in harm. All four required: (1) duty to act (2) breach of duty (3) damages (4) causation.

Abandonment: unilateral termination of care without consent and without transfer to equal or higher level when the patient still needs care.

Avoid both: follow protocols and stay in scope; continuous care until proper handoff; document informed refusals; assess and document; never leave a patient who still requires care.

Duty-to-act off-duty details: not in his notes / don’t invent.

[Patient Treatment and Transport] Consent

Expressed: alert, oriented, decision-making capacity; verbal or non-verbal agreement; must be informed (plan, risks, benefits). Example: ask to check BP and start an IV; patient says yes or holds out an arm.

Informed: more complete expressed consent; patient understands nature, risks, benefits, and alternatives. Example: explain risks of refusing transport for chest pain and they still agree to go. All informed consent is expressed; not all expressed consent is fully informed.

Implied: unresponsive, severely altered, or unable to consent; assumes a reasonable person would want emergency care; true emergencies only. Example: unconscious crash patient — treat and transport.

Involuntary: against the patient’s will under legal authority (mental health hold, court order, or lacks capacity and is a danger to self/others). Example: suicidal, severely altered, refuses — LE places a hold and you transport.

Key: capacity → can refuse. Lack of capacity + emergency → implied consent and treat.

HIPAA as a statute name, age of majority, and exact hold statute numbers: not in his notes / don’t invent.

[Scene Size-up and Safety] Forcible / physical restraint

Allowed when: danger to self or others; needed to protect patient, crew, or public; needed to allow assessment/treatment; less restrictive options have failed.

Requirements: least restrictive method; never prone (face-down); never hog-tie; consult medical control prior if possible; do not interfere with breathing or circulation; continuously monitor ABCs and mental status; document thoroughly; involve LE when possible.

Types: soft restraints (padded/Velcro) preferred for EMS; hard restraints (handcuffs) usually LE only.

Chemical restraint: meds to sedate; ALS/paramedic skill — not in normal EMT scope.

Last resort. Safety, monitoring, documentation are critical.

[Patient Treatment and Transport] Right to refuse

A competent adult with capacity may refuse assessment, treatment, or transport even if refusal may cause serious harm or death.

Valid refusal: capacity; informed of condition, recommended care, risks of refusing; voluntary; sign a refusal form when possible.

Capacity: understand the information; appreciate consequences; reason through the decision; communicate a clear choice.

You must: explain risks; offer alternatives when appropriate; contact medical control for high-risk refusals; document (what you told them, responses, capacity, witnesses, signature).

Before leaving: reassess once more; confirm they still understand and are refusing; encourage calling 911 if worse; leave with a responsible person if possible; signed refusal or document refusal to sign; notify medical control if protocol requires.

[Operations] Confidentiality

Share only with those who have a legitimate need to know for care, or when required by law.

Protected: name, address, phone; history, findings, vitals; treatment and responses; anything that identifies the patient and their condition.

May share: other providers involved in care (hospital, medical control, receiving facility); required by law (certain crimes, GSW, abuse, public health); valid court order/subpoena; patient’s written permission.

May not: friends/family unless permission or they are the legal decision-maker; media; social media; casual talk with people not in care.

If in doubt, do not share. Breaking confidentiality can mean legal action, loss of certification, and termination.

[Patient Treatment and Transport] Advanced directives

Legal documents for care if the patient cannot decide.

  • Living will / health care directive: which life-sustaining treatments they do or do not want (ventilator, feeding tube, etc.).
  • Durable power of attorney for health care: names a proxy/agent.
  • DNR: no CPR/advanced resuscitation if heart or breathing stops.
  • POLST/MOLST: medical order with specific EMS/hospital instructions (often more detailed than a basic DNR).

EMS: follow a valid DNR or POLST if present and appears legitimate. A living will alone is often not enough for EMS to withhold emergency care — usually need a specific DNR or POLST. If conscious with capacity, current wishes override the written directive. When in doubt, begin treatment and contact medical control. If any question, resuscitate and let the hospital sort it out.

Exact AZ DNR form color/requirements: not in his notes / don’t invent.

[Primary Assessment] Physical signs of death

Presumptive (not enough alone): unresponsiveness; no breathing; no pulse; fixed, dilated pupils.

Definitive (obvious death — do not resuscitate): dependent lividity; rigor mortis; putrefaction/decomposition; injuries incompatible with life (decapitation, massive trauma, etc.).

Need a definitive sign or a valid DNR/POLST before withholding resuscitation. When in doubt, start CPR.

ME/coroner: unexpected death or previously good health; trauma, violence, accident, or suicide; suspicious/unusual; no recent attending physician. Notify law enforcement first. Do not move the body until ME/coroner clears it.

[Secondary Assessment] Therapeutic communication

Build trust and encourage the patient to share. Open-ended first (“What happened?”), then closed-ended for specifics. Eye contact, speak clearly, listen actively, show respect. Adjust for age, culture, hearing/vision impairment, or anxiety. Honest without harsh. Avoid false reassurance.

Types

  • Confrontation: point out inconsistency calmly — “You said you don’t drink, but I smell alcohol.”
  • Interpretation: restate what you think they mean — “It sounds like you’re worried this is a heart attack.”
  • Explanation: tell them what is happening — “I’m putting this oxygen mask on to help you breathe.”
  • Summary: repeat main points — “Pain for two hours, started while walking, worse with deep breaths — correct?”

Avoid: false reassurance; leading questions (“The pain is in your chest, right?”); giving advice (“You should really quit smoking”); interrupting; medical jargon (“You have tachycardia and dyspnea”); judgmental statements; talking over the patient with your partner.

[Patient Treatment and Transport] 10 golden rules (calm and reassure)

  1. Make and keep eye contact.
  2. Provide your name and use the patient’s proper name.
  3. Tell the patient the truth.
  4. Use language the patient can understand.
  5. Be careful what you say about the patient to others.
  6. Be aware of your body language.
  7. Speak slowly, clearly, and distinctly.
  8. For hearing-impaired patients, face them so they can read your lips.
  9. Allow time to answer or respond.
  10. Act and speak in a calm, confident manner.

[Operations] Radio and verbal reports

Brief, clear, professional. Plain English (avoid codes unless required). Do not say the patient’s name over the radio. Repeat orders back to confirm.

Hospital notification format (SBAR): Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation / ETA.

10-codes and local radio templates: not in his notes / don’t invent. Full CHART/SOAP formats: not in his notes / don’t invent. (SBAR is the format given.)

[Operations] PCR

Legal document; part of the permanent medical record. Must include: demographics, chief complaint, history, vital signs, assessment findings, treatments, response to treatment, and times. Write objectively — facts only, no opinions or judgments. If it isn’t documented, it didn’t happen.

PCR errors: single line through the error, initial, date, write the correct information. Never erase or cover up.

Refusals: document capacity, risks explained, patient understanding, and signature (or refusal to sign).

[Patient Treatment and Transport] Transfer of care

Official only after a verbal handoff to a provider of equal or higher level. Clear oral report and leave a completed PCR.

[Secondary Assessment] Special situations / confidentiality

  • Children / special needs: speak at their level, involve caregivers, use simple language.
  • Protect all patient information. Share only with those involved in care or when required by law.

Key point: clear communication and accurate documentation protect the patient, protect you legally, and ensure continuity of care.

[Secondary Assessment] Anatomy of a medical term

  • Prefix — beginning
  • Word root — core meaning
  • Combining vowel — usually “o” (pronunciation only)
  • Suffix — ending

Break the word into prefix – root – suffix.

Prefixes

hyper- high/excessive; hypo- low/under; tachy- fast; brady- slow; a-/an- without; dys- difficult/painful/abnormal; pre- before; post- after.

Roots

Suffixes

-itis inflammation; -pathy disease; -algia pain; -ectomy surgical removal; -megaly enlargement; -ology study of; -osis abnormal condition; -emia blood condition; -pnea breathing; -phagia eating/swallowing.

Numbers

uni- one; bi- two; tri- three; quadri- four; multi-/poly- many; nulli- none; hemi-/semi- half.

Colors

cyan/o blue; erythr/o red; leuk/o white; melan/o black; poli/o gray; cirrh/o / jaund/o / xanth/o yellow.

Positions and directions (word parts)

[Secondary Assessment] Anatomical directions (patient’s body)

Always the patient’s right and left.

  • Superior = toward the head / above; Inferior = toward the feet / below
  • Anterior (ventral) = front; Posterior (dorsal) = back
  • Medial = toward midline; Lateral = away from midline
  • Proximal = closer to trunk/attachment; Distal = farther
  • Superficial = closer to surface; Deep = farther from surface

Referenced to anatomical position: standing facing you, arms at sides, palms forward.

[Secondary Assessment] Movement

  • Flexion = bend / decrease angle (elbow or knee)
  • Extension = straighten / increase angle
  • Abduction = away from midline (arm out to side)
  • Adduction = toward midline (arm back to side)
  • Rotation = turn around an axis (head left/right)
  • Circumduction = circular (arm circles)
  • Supination = palm up; Pronation = palm down
  • Plantar flexion = toes down; Dorsiflexion = toes up toward shin

[Patient Treatment and Transport] Patient positions (document clearly)

  • Anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, arms at sides, palms forward
  • Supine: back, face up
  • Prone: stomach, face down
  • Lateral recumbent (recovery): on the side
  • Fowler: sitting upright ~45–90°
  • Semi-Fowler: partially upright ~30–45°
  • Trendelenburg: supine, feet higher than head (rarely used now)
  • Shock position: supine with legs elevated (older term; use caution)

Document the position clearly (“found supine,” “transported semi-Fowler”).

[Secondary Assessment] Anatomy vs physiology

  • Anatomy = structure (what it looks like and where things are)
  • Physiology = function (how things work)
  • Topographic anatomy = external landmarks and surface features, used to locate deeper structures. Always referenced to anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, arms at sides, palms forward.

Learn directional terms and surface landmarks first.

[Secondary Assessment] Planes

  • Sagittal: left and right. Midsagittal (median) = equal halves.
  • Coronal (frontal): front (anterior) and back (posterior).
  • Transverse (horizontal / axial): upper (superior) and lower (inferior); also called a cross-section.

[Primary Assessment] Cells to systems (why ABC is first)

  1. Cells — basic unit of life
  2. Tissues — similar cells together
  3. Organs — different tissues, specific function
  4. Organ systems — organs together
  5. Organism — complete human body

Cells need: oxygen, glucose (sugar), water and nutrients. Energy: Oxygen + Glucose → Energy (ATP) + Waste (CO₂ and water) + Heat. High-energy cells (brain) die quickly without oxygen or glucose. Lower-energy cells (skin) survive longer. This is why airway, breathing, and circulation are the top priorities.

[Secondary Assessment] Skeletal system

Functions: shape and support; protect vital organs; movement (with muscles); store minerals (especially calcium); produce blood cells (bone marrow).

Adult skeleton = 206 bones.

Axial

  • Cranium (4 bones per the notes): frontal, parietal (2), occipital. Temporal and others: not in his notes / don’t invent.
  • Face (14 bones): nasal, zygomatic (cheek), maxilla (upper jaw), mandible (lower jaw), and others (not named).
  • Spine 33 vertebrae: C7, T12, L5, sacrum 5 fused, coccyx 4 fused.
  • Thorax: 12 pairs of ribs (24), sternum 1.

Appendicular

  • Shoulder girdle: clavicle, scapula
  • Upper: humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges
  • Pelvis: ilium, ischium, pubis (fused on each side) — highest potential to bleed (most blood)
  • Lower: femur, patella, tibia, fibula, tarsals, metatarsals, phalanges

[Primary Assessment] Pump, pipes, fluid

Circulatory / cardiovascular system = three parts that must all work: pump = heart; pipes = vessels (veins and arteries); fluid = blood. If any one fails → problem. Together they make blood pressure / enough circulation to deliver oxygen and sugar to every cell for energy.

Two loops: pulmonary (heart → lungs to pick up O2); systemic (O2 blood out through the body and back). Left side = systemic. Right side = pulmonary.

Heart is a hollow muscular organ; cardiac muscle (own category). Last week’s three muscle types: skeletal/voluntary/striated; smooth involuntary; cardiac. Muscle only contracts or relaxes. Every muscle except the heart needs a brain/nervous zap — limp when unconscious. Heart makes its own electricity (brain can speed/slow it). Unconscious ≠ heart immediately stops.

[Primary Assessment] Chambers, flow, valves, backup

Top = atria (receive). Bottom = ventricles (pump out). Deoxygenated body → RA → RV → lungs (alveoli, pick up O2). Oxygenated → LA → LV → whole body. Then home to RA. Learn the normal direction. If the pump fails, blood backs up the wrong way (weak left → backup to lungs / trouble breathing; CHF preview — more in ch 17 / shock).

Atria pump together; slight lag, then both ventricles. “Bump, bump”: atria fill the ventricles, then ventricles fire. LV wall much thicker (RV only to lungs; LV to fingertips/toes/head).

AV valves: right tricuspid (3 flaps); left bicuspid = mitral (2 flaps). Same 3-vs-2 as right 3 lung lobes / left 2. Semilunar: pulmonic (RV out); aortic (LV into aorta). Sequence: tricuspid → pulmonic → bicuspid/mitral → aortic (TPMA; “try before you buy”). Atria contract → AV valves open. Ventricles contract → AV valves snap shut (heart sounds) and outflow valves open. Mitral valve prolapse: valve should close but partially/fully inverts.

Superior vena cava: upper body into top of RA. Inferior vena cava: lower body into bottom of RA. RV → pulmonary arteries (away, deoxygenated). Lungs → pulmonary veins (back, oxygenated). More O2 = brighter red. Aorta hooks above the heart (he said three top offshoots visual is helpful but not exact).

Gas exchange in two places: lungs (O2 in, CO2 out at alveoli); every cell (O2 leaves blood; CO2 back → lungs).

[Primary Assessment] HR, SV, CO, preload, afterload

Adult normal resting HR 60–100. Average 70. >100 = tachycardia; <60 = bradycardia. Whether it’s a problem = how they’re handling it. Endurance athletes can sit in the 40s.

Stroke volume = blood ejected in one beat (like tidal volume). Cannot measure SV in the field. Judge by BP, skin, mental status. Cardiac output = blood in one minute = SV × HR. Example: SV 80 mL, HR 70 → 5600 mL = 5.6 L/min. Adult blood volume: about five to six liters (~ three 2-liter sodas). Average CO ≈ almost the whole blood volume every minute. He said CO is rarely tested vs last week’s minute volume.

Aorta rupture (trauma or medical): rapidly fatal in seconds — BP tanks, pass out, nothing left to circulate.

Blood thinners (anticoagulants): take away clot ability. Helpful for clot/clot risk; worse in trauma. Chronic meds including daily BP meds can work against fight-or-flight — they crash faster.

Preload: how much blood is returning to / filling the heart. More in → more out. Frank-Starling / Starling’s law: rubber band — more fill → stronger snap. Bleeding on the floor = decreased preload → less out → BP hit. Drop in preload can black you out. Brain’s big three: O2, sugar, temperature.

Afterload = pipes the heart pumps into / systemic vascular resistance. Vasoconstriction = smaller pipes = higher pressure. Vasodilation = bigger pipes = lower pressure. Body: BP too low → constrict; too high → dilate. Smooth muscle around vessels is involuntary. Anaphylaxis: widespread vasodilation. EpiPen one job he named here: massive vasoconstriction.

[Primary Assessment] Automaticity, vessels, coronaries, pulses, BP, bleeds

Automaticity: cells can spontaneously fire. SA node high in right atrium = primary pacemaker. Every normal pulse starts here. Sinus rhythm = SA-node electricity. AV node holds a split second so ventricles fill. Arrhythmia interpretation = paramedic. EMT test emphasis: SA node = primary pacemaker. Pacemaker wire placement depends (age, med toxicity, electrolyte imbalance, structural damage / heart attack).

A-A: arteries away; veins back. Every artery oxygenated except pulmonary arteries. Other exception: fetal / umbilical (arteries deoxygenated baby → placenta; vein oxygenated placenta → baby). Pregnancy chapter later.

Coronary arteries on the surface of the heart. Chamber blood does not feed the muscle. Off the aorta immediately: right coronary → right side; left coronary → left. Heart attack = those pipes clogged → not enough O2/sugar → cells die.

Carotids: neck, O2 to brain. Every pulse you feel = LV contracting. MMA choke: both carotids → pass out fast.

Hepatic arteries: to liver. Liver detoxifies. He said a later chapter will say something like 40% of total blood volume in the liver — treat as his preview, not a locked protocol. Liver trauma = major internal bleed.

Renal arteries: kidneys filter / clean blood; keep vs package into urine.

Mesentery: fold holding intestines up; supplies blood; without it intestines sink into the pelvis.

RBC lifespan ~120 days. Spleen removes old RBCs. Bones produce new RBCs. No spleen → old RBCs not cleared; he thinks the blood-draw removal is apheresis (“if I remember right”) — not a locked fact.

Gas exchange on the blood side happens only at capillaries (not aorta). Arteries → arterioles → capillaries (U-turn) → veins → vena cava → RA. Capillaries meet alveoli; O2/CO2 by diffusion. Same at every cell.

Pulse sites named: carotid; radial; femoral (just inside groin — “we don’t do it as much”); brachial (under bicep against humerus); top of the foot; inside and behind the bony bump of the ankle. L vs R side: 99.9% of the time it does not matter except distal-to-injury. More tissue → push harder; vessel stays next to bone.

Arterial bleed: bright red + spurting. Venous bleed: darker + flowing / oozing. IVs go in a vein. Arterial usually more critical unless sizes are skewed.

[Operations] How to study Ch 8 / gurney

[Operations] Backboards / when to board / log roll / power grip

6–7 ft, heavy plastic now. Patient stays on it to the hospital; agency later sorts boards at the ER pile.

Main use: suspected spinal cord injury — giant full-body splint. Skills test: one of seven stations is backboarding. Typical straps: X across torso, strap over hips, one or two over legs.

Obesity / won’t fit: if width might worsen spine protection, justify leaving it off; if unsure → medical control. Bariatric gurney next talk.

Body mechanics: shoulders over pelvis; hands close to the legs. Don’t lift leaning forward or with the back. Legs shoulder-width, back straight, lift by straightening the legs, weight close to the body.

Power grip: hands about 10 inches apart, palms up, all fingers same angle.

Log roll (by the book: four people):

  • One person at the head: manual stabilization of head / cervical spine. In charge — calls the count.
  • Three along the side do the roll.
  • Head person does not lift by the head; keeps head in line.
  • Before the roll: cervical collar.
  • Of seven stations, two are team. You can talk.

When to backboard (current vs old)

  • Conscious: neck or back pain; or paralysis, numbness, tingling, weakness.
  • Unconscious: lose those clues → based on situation, maybe → board just in case.
  • Old: almost every trauma. Now: pain after MVC/etc., neuro symptoms, or unconscious, also poor historian (intoxicated “no” while saying ow).
  • Combative: depends. Head injury → maybe paramedic sedates. If they would be restrained anyway, board is a kind of restraint. Claustrophobic: talk; if they keep refusing: med control so you don’t break standard of care.

When possible, use a device that can be rolled. Estimate weight if unconscious; ask if conscious. Larger patient: request additional resources / lift. Local 911: fire + ambulance — “we got six.”

[Scene Size-up and Safety] Safety order, parking, MOI/NOI, six lines

[Primary Assessment] General impression / AVPU / A&O×4

GI starts with eyes on: awake vs unconscious vs dead vs head-bob; age, sex, race, distress, appearance, position (couch clutching chest; face-down; tripod), weird breath sounds. From Ch 4: don’t stand over them; introduce; start formal (“Hey sir. Hey Mr. Smith. My name’s Andrew…”) and let them dial you back. Ask the chief complaint.

Life threat in GI → stop and deal (supine, eyes closed, bright red spurting from the arm → bleed now). First question: is there a chance they are dead? Supine, eyes closed, not breathing, blue → pulse right away. Awake and looking = not dead. Then sick vs not sick — a starting point only. Stories: elderly “not acting right,” looks chill, massive stroke (speech center; family said she usually talks — get going). Looks unconscious, was asleep.

AVPU (he said A-V-P-U; transcript also garbled “AVU”):

  • A awake/alert: looking around, they see you. Slumped on the couch = not awake.
  • V: talk — “Hey sir. My name’s Andrew. I’m an EMT.” Any response (they say hey, or eyes open then out) = verbal.
  • P pain/pressure if no V: sternal rub with knuckles; trap pinch on top of the shoulder; pin/squeeze the nail bed. Any moan/groan, swat the hand, wiggle/turn away = pain. Pain response = deep evolutionary “get away”; no reaction = brain deeply off.
  • U unresponsive if no pain response. No more checks.
  • Once you know the level, stop. Do not sternal-rub someone who is A or V.

If A, then orientation: person, place, time, event. Not a hard-and-fast script. His forever set: What’s your name? Do you know where you are? What year is it? Who’s the president? Evaluates short/medium/long-term memory (name = long-term). A and O times four = alert and oriented to all four. Done early because of decision-making capacity / consent. Altered = any deviation from A&O×4 or from that patient’s normal. Nobody who knows them → assume newly altered, rapid transport; family later can say this is baseline.

[Primary Assessment] Life threats / airway / breathing / circulation (to the cap)

Big-ticket list: airway obstruction (food/choke or the tongue — #1 when muscles relax); respiratory failure (still breathing, not enough — OD at six and shallow); respiratory arrest (not doing it at all); shock / hypoperfusion; severe major bleeding; cardiac arrest. Respiratory distress did not make the list: they complain of difficulty but are moving plenty of air enough times per minute — not an immediate life threat. Those map to ABCs. Find → treat. One thing at a time, in order.

Airway: goal is a patent (open and clear) upper airway. Talking or a kid crying = airway good → breathing. Otherwise make it patent. Unconscious: immediately assume the tongue. Identify a problem → stop the assessment and work the airway until it is clear.

Two openers (full how-to tomorrow, Ch 11 — that chapter lingers on primary steps 3–4):

  • Head-tilt chin-lift if no trauma / no spine worry — tilt the head back, tongue comes out of the throat. Do not crank the head if you worry about the cord.
  • Jaw-thrust if trauma/spine: give an underbite; whole jaw forward, tongue comes with it. Patient supine. Stabilize on the cheekbones; other fingers behind the angle of the jaw; push the jaw up. Moves jaw/tongue without moving the spine.

Then look inside. Blood or vomit → get it out (why it is there does not matter). Signs of obstruction in the unconscious: obvious trauma or blood, noisy breathing. Normal breathing is quiet. Gurgling → suction. Snoring. High-pitched whistle.

Four airway steps: open (tongue out) → assess (look and listen) → clear → maintain (put something in so the tongue stays out — adjunct later / Ch 11).

Breathing only after the pathway is open (blocked tongue can make the rate look like zero). Ask rate, rhythm, quality. Rate = times/min. Rhythm: regular vs bouncing. Super irregular / fast-and-slow / deep-and-shallow chaos → something upstream of the diaphragm: brainstem (major head trauma as an example). Quality/depth = tidal volume; visual = chest rise. Enough is enough when you see rise — whether they breathe, you ventilate, bystander mouth-to-mouth, medic tube, or trach.

Decisions: breathe for them vs extra oxygen. Consider positive-pressure ventilations (breathe for them with an adjunct — tomorrow) when adult respirations are faster than 28 or slower than 8. Kids: younger = faster baseline; skew those numbers (no kid numbers given). Slow = pretty safe bet you ventilate (six/min is not enough). Fitness can give a low heart rate, not a resting low breathing rate — the body still uses O2. Fast = a maybe: evaluate; body may be helping a problem (don’t mess with it) vs anxiety hyperventilate (causing a problem — still don’t walk up with an airway device). Hypoxia clues: pulse ox (end of chapter), cyanosis.

Oxygenation goal: saturation approximately 94 to 99%. Probe measures % of hemoglobin “seats on the bus” that have something on them. Machines can falsely skew; one piece of the total picture; generally pretty accurate.

Work of breathing (look of difficulty, not the diagnosis): accessory muscle use (neck/chest/back when diaphragm/intercostals fatigue — kids’ breathing is almost all diaphragm); retractions (skin drawn between ribs or around the clavicle); nasal flaring (bigger nose pipes); two-to-three-word dyspnea (dys = difficulty, pnea = breathing; “talking tiredness”); tripod (hands on knees) often with sniffing (straighter shot for air); labored / increased work. That package plus how good/bad the effort is will dictate if and how extra O2 tomorrow — not in his notes / don’t invent device choice.

Circulation (talk cut mid-topic): start with pulse rate, rhythm, quality. Quality = strength = early BP clue. Alive, talking, no radial = very low blood pressure. Barely feel it ≈ low BP. Nice and strong = BP good. Unresponsive + no pulse → CPR (cardiac arrest). Sites: brachial under the biceps, radial at the wrist, carotid in the neck.

  • Conscious / clearly alive → radial (more approachable than the neck).
  • Unresponsive child or adult (child = one until adult) → carotid.
  • Infant (up to one) unconscious → brachial. Carotid pressure on a floppy infant can collapse the airway. After one, structures are solid enough.

Priority. After personal safety and confirming they are alive, airway is at the top. The only patient-care thing that may jump it is a major life-threatening bleed you already saw on the general impression — find a life threat, treat a life threat. Then airway.

If cells lack O2 they make less energy and flip to anaerobic metabolism; waste becomes lactic acid instead of easy-to-dump CO2. You work the respiratory checks first. If breathing truly looks adequate and they are still sick, it may be cardiac (pump not delivering). Dispatch “I’m having a hard time breathing” can still become a heart call; chest-pain calls can become respiratory.

A&P path. Goal is air to the alveoli, which meet pulmonary capillaries. Upper airway (everything above the larynx / vocal cords): warm, filter, humidify. Nasal cavity / nasopharynx, oral cavity / oropharynx, epiglottis over the larynx so swallowed food does not enter the lungs. Lower airway: pathway + gas exchange. Trachea → carina (splits / bifurcates) → right and left bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli (grape sacs).

Mediastinum (media = middle, sternum): heart, trachea, first bronchi, aorta (LV output), SVC + IVC back to right atrium. Lungs live in pleural compartments.

Phrenic nerve. Brain → brainstem → cord → exits C3, C4, C5 (“keeps the diaphragm alive”) → diaphragm shock. High cervical injury: paralyzed and diaphragm out → cannot breathe. Injury below C5: still badly paralyzed, pathway intact → can still breathe. Heart is the exception that makes its own electricity.

Three words, in order. Ventilation = mechanical move of air in/out. Respiration = gas exchange. Oxygenation = O2 bound to hemoglobin. Then circulation delivers it. Field order: Is air moving? Adequately (rate, rhythm, quality / enough volume × enough times)? If yes but they look terrible → listen for a gas-exchange problem (edema, fluid = gases will not diffuse through fluid) → pulse ox → if sat looks fine, think circulation / cardiac.

Example he used: 18/min, good rise = great ventilation, but blue → not a vent fail.

Mechanics. Inhalation is active: diaphragm contracts down, intercostals expand the cage, container bigger, pressure drops, negative intrathoracic pressure pulls air in. That is normal breathing. PPV is the opposite: we push. Air also goes down the esophagus, fills the stomach, later they vomit on you. Body does not pull air into the stomach. Lungs are passengers — paralyzed diaphragm = useless lungs. Diaphragm is both involuntary (brainstem) and voluntary (you can take a breath).

Exhalation should be passive (relax, container smaller, pressure out). Asthma / COPD: they work to get air out as well as in → extra energy → fatigue.

Volumes (concepts, not numbers). Tidal volume = one breath; we cannot measure it; chest rise means enough air likely reached alveoli. Too-small TV may stop in the pipes. Inspiratory reserve = extra you can force in; stretch receptors + Hering–Breuer let the brain shut you off so you do not pop lungs. When you bag, that reflex is not protecting them — add air only until you see chest rise, then stop. He rejected “half a squeeze” as unknowable. Expiratory reserve = extra you can force out. Residual volume = leftover that keeps alveoli from collapsing.

Drive. Chemoreceptors watch blood chemistry and CSF. Primary trigger: CO2 high → phrenic fire → inhale O2, exhale CO2. Backup = hypoxic drive (O2 low-low). COPD (next weekend): they retain CO2 no matter what, so the brain stops trusting CO2 and watches O2 drop instead. Do not invent COPD O2 rules here.

Respiration needs air in alveoli and blood in the capillary. PE-style clot = blood blocked. No air to alveoli = nothing to exchange.

Metabolism. Aerobic: O2 + sugar → lots of ATP (“energy”) + CO2 + water + heat. Anaerobic (an- = without): less energy, lactic acid. Supply vs demand; Ch 17 will use this for heart muscle.

Diffusion = high to low, balances, does not overshoot as a story. Happens twice: external / pulmonary (alveoli ↔ blood) and internal / systemic (blood ↔ cells). Every cell needs O2 and sugar inside it.

What breaks which arrow. Ventilation intrinsic: infection, allergy swelling, unresponsive tongue. Extrinsic: trauma swelling, blood in the mouth. Respiration intrinsic: pneumonia mucus, pulmonary edema, COPD. Extrinsic: atmosphere — Everest low quantity/pressure of O2. Room air ~21% O2 (mostly nitrogen); percent stays ~21% up high; there is just less of everything.

If respiratory checks pass and they are still dying, circulation: pneumothorax (air in thorax outside lung = collapse; we can cause it by over-bagging), hemothorax (blood), hemo-pneumo. Usually trauma (rib, GSW, overinflation). Early collapse still exchanges; late = one lung working. EMT cannot fix the hole. Medic needle lets pressure out temporarily; it rebuilds; surgery closes the hole. Timeline = hole size (GSW fast; tiny cough-hole slow). Survive bilateral holes only if surgery is fast enough. Also blood loss, anemia (an- + -emia = not enough blood / RBCs), shock.

Adequate vs inadequate (the test). Adult adequate: 12–20, equal rise/fall, regular I:E pattern, clear equal bilateral sounds, adequate depth = chest rise. That is R/R/Q. They can still complain of SOB, be hypoxic, be cyanotic, sit at SpO2 91% — adequate only means they are moving enough air enough times. Then you may only change the percent they inhale (devices later). Inadequate = not enough movement → you ventilate (4/min; very shallow). Most scenario items split two answers each way — get this binary right first.

Abnormal ≠ automatically inadequate. 22 with good rise, regular, clear = a bit fast, still moving well. Slow is a safer bet to ventilate. Irregular (head trauma) probably not adequate. Absent/minimal rise = help them. Accessory muscles, retractions (ribs / clavicle), skin CTC (circulation assessment that also clues breathing).

Patterns. Agonal gasps = heart stopped, CPR; brain has not caught up; not functional ventilation. Do not use that phrase for an awake gasper on the couch. Cheyne–Stokes = slow/shallow → deeper/faster → slow → apnea → repeat; you can follow the pattern. Biot / ataxic = no pattern at all. Both = brainstem / severe head trauma. Kussmaul = deep + rapid to blow off CO2 because the body is acidic (CO2 becomes acid). DKA (Ch 20, type 1, sky-high sugar), aspirin OD, tricyclics. Compensation — do not take over just to “fix” a high rate; yesterday’s >28 consider PPV is a maybe on the fast side.

Ventilating well + fluid-filled lungs = respiration fail (cyanosis, SpO2 80% in his example). LOC drops fast (brain hates low O2). Skin. Pulse ox. ETCO2 more medic.
