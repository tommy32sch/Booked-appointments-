Allergy and anaphylaxis — detailed

This recap is how you tell a mild allergy from anaphylaxis, and what you do for each.

Skin only is mild. Itch, hives, or a rash at one spot, like a hornet sting plus rash only, stays in one system. Do not give epinephrine (Epi). Anaphylaxis is more than one body system. You need skin findings plus at least one more system. Breathing counts: face or tongue swelling, stridor, wheezing, or they say it is hard to breathe. Heart and vessels count: adult systolic blood pressure (SBP) under 90, or other shock signs. The book also counts nausea or vomiting as a gut second system. Breathing trouble or low pressure is the usual pair with skin. Either one is enough. You do not need both.

Learn the words. Pruritus is itching. Urticaria is hives: many raised itchy bumps. A wheal is one single bump, like a mosquito bite. Edema is swelling from fluid leaking into tissue. Angioedema is swelling in one area. Tongue and face are the ones that close an airway. Dyspnea is hard to breathe. Stridor is a high whistle, usually on inhale. That is the upper airway. You can hear it without a stethoscope. Wheezing is a high whistle, usually on exhale. That is the lower airway. You need a stethoscope. Hypersensitivity means allergy. A sympathomimetic is a drug that copies fight-or-flight. Epi is that class. Hives from an allergy are not contagious. If it looks like itching and hives, treat it as an allergy even if they say they have none.

The immune system overreacts to something: food, a sting, a drug, latex, a smell, or almost anything. The body must be sensitized first. First exposure usually just teaches the body. The next exposure can cause the big reaction. The body dumps histamine (and leukotrienes; these notes just call it histamine). Histamine causes itching, hives, swelling, wide-open vessels, leaky vessels, squeezed lungs, and low heart output. Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) blocks how the body reacts to histamine. It is not your main treatment for anaphylaxis.

Epi is the answer for anaphylaxis. Alpha receptors squeeze vessels back down, so blood pressure comes up. Beta-1 speeds and strengthens the heart, so output comes up. Beta-2 opens the lower airways. Epi also stops the body from making more histamine. It does not wipe out histamine already in the blood. If it is anaphylaxis, Epi is the answer for you, the medic, and the emergency room.

This is a pipe problem: distributive shock. Same group as septic and neurogenic. Adult SBP under 90 is decompensated shock. Skin in this shock group is the most mixed. Anaphylaxis is often red, flushed, and full of hives. It is not always cool, pale, and sweaty.

Four things for every shock patient: lay them down (supine), high-flow oxygen, keep warm, rapid transport. Extra for anaphylactic shock: call medical control for Epi.

Position follows the problem. Hard to breathe only → sit them up (High Fowler). Shock → lay them down. Both problems → shock wins. Lay them down. Why: if breathing gets worse, you can use a bag-valve mask (BVM). If sitting them up drops pressure, you cannot easily fix that.

Airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs) always come first. Give oxygen before Epi. In the field those two often happen close together. Still keep the order in your head: ABCs, oxygen, field impression, then call medical control for Epi.

The tongue or face may swell shut (angioedema). You may hear stridor or snoring. An oropharyngeal airway (OPA) may not fit. A nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) may meet swelling too. You cannot un-swell an airway as fast as it swells. Give Epi quickly once you know it is anaphylaxis.

The oxygen device is based only on rate, rhythm, and quality right now. Adequate breathing, but they need oxygen → non-rebreather (NRB). Respiratory failure: still breathing, but not enough (slow, shallow) → BVM. Respiratory arrest: not breathing at all → BVM. A punctured lung does not automatically mean failure. Use rate, rhythm, and quality. Tripod, extra muscles, nasal flaring, and head bobbing only prove they are working hard to breathe. Hives and itching are what point you to allergy instead of asthma.

Check pulse rate, rhythm, and quality. Check skin color, temperature, condition, and cap refill. Call advanced life support (ALS) early. Paramedics always carry Epi. Reassess every 5 minutes if they are critical. Wash the area. Ice or a cold pack on a sting site. Take off rings and watches before swelling turns them into a tourniquet. If a stinger is still in, scrape it out with a credit card. Do not use tweezers. Tweezers squeeze more venom in.

Adult Epi is 0.3 mg intramuscular (IM) auto-injector. Child Epi is 0.15 mg IM auto-injector. IM into a big muscle still absorbs well in shock. The needle can go through clothes. Bare skin is nicer if you have a second. Pens usually come as a 2-pack. The practical limit is often 2. Give Epi for itching or hives plus a breathing problem, or itching or hives plus low pressure (SBP under 90). The book also allows itching or hives plus nausea or vomiting. Do not give Epi for skin only.

Call medical control to give Epi and to repeat it. They may have already used their own pen before you arrived. The doctor will look at current blood pressure and heart rate before saying yes. If you only have an adult pen for a child, call the doctor. Do not guess.

Side effects are Epi doing its job too well: blood pressure too high, heart too fast, chest pain, bad rhythms, pale or ashen skin from vessels clamped down, headache, dizziness, nausea. Higher risk in adults with heart disease or high blood pressure. Kids usually handle it better.

Do not memorize an allergen list. People can be allergic to anything. Food often shows in 20–30 minutes because it must be absorbed. That is not a guarantee. Some peanut patients swell from a touch. Injected drugs and stings can be immediate and severe. They are already past the skin. Penicillin is the most common drug allergy worldwide. Also common: plants, chemicals, makeup, smells, seasonal stuff, latex. Ask how bad this has gotten for them before, how fast, and when they were exposed this time. If last time the throat closed at 20 minutes and they were exposed 10 minutes ago, expect them to still get worse.

Insect stings can stay local in one spot or spread through the body. Honeybees cannot pull the stinger out. It can keep pumping venom for about 20 minutes. About 62 deaths a year in the US come from insect stings. About half of those deaths had no reaction to earlier stings. Local signs: pain, swelling, warmth, redness, itching, one wheal. Severe signs: stridor, wheezing, hard to breathe, anxiety, nausea, low blood pressure. Scrape the stinger. Do not tweeze.

Untreated anaphylaxis can kill quickly. More than two-thirds of anaphylaxis deaths happen in the first 30 minutes. Airway swelling and low oxygen kill first. Low blood pressure is second. That is why oxygen still comes first, then Epi as soon as you know it is anaphylaxis.

Practice

Do this: walk a rash-only sting, then a sting plus stridor. You did it right if only the second one got Epi after oxygen.

Do this: map alpha to squeeze, beta-1 to the heart, and beta-2 to the lower airways. You did it right if you also said Epi does not wipe histamine already in the blood.

Do this: lay a hypotensive, short-of-breath patient down. You did it right if shock beat High Fowler.

Do this: refuse an OPA that will not pass a swollen tongue. You did it right if you still moved toward Epi and did not wait for the swelling to reverse.

Do this: scrape, rings off, wash, ice. You did it right if tweezers never touched the stinger.

Do this: call medical control before a second pen. You did it right if current blood pressure and heart rate were in the report.

Do this: name penicillin as the most common drug allergy. You did it right if food timing was 20–30 minutes, not instant by default.
