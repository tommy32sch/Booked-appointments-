# Head and spine injuries — detailed

Work from the outside in: scalp → skull → bleeds around the brain → the brain itself → the cord.

## Big rules

The primary brain injury already happened. Your job is to fight secondary killers: low oxygen, low blood pressure, and infection.

Bleed and pain should make a FAST heart. Slow heart after trauma is a red flag for only two problems. Use blood pressure to tell them apart:

- Slow heart + HIGH blood pressure → pressure inside the head (Cushing’s).
- Slow heart + LOW blood pressure → spinal cord / neurogenic shock.

Care basics: jaw thrust. No NPA. Stop bleeding. Wrap the head loosely. Keep oxygen up. Do not let the blood pressure crash. Do not drop it with nitro. Open head wound gets a dry sterile dressing.

## Anatomy

Central nervous system = brain + spinal cord.

- Cerebrum = thinking, language, senses, personality. Right brain runs the left body.
- Cerebellum = balance and coordination.
- Brainstem = breathing, blood pressure, pupils, temperature. Deepest and most protected.

Meninges, outside to inside:

- Dura: tough outer layer.
- Arachnoid: middle. CSF lives here. Bigger vessels live here.
- Pia: sits on the brain.

CSF is made inside the brain (about 125 mL). It cushions like fluid around the heart or a baby. It can leak out only if the skull is cracked.

Foramen magnum = the big hole at the base of the skull. Late in rising pressure the brainstem gets shoved down that hole (herniation).

Vagus nerve (cranial nerve 10) goes to the heart. Stimulate it → heart slows. That is parasympathetic. Rising pressure in the head hits the vagus.

## Cushing’s triad

Pressure inside the skull squeezes brain against bone. That tissue loses blood flow. The brain raises blood pressure to push blood in. That squeeze gets worse. It keeps raising the pressure.

The three signs:

1. High and WIDE blood pressure. Systolic shoots up, diastolic stays nearer normal. Example: 200/90. Regular high blood pressure usually climbs together, like 160/110.
2. Slow heart. Pressure hits the vagus nerve.
3. Irregular breathing. Brainstem is being squeezed. Cheyne-Stokes = slow/shallow → deep/fast → pause → repeat. Ataxic = no pattern at all.

The same vital-sign pattern can happen from a tumor, extra CSF, or infection. Trauma is the usual context.

Do not give nitro to drop that blood pressure. Nitro is for heart-attack chest pain. A drop in BP after a brain injury is a secondary killer.

In real life a person can have a head injury AND be bleeding out. Low volume wins. You lose the high-BP clue.

## Skull fractures

The bone itself is not the emergency. The brain under it is. Bruises may not show yet. Mechanism still counts.

- Linear: about 80%. Crack only. Feels stable. Hospital X-ray finds it.
- Depressed: high-energy blunt hit. Dent or soft spot. Do not keep pushing. Bone may be driven into the brain.
- Open: better for pressure (the box can give). Worse for infection (dirt goes straight to the brain). Dry sterile dressing.
- Basilar (base of skull): raccoon eyes. Battle’s sign (bruise on the mastoid, behind the ear). CSF from nose or ear.

## Bleeds inside the skull

All of these are intracranial. Hematoma and hemorrhage are used almost the same way here.

- Epidural (above the dura, under the skull): usually an artery. Fast. Lucid interval — down → up → down: hit, pass out, wake up talking, pass out again and stay out. Real life only about 1/3 do this pattern.
- Subdural (under the dura): usually a vein. Slow. Fell yesterday, seemed fine, found down today. Common in older people and alcoholics.
- Subarachnoid (under the arachnoid): sudden worst headache. Clutch the head and drop. Same sudden-headache clue as a hemorrhagic stroke.
- Intracerebral (in the brain itself): trauma name = intracerebral bleed. Medical name = hemorrhagic stroke. Same problem, different label. Symptoms depend on which part of the brain is hit.

There is no “sub-pia” bleed. Below the pia is the brain.

## Concussion vs contusion

- Concussion: brain function is off. CT looks normal. Confusion, amnesia, repeat questions, dizzy, nausea, ringing, slurred speech. About 90% never pass out.
- Contusion: CT shows bruised brain. Can last or be permanent. Same symptoms until they scan.

You cannot tell them apart on scene. Hope for a concussion. Treat as if the brain is damaged.

## Spinal cord

Motor signals go down. Sensory signals go up. Cut the freeway and both stop below that level.

Complete cut = no feel and no move below. Above the injury still works.

Bones out of place can pinch the cord without cutting it. Same numbness and paralysis until the pinch is gone. When the bones pop back and the pressure comes off, feeling and movement can return. Pressure, not a cut.

MOI words — what happened to the spine:

- Flexion: bent too far forward.
- Extension: head snapped back (rear-end).
- Axial load: compressed. Land on the head or the feet. Diving.
- Distraction: pulled apart. Hanging. Different from a “distracting injury,” which is the ugly wound that steals your eyes.

### When to board

Boards cause pressure sores. You do not board every wreck anymore. Need a reason:

- Unconscious (you cannot ask).
- Numbness or paralysis.
- Neck or back pain after trauma.
- You do not believe them (drunk, head rattled, they jumped when you pressed the spine).

Walking around after the wreck is a reason not to board just because of the crash.

### Helmet

Leave it on if it fits, holds the head still, and you can still do airway and breathing.

Take it off if it is full-face and you cannot manage the airway, if the head rattles inside, if they are in arrest, or if you cannot immobilize with it on.

Two people: one holds the head, one slides the helmet off without bending the neck.

Bring the helmet. The damage is a clue for the doctors.

Football: the face mask can come off and the helmet can stay.

## Care

- Jaw thrust. No NPA.
- Stop bleeding. Scalp bleeds a lot. Wrap the head loosely.
- Keep oxygen up. One minute of low oxygen after a brain injury makes death much more likely.
- Do not let the blood pressure crash. Do not drop it with nitro.
- Open head wound: dry sterile dressing so germs do not go into the brain.
