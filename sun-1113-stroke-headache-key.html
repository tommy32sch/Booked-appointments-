Stroke and headache overview

Key notes. Sunday 11:13. 12-question recap.

Stroke is interrupted blood flow to part of the brain. Motor nerves cross in the brainstem, so a right-brain stroke hits the left body, and a left-brain stroke hits the right body. Left-brain strokes often bring speech problems. Right-brain strokes can leave them unaware of the deficit.

About 87 percent are ischemic: a clot that formed there or traveled there. AFib and plaque raise clot risk. About 13 percent are hemorrhagic: a vessel bursts, often from chronic high pressure. Those are more often fatal. Clot treatment wants to break clots. Bleed treatment wants clotting. One can kill the other. A CT of the head is how the hospital tells them apart. You can suspect stroke. You do not treat the type.

Brain cells start dying in about 10 minutes without blood and oxygen. Clot-buster drugs are often only given if they are inside about 3 hours from onset. Get last known well as exactly as you can: last time anyone saw them normal. If they were last normal at 1900 and you find them in the morning, last known well is 1900.

Signs are often one-sided: facial droop on a smile, arm drift with palms up and eyes closed, one-sided weakness or numbness, slurred speech, aphasia, vision changes, sudden severe headache. Expressive aphasia: they understand you but cannot get words out. Receptive aphasia: they do not process what you said and may talk nonsense. Ask if this is new or their old baseline.

Hypoglycemia can look exactly like a stroke. Always check sugar on an altered or stroke-like patient before you lock in stroke. A post-seizure state, a complex migraine, and bleeding outside the brain tissue can also mimic. Treat low sugar. Recheck. If it clears, it was likely not a stroke.

Do not give aspirin for stroke. Aspirin in this recap is for suspected cardiac chest pain. Do not delay transport. Rapid to a stroke center if protocol allows. Pre-notify with age, findings, last known well, sugar, vitals.

Tension, migraine, and sinus headaches are usually not emergencies, but you cannot be sure in the field. Red flags: sudden worst-ever headache, or headache plus fever, seizure, altered mental status, or trauma. You have no EMS headache medicine. Support, ABCs, position of comfort, dim lights if they want. Do not recommend over-the-counter pills. If they called 911 for it, push hospital. No lights and sirens unless they have marked neuro findings, look like a major stroke, or are unstable.

  • Do this: check sugar on a stroke-like classmate drill. You did it right if sugar came before you called it a stroke.
  • Do this: name last known well from a found-down story. You did it right if you used last seen normal, not when you arrived.
  • Do this: refuse aspirin on a stroke drill. You did it right if you said it is for cardiac chest pain in this recap, not stroke.
  • Do this: split expressive from receptive aphasia. You did it right if understand-but-cannot-speak was expressive.
  • Do this: pick a sudden worst headache plus fever as a red flag. You did it right if you still transported.
  • Do this: dim the lights and skip recommending ibuprofen. You did it right if they still went to the hospital.
  • Do this: map right-brain stroke to the left body. You did it right if speech problems were the left-brain list.
