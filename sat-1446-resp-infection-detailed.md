# Respiratory and infection overview — detailed

This talk is infection plus breathing complaints, run through your normal medical assessment. Scene size-up with personal protective equipment (PPE). Primary: impression, AVPU, airway, breathing, circulation. History: OPQRST and SAMPLE. Secondary: full vitals and lung sounds, left vs right, upper vs lower. Field impression comes after the data. Reassess.

Fever or hot skin: suspect infection. The life-threat question is septic shock: infection plus hypotension. Protect yourself. High-flow oxygen. Flat if they tolerate it. Keep warm. Rapid transport for fluids and antibiotics you do not have.

Dyspnea is the usual chief complaint. The problem can be airway swelling, a disease process, trauma, or a heart that is backing fluid into the lungs. Residual volume keeps alveoli from collapsing between breaths. Atelectasis is when they do collapse.

Hear stridor, snoring, or gurgling without a stethoscope: upper. Stridor is inspiratory and high. Wheeze, rhonchi, and crackles are lower and need the stethoscope. One pocket of abnormal sound: pneumonia. Both sides wheezing: asthma, bronchiolitis, RSV.

Croup is viral in young kids, usually under 6. Low fever, slow onset, seal-bark, stridor. Humidified oxygen is preferred. Do not withhold dry oxygen. Keep them calm and sitting how they want.

Epiglottitis is bacterial, less common after the Hib vaccine. High fever, fast onset, stridor, tripod, drooling because swallowing hurts. Do not poke the throat. Do not agitate. Same oxygen and calm-upright plan. Rapid transport. Bag if the airway fails.

RSV and bronchiolitis hit infants and toddlers and spread in daycare. Wheezing and a lot of mucus. Suction the nose. Infection control.

Pneumonia can be viral or bacterial. Colored sputum and a localized sound. Elderly and chronically ill get the sickest. Treat ABCs, oxygen, and watch for septic shock.

Pertussis is the whoop after a coughing spell. Flu looks miserable with no unique sound. Tuberculosis is fever, night sweats, and hemoptysis. N95 on you. Mask on them. There is no US vaccine in this recap, which is why the respirator matters.

Sit most short-of-breath patients up. If they are in shock and breathing is okay, they can be supine. You never treat the infection itself.

## Practice
Do this: walk scene, primary, history, lungs, then impression. You did it right if the label came after the data.

Do this: pick N95 for TB or a high-risk cough and fever. You did it right if you were in a respirator and they had a surgical mask.

Do this: name residual volume in one sentence. You did it right if leftover air keeps alveoli open.

Do this: call gurgling a suction problem. You did it right if you treated it as fluid in the upper airway.

Do this: put a pneumonia patient on oxygen and recheck pressure. You did it right if septic shock was on your list.

Do this: keep a croup kid calm in a position of comfort. You did it right if you did not wrestle them flat.

Do this: ask fever, cough color, sick contacts, and past lung or heart disease. You did it right if those four got asked.

Do this: reassess ABCs and mental status on the way in. You did it right if you did not set it and forget it.
