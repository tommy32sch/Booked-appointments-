Soft tissue part 1 — detailed

This half is bleeding control and special wounds. Burns come later.

All bleeding control starts with direct pressure. Most bleeds stop with that. When direct pressure works, put on a pressure dressing so you can use your hand again. A pressure dressing is not a tourniquet. It does not cut off all flow. If the bleed is on an arm or leg and pressure does not stop it, go straight to a tourniquet. Do not keep stacking gauze. If the bleed is in the groin or just inside the shoulder, you cannot get a tourniquet above it. Pack that wound with clotting gauze. Tourniquets and wound packing hurt a lot. Do them anyway. Lost red cells cannot carry oxygen. Blood at the hospital takes time.

Book tourniquet placement: a couple inches closer to the body than the hole (proximal). High and tight means all the way up the limb. Police and military teach high and tight so a second wound higher up is still covered. Either is okay. The most common tourniquet here is the CAT (combat application tourniquet).

How to put a CAT on: Velcro the strap down tight first. That alone already slows a lot of flow. Twist the stick until the bleeding STOPS — not until it “feels tight.” Lock the stick in the C-clamp. Write the time on the white strap. No blood and no oxygen are reaching that tissue, so time matters. A loose gap between the band and the arm cannot be fixed by twisting.

Skill station (shortest sheet, about 60 seconds): body substance isolation (BSI) and scene safety. Major arm bleed → life threat → direct pressure. Still bleeds → tourniquet on. Bleed stopped, patient in shock → high-flow oxygen, lay them down (supine), keep warm, rapid transport. People fail it by not practicing the CAT.

Other tourniquets: a stretchy / elastic band wraps tighter and tighter and conforms to any size. Better for small kid arms. The CAT has a stiff plastic piece that may not wrap a tiny arm. A junctional tourniquet is not common on local EMS trucks. It is more military / tactical. Use it for a bleed just inside the shoulder or in the groin — close to the surface but too high for a regular tourniquet. It is a belt with an inflatable pad you pump like a blood pressure cuff. The pad pushes down into the hole.

Wound packing uses clotting powder or, more often, gauze soaked with a clotting chemical (Celox-type). One common roll is about 3 inches by 5 feet. Shove it down to where the blood is coming from. Then hold hard pressure. First pack is about 80% effective. If it soaks through, pull it and pack a fresh one → about 90%. Use packing for groin / inside-shoulder holes. Arms and legs: just use a tourniquet. If they fight you because it hurts, stopping the bleed still comes first.

Crush on the belly versus the arm: an arm crush can use a tourniquet to trap junk from spreading. A belly crush has no tourniquet option. When the weight comes off, the next few minutes can go bad. Call medical control. Get them to the hospital.

Every open wound is dirty and carries infection risk. Do not pick rocks or dirt out. Leave cleanup to the hospital. Dirt is not actively making it worse right now. Chemical in the wound is still burning, so wash that now.

Evisceration means organs sticking out through an open belly wound — often intestines. Worries: bleeding, infection, organs drying out, heat loss. Skin controls temperature. Exposed guts dump heat. Do not push them back in — same idea as a popped-out shoulder. If it is out of place, protect it. Put a moist sterile dressing right on the organs to keep them wet. Then put a plastic / occlusive dressing over that. The plastic acts like skin and traps heat. Wet things lose heat faster, so you need both layers. You make a moist dressing by pouring sterile water or saline on regular sterile gauze. Anything that should be inside and is now outside gets a moist dressing. Eyeballs too.

Impaled objects stay in. Pack gauze around them so they do not wiggle. Dressings are for stopping more injury, more infection, and bleeding. Take an impaled object out only if it is in the cheek or mouth AND it blocks the airway you need to manage (breathing is fine → it can stay), or it is in the chest or back AND you need to do CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation).

Penetrating neck wounds sit in a small space with airway, carotids, jugulars, spine, and esophagus. For a major bleed: direct pressure on that side only. Never wrap all the way around the neck. Never squeeze both sides — the brain passes out. Put gauze on the wound and wrap under the opposite armpit. If air is getting into a vessel, a bubble acts like a clot and gets stuck — use an occlusive dressing to keep air out. No tourniquet on the neck.

Animal and human bites are filthy with high infection risk. The hospital may clean it out, give antibiotics, tetanus, or operate. You do none of that. EMT care is a dry sterile dressing — just cover it. Rabies shots are a hospital / public-health call if they know the animal. A human bite can be a punch to the mouth where a tooth cuts the knuckle. If they wait, the hand can swell so tight that blood flow drops (like compartment syndrome). Still just cover it.

Sometimes you immobilize even if it is not broken. Splinting can slow blood flow to the area. Less motion means less extra blood and swelling builds slower.

An open / sucking chest wound gets an occlusive (plastic) dressing. Tape 3 sides so extra air can vent out. Store-bought seals do the same job. Which side you leave open does not really matter.

Practice

Do this: say the big bleed path out loud — direct pressure first, then pressure dressing or tourniquet or packing. You did it right if groin/inside-shoulder packed and arms/legs got a tourniquet.

Do this: place a CAT proximal a couple inches or high and tight. You did it right if you wrote the time and locked the stick in the C-clamp.

Do this: twist until bleeding stops, not until it feels tight. You did it right if you Velcroed tight first and knew a loose gap cannot be fixed by twisting.

Do this: run BSI → pressure → TQ → oxygen / supine / warm / go for the shocked major-arm-bleed station. You did it right if the whole path fit about 60 seconds of practiced moves.

Do this: name junctional TQ as military/tactical with an inflatable pad for groin or inside-shoulder. You did it right if local EMS trucks were not assumed to carry it.

Do this: belly crush → medical control and hospital; no TQ. You did it right if arm crush still allowed a TQ to trap junk.

Do this: moist then occlusive for evisceration; dry sterile for bites. You did it right if dirt stayed in place and chemical got washed now.

Do this: leave impaled objects unless airway block in cheek/mouth or CPR needs chest/back clear. You did it right if neck pressure stayed one-sided with wrap under the opposite armpit.
