Types of shock and CPR fundamentals

Detailed notes. Saturday 10:00. 14-question recap.

This class is types of shock plus a BLS/CPR start. Ask four things: pump, pipe, or fluid. What one or two clues name the type. Are they still compensating or crashing. Then do standard shock care plus any type-specific step you are allowed.

Stages

Compensated: epinephrine is still holding. Adult systolic ≥ 90. Heart rate and breathing up. Skin often cool, pale, clammy (sepsis and anaphylaxis can look different). They may look okay.

Decompensated: that hold fails. Adult systolic under 90. Poor perfusion. Some tests say uncompensated; it means the same thing.

Kids compensate longer, then crash fast once pressure drops. Capillary refill over 2 seconds is delayed and is a shock sign in kids even with a near-normal pressure.

Distributive (pipes too open)

Septic: infection and toxins. Vessels dilate and leak. Often dry from poor intake or vomiting and diarrhea. Fever or an infection source, hot or flushed skin, low pressure. No antibiotics in the field. Oxygen, supine, warm, rapid transport, tell the hospital possible sepsis.

Neurogenic: high cord injury. Sympathetic tone is gone below the injury, so those vessels open. Trauma, low pressure, slow pulse (almost every other shock speeds the pulse). Numbness or paralysis if they are awake. Cool pale clammy above the injury, warm flushed below. Spinal immobilization (backboard, C-spine). Keep warm. Trauma center. ALS for fluids.

Anaphylaxis: two or more systems after an allergen (skin, breathing, circulation, gut). Hives plus trouble breathing, low pressure, or nausea is enough. Adult epinephrine 0.3 mg IM lateral thigh. Child 0.15 mg. Hold about 3 seconds. Airway and oxygen before the drug, then shock care.

Psychogenic: a scare or the sight of blood opens vessels for a moment. They faint, then often wake when they are flat. Not true cellular shock. Still work it as a medical call. Look for a serious cause. Check injuries from the fall.

Hypovolemic (not enough volume)

Hemorrhagic: bleeding you see or internal (trauma, belly pain, bruising, vomiting blood). Direct pressure, then a tourniquet if you need it. Shock plus a mechanism and no visible bleed: treat as internal bleeding and go to a surgery-capable hospital.

Non-hemorrhagic: days of vomiting or diarrhea, no intake, or burns with third-spacing (fluid leaves the vessels into tissue; swelling and blisters are clues).

Pump and obstructive

Cardiogenic: the pump is weak, often a big MI. Chest pain, pulmonary edema, low pressure, poor perfusion. No nitro if hypotensive. Aspirin only if it is indicated for MI and they have no contraindication.

Obstructive: the pump is blocked from outside. Tamponade: JVD, muffled tones, narrowing pulse pressure; needs hospital drainage. Tension: trauma, absent sounds on one side, JVD, possibly tracheal deviation; needle decompression is paramedic or ED. PE is a clot in a pulmonary artery. Standard shock care and rapid ALS. You do not fix these as an EMT.

Always

High-flow oxygen, non-rebreather or bag from breathing adequacy. Supine unless breathing falls apart. Keep warm, even if they feel febrile. Rapid transport. Unstable reassess every 5 minutes. No food or drink. Request ALS; fluids are a paramedic step.

CPR

Alive patients: ABC. Suspected arrest: CAB, circulation first. Unresponsive: check breathing and pulse together, 10 seconds or less. Adults and children: carotid. Infants under 1 year: brachial. No definite pulse and not breathing normally: compressions first. High-quality compressions. Pauses 10 seconds or less. There is already oxygen in the blood for the first minutes, which is why moving blood matters.

AED looks for ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. It does not restart the heart. It stops the chaos so a pacemaker can try. Flatline (asystole): no shock advised. Clear for analyze and shock. Resume CPR right after a shock or a no-shock prompt.

Practice

  • Do this: write pump, pipe, and fluid with one example each. You did it right if pump is cardiogenic, pipe is distributive, and fluid is hypovolemic.
  • Do this: run a neurogenic vs septic skin and pulse contrast. You did it right if neurogenic is slow pulse plus a warm line below the injury, and septic is fever and hot skin.
  • Do this: name anaphylaxis from two systems and give adult epi. You did it right if 0.3 mg IM lateral thigh, hold about 3 seconds.
  • Do this: hold food and drink on a shock drill. You did it right if nothing by mouth.
  • Do this: switch ABC to CAB on an unresponsive, not-breathing classmate drill. You did it right if compressions start first.
  • Do this: time a pulse-and-breathing check. You did it right if it stays at 10 seconds or less.
  • Do this: pick brachial on an infant and carotid on an adult. You did it right if the sites match age.
  • Do this: say asystole to an AED prompt. You did it right if you said no shock advised and you restarted CPR.
