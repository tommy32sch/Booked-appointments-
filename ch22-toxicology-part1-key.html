Toxicology part 1 — key

First step: recognize this is a toxicology event. Step 2: try to name the substance. How they look depends on what they took. Most poisons: you cannot pull them out. Do airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs) and go to the hospital. Opioids are the special one. You have naloxone (Narcan) for those. Give oxygen and ABCs before Narcan. Same idea as oxygen before epinephrine (Epi).

A poison is any substance whose chemical action can damage the body or stop it from working right. A toxin is a poison made by bacteria, animals, or plants. Substance abuse is using a substance the wrong way to get a wanted effect. Overdose is a toxic dose. It can just mean they took the next pill too soon. A toxidrome is the pattern a drug class makes. Heroin and fentanyl look the same. Potentiate means one drug makes another drug stronger, like alcohol plus a sleeping pill. Tolerance means they need more and more to get the same effect. Miosis means pinpoint / very small pupils. That is a classic opioid clue. An adsorbent binds a poison in the gut so the body does not absorb it. Charcoal is that kind of drug.

There is no one overdose look. Stimulants (meth) push everything up: blood pressure, breathing, heart rate, energy. Depressants (heroin, alcohol, benzodiazepines) push everything down: blood pressure, breathing, heart rate, mental status. Breathing going down is the life threat for depressants.

Look for needles, syringes, empty alcohol bottles, pill bottles, labels, pill pieces in vomit, chemical leaking from a truck, or a drug-lab look. Safe senses: sight and sound. Not safe: smell, touch, and never taste. If you smell it, you already breathed it. Your safety first. A gas leak may not be safe for you. A pill overdose is usually safer for you than a gas leak.

Ask and write down: what was it, when, how much and over how long, anything to eat or drink before or after, any antidote already given, and how much they weigh. Twelve hours ago is already absorbed. Five minutes ago may still be in the stomach. Symptoms may not have started yet. Bring bottles, labels, and workplace chemical sheets (MSDS = material safety data sheet) if you can. Tell the hospital even if you do not know what it was.

Four routes: inhale, absorb through skin, swallow, inject. Injection is the fastest life threat. It goes straight into the blood. Inhalation can also get bad fast because it hits airway and breathing. About 80% of poisonings are swallowed. First job: stop the problem from getting worse. Then try to make it better.

Inhale: get them into fresh air. You cannot wash out the lungs. Give high-flow oxygen instead: 10–15 L/min on a non-rebreather (NRB) or bag-valve mask (BVM). The device still depends on rate, rhythm, and quality. Rapid transport.

Absorb: do not get it on you. Take off clothes. Powder: brush off first, then flush what is left. Liquid: go straight to flush. Flush 15–20 minutes. Five minutes is wrong. If it needs a special hazmat suit, that is hazmat’s job. If nobody knows what it is, wash it off. Most things are not water-reactive.

Swallow: ABCs plus rapid transport. Call Poison Control. Kids: usually an accident. Adults: often on purpose.

Inject: ABCs plus transport. You cannot dilute it or take it out in the field. Narcan only if you think it is an opioid.

Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222. They are the poison experts. They are not medical control. If they tell you to do something you are not already allowed to do: hang up, call medical control, tell the doctor what they said. Many agencies say: if Poison Control tells you to do it and it is in your scope, just do it.

Activated charcoal is an adsorbent. It binds poison in the gut so the small intestine cannot absorb it. Dose concept: 1 gram per kilogram. They must be awake and able to protect the airway. It is not in Arizona EMT scope. Rare. Know it exists. Do not make people vomit on purpose. The old vomit drug was syrup of ipecac. Extra vomit raises the risk of stuff going into the lungs.

Tolerance is dangerous after they get clean. They lose tolerance, then take the old amount. Worst overdoses are often a relapse, not “they took more today.”

Alcohol is a central nervous system (CNS) depressant. Everything down. Breathing down is the life threat. Alcohol potentiates other drugs. You cannot reverse it. ABCs and transport. Delirium tremens (DTs) is a heavy long-time drinker who stops cold turkey. Agitation, restlessness, fever, sweating, shakes, confusion, delusions, hallucinations, seizures. Treat the symptoms. ABCs first.

Opioids: slow and shallow breathing is what kills them. Heart and blood pressure go down. Mental status goes down. Pinpoint pupils (miosis) are a classic clue, not a guarantee. Narcan (naloxone) only works on opioids. It does nothing for alcohol, benzodiazepines, or barbiturates. Intranasal (IN) is preferred. No needle. Give oxygen / BVM before Narcan. Do not stand there waiting for Narcan while they get no air. If you expect them to wake up fast, a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) may be smarter than an oropharyngeal airway (OPA). OPA plus gag reflex back can mean vomit. You can still spray Narcan in the other nostril if an NPA is in one side.

Barbiturates have “barb” in the name. Benzodiazepines end in -pam or -lam. Both are CNS depressants. Everything down. Breathing down is the immediate life threat. Narcan will do nothing. Different receptors. If Narcan twice does nothing, think “not an opioid,” or mixed drugs.

Practice

Do this: say the first step out loud on a mystery bottle call. You did it right if you named it a toxicology event before you named the drug.

Do this: pick sight and sound as safe senses, and refuse smell, touch, and taste. You did it right if smell was treated as already inhaled.

Do this: ask what, when, how much, food or drink, antidote already given, and weight. You did it right if bottles or an MSDS came with you.

Do this: brush powder first, then flush 15–20 minutes. You did it right if liquid went straight to flush and five minutes was never the answer.

Do this: move an inhaled-poison patient to fresh air, then high-flow oxygen at 10–15 L/min. You did it right if the device still matched rate, rhythm, and quality.

Do this: give oxygen or BVM before Narcan on an opioid drill. You did it right if air came before the spray.

Do this: spray Narcan only for an opioid picture. You did it right if alcohol, a benzo, and a barb got no Narcan.

Do this: choose an NPA when you expect a fast wake. You did it right if you still sprayed Narcan in the other nostril.

Do this: dial Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222, then hang up and call medical control if they ask out of scope. You did it right if Poison Control was never treated as medical control.

Do this: refuse forced vomiting and syrup of ipecac. You did it right if charcoal stayed “know it exists,” adsorbent, 1 g/kg, awake with an airway, and not Arizona EMT scope.
