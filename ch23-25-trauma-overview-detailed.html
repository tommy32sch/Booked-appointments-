Trauma overview (Ch 23–25) — detailed

Short wrap of behavioral health and gyn. Main lecture is Chapter 25. Know the three car-crash collisions, fall factors, blast phases, and Platinum 10. Soft tissue injuries come next.

The big rule: index of suspicion means how worried you are about injuries you cannot see yet. Those inside problems need surgery. There is no operating room in the ambulance. Do not waste time wrapping every broken bone while they bleed inside or the brain dies. Biggest driver of survival: get them to a trauma center fast. Platinum 10 minutes = from arrival on scene to wheels rolling.

Chapter 23

Behavioral health is a light review. Scene safety first. Always. Do not start with “this is just psych.” Do a full medical check. It could be low oxygen, low blood sugar, or a head injury. Ask people who know them: how is this compared with their normal? Nobody who knows them is there → treat it as new and bad. Then airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs) and transport. Restraints were already taught.

Chapter 25

Nature of illness (NOI) is a medical problem: chest pain, trouble breathing, belly pain. Mechanism of injury (MOI) is what physically happened: fall, crash, gunshot, ejected, car vs person. “Fall” is not enough. Standing-height versus 30 feet off a roof are different. Landing on the feet versus landing on the head are different. Most common trauma calls: car crashes and falls. Not shootings and stabbings. Bruising is bleeding under the skin. It often does not show yet on scene. Do not wait for bruises. Pain plus a real MOI is enough. Fast heart rate or fast breathing means the body is reacting. Go.

Kinetic energy is energy of motion. The formula is ½ mass × speed². A little more speed matters more than a little more weight. A huge weight change still wins (bike at 10 mph versus semi at 5 mph). Potential energy for falls: height is the big number. Main trauma types: blunt, penetrating, and burns. Barotrauma (pressure, like scuba) is its own small group. Blunt = force spread over a wide area; skin is not cut open. Penetrating = lots of force on a small spot; goes through the skin. Burns are their own category. They can have both blunt and penetrating injuries. Treat life threats first (often an outside bleed). Inside bleeding you cannot stop → hospital. Then do the head-to-toe.

Three collisions in a car crash. (1) Vehicle: the car hits the object and stops. You can see this damage on scene. (2) Body: the person keeps going and hits the wheel, dash, airbag, or windshield. (3) Organs: the body stops; organs keep going and smash the inside of the body. Collision 3 is the life threat. Organs can tear off their attachment points. The aorta can shear at the top of the heart → bleed out in seconds. A kidney can tear off the renal artery and vein. Gut vessels can tear. You cannot fix that in the field. Surgery.

What to tell the hospital: how smashed was the car; where was the patient sitting; how far the car was dented in (intrusion), and WHERE. Twelve inches into the trunk is not the same as twelve inches into the driver’s door. The trunk and engine are built to crush. Intrusion into the passenger compartment (where people sit) is the big worry. Cars are well built. A wrecked car can still have people walking around. Still use the damage as a starting concern. Inside the car — assume the worst: bent steering wheel → chest hit it; damaged lower dash → knee hit it; starred / cracked windshield → head hit it. Force travels. Knee hits the dash → force goes up the leg → hip can shatter even if the hip never hit anything. That is an indirect injury. Coup-contrecoup: head hits → brain slides forward; front of the brain smashes; back of the brain tears away; then it can whip the other way. Brain ping-pongs inside the skull.

Significant crash = higher worry: someone else in the car died; severe denting especially into where people sit; severe rear damage; rollover, spin, or rotation (organs get yanked many ways); ejected from the car. Also ask: seatbelt on or off?

Other crash patterns. Motorcycle thrown forward: classic broken femurs in both legs (legs hit the bars). Look at bike damage, where the rider landed, helmet / leathers / boots. Car vs person: adult versus a regular car bumper (about knee height) is more likely to go up and over. A child versus the same bumper (about chest height) is more likely to go under. If the car does not stop they may also get run over. Ask about helmet or safety gear. These patients get spine care / a backboard. Head-on, T-bone, and rear-end crashes hit the body in different ways. You will not always be right. It is a starting point.

Falls: three things grade how bad the fall is. Height: 20 feet is the magic number. Also more than 2–3 times the person’s height (better for kids). A 6-foot fall can be huge for a toddler. Surface: cement versus grass versus a foam pit — did anything soak up the hit? What hit first: feet versus head. Very different injuries. Not a guarantee. A standing-height fall in an older person can still break a lot of bones. Older bones break easier. Always add what the patient actually looks like.

Penetrating trauma energy: low = stabbing, hand-powered; medium = handgun; high = rifle. A handgun bullet and a rifle bullet are close in size. The rifle is much faster, so it carries more energy. Knife: assume the worst. Best case = it went straight in. Worst case = it was twisted, the blade is as long as it could be, and it hit everything in range (heart, aorta, lung, diaphragm, spleen, stomach). Whatever it hit needs surgery. Bullet path inside is unknown even if you see an entrance and an exit. Cavitation = the bullet pushes nearby tissue aside. Things can be hurt even if the bullet did not hit them. You do not need to know guns. Ask police what it was. Tell the hospital.

Leave impaled objects in. The object can plug the hole and slow bleeding. Pull it and the dam comes out. Take it out only if something worse is blocked: (1) in the way of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and they have no pulse — if they still have a pulse, leave it; (2) in the way of the airway; (3) they are stuck on something you cannot move. No CPR or no airway = 100% dead. Bleeding after you pull it = very high chance of death, but not 100%. Pick the less bad option. Impaled in the back plus no pulse: it still has to come out so you can lay them flat for CPR. Long rebar: fire cuts it shorter. The object stays in. Stabilize it. You can ride them on their side if needed. A bandage over a chest hole will not stop bleeding deep inside. You are only covering the hole.

Blast injuries in four phases. Primary: pressure wave. Hits hollow spaces. Eardrums pop easiest (not the killer). Lungs are the real worry: collapsed lung or air pushed into the blood (air bubbles act like clots). Secondary: shrapnel and debris — blunt and penetrating. Tertiary: body is thrown into the ground or an object. Quaternary: everything else that day — building collapse, burns, toxic gas, broken pipes. If the pressure wave hit them, they were close enough that the other phases could have hit too. Injury from each phase is not guaranteed. You cannot fix air bubbles in the field. Hospital. A pressure chamber can shrink them (same idea as scuba). That may not be possible if they also have major trauma.

Trauma assessment and time. Order on scene: gloves → scene safety → what happened → how many patients → extra help → think about the spine → XABCs → board them → trauma center. XABCs: X = huge outside bleed. Comes BEFORE the airway. You usually see this the first second you look at them. Stop and fix it. Lost red cells = lost oxygen carriers. You do not carry blood. Medic intravenous (IV) fluid adds volume but carries no oxygen. Stop the bleed, then airway, breathing, circulation as usual. Then treat for shock, immobilize the spine, and drive.

Platinum 10 minutes: from the moment you park to the moment you start driving. XABCs, board them, go. Secondary exam, history, and medic IVs happen on the way. This is for a real trauma concern. Not a rolled ankle on a curb. High priority: significant MOI, any altered mind, any ABC problem.

Helicopter landing zone: ask early. Helicopters take time to arrive. Minimum landing zone: 60 feet by 60 feet. Better: 100 by 100. Does not have to be right next to the wreck. Nearest safe open space is fine (school playground). Local flight crew is usually a pilot, a flight nurse, and a flight medic. The pilot is usually just a pilot.

More than one patient: do not leave the patient you already have. New wreck next to you: send someone if you can, and call more help. Not enough hands for everyone → mass casualty / triage. Those rules are different and come later. Two critical patients and a two-person crew is already too much to treat well at the same time. A dead patient in the car: work the living. You still cannot abandon anyone you started with unless it becomes a true mass-casualty triage event.

Closing clues: index of suspicion = unseen inside injury. Real treatment for bad trauma = fast transport to surgery. Three collisions: vehicle, body, organs. Organs = life threat. Intrusion into where people sit matters more than the trunk. Starred windshield = head. Bent wheel = chest. Dash = knee. Falls: height, surface, what hit first. 20 feet, or 2–3 times their height. Leave impaled objects in unless they block CPR or the airway. Blast primary = pressure wave / hollow organs / lungs. X comes before ABC when there is a huge outside bleed. Platinum 10 = on-scene time for critical trauma. Do not wait for bruises to show.
