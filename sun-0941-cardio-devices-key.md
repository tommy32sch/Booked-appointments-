# Cardiovascular emergencies and devices — key

Hypertensive emergency is typically a systolic pressure over 180 with end-organ signs. Think above-the-shoulders: headache, bounding pulse, ringing in the ears, nosebleed, nausea, dizziness, altered mental status. The pipes can burst. Stroke and aortic dissection are the big field risks. Do not give nitroglycerin just to drop the pressure. It falls, then rebounds, and the rebound is when rupture risk is highest. Calm them. Position of comfort. Rapid transport. Blood-pressure drugs are for the emergency department or paramedics.

An aneurysm is a ballooned artery. A dissection is blood tearing between the wall layers. If the aorta ruptures they can lose nearly all their blood in about a minute. Classic field picture: sudden, 10 out of 10 from the start, tearing chest pain that goes to the back between the shoulder blades. That is not crushing, squeezing MI pain that builds. Arms can have different pressures. Time-critical. High-flow oxygen per protocol. Limit movement. Do not give nitro just to drop pressure.

Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) sews a clean artery onto the heart to bypass clogs. A stent is a metal tube after a balloon pushes plaque aside. Both mean real heart disease. They can still have an MI. Stent patients are often on antiplatelet drugs.

A pacemaker sits as a lump near the clavicle. Fixed-rate fires a set rate. Demand fires only when the heart is too slow. If it fails, the old slow rhythm comes back. Treat bradycardia and shock. You cannot fix the pacemaker.

An implanted cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) watches the rhythm and shocks inside for ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia. The jolt is low and internal. You can touch them when it fires. If they arrest, do standard CPR and AED. An external defibrillator vest is a wearable AED. It warns out loud. Do not touch while it shocks.

A ventricular assist device (VAD) is a pump from the left ventricle to the aorta. Many are continuous flow: no palpable pulse, and a cuff or pulse ox can lie. If they are talking, they are perfusing. If the power dies, they die. Keep the batteries and controller. Call the phone number on the controller and follow that team.

AED shocks ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. No shock advised only means it is not those two. Check a pulse. No shock and no pulse: keep CPR. No shock and a pulse: return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). Kids usually arrest from respiratory failure, then hypoxia, then a slow heart, then pulseless electrical activity or asystole. Still put an AED on. Expect no shock more often. Leave pads on. It rechecks about every two minutes. Stop the ambulance to shock. Hairy chest: shave or stick a pad set and rip it off, then put a second set on. Do not delay a shock for small jewelry. After ROSC, adult breaths every 6 seconds. Kids every 2 to 3 seconds. Saturation 94 to 99 percent.

## Practice
Do this: refuse nitro given only to drop a systolic over 180. You did it right if you named the rebound rupture risk.

Do this: pick tearing sudden 10-out-of-10 pain to the back as dissection. You did it right if you did not call it crushing MI pain that builds.

Do this: find a pacemaker lump and name demand versus fixed-rate. You did it right if demand only fires when the heart is too slow.

Do this: stay on an ICD patient when it fires, then clear a vest. You did it right if the vest got a hands-off like an AED.

Do this: call a talking VAD patient perfusing with no pulse. You did it right if you did not start CPR just for a missing pulse.

Do this: find the phone number on a VAD controller. You did it right if the batteries and box stayed with them.

Do this: after no-shock-advised, check a pulse. You did it right if no pulse meant more CPR.

Do this: stop the ambulance to shock. You did it right if you did not analyze while rolling.
