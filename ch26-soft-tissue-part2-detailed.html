Soft tissue part 2 — detailed

This half is definitions, crush syndrome, wound types, and dressings.

Ugly injuries steal your eyes. Ask: will this kill them in the next minute? If no, note it and keep going. A bone sticking out of the arm looks bad. It will not kill them right now. Not breathing will. On the head-to-toe sheet: find a bump or crepitus → write it down, keep going. Find a major bleed → stop and treat. What actually kills soft-tissue patients: bleeding (hemorrhagic shock) and later infection (septic shock). The wound name is rarely the life threat. The amount of blood is. You do not need vital signs to say “that is a lot of blood.” Compare it to a puddle size instead of guessing milliliters.

Skin: epidermis is the outer layer. Dermis is the inner layer with vessels, nerves, and sweat glands. Jobs: keep germs out, feel the world, control body heat, control fluid. After a big burn stops, worry about them getting cold. Burned skin cannot hold heat. Burn depth names: superficial = 1st degree (epidermis only). Partial thickness = 2nd degree (into the dermis). Full thickness = 3rd degree (through the skin). Also describe burns by what caused them and how much of the body is burned. Full burn talk comes later.

Closed means the skin is still whole. Open means the skin is cut or punctured. Infection risk jumps when the skin is open. Closed injuries can still get infected, but less often. Exception: a hollow belly organ ruptures and leaks. Open fracture = broken bone + a cut in the nearby skin. The bone does not have to stick out.

What the body does after a cut: first the vessel clamps down so blood slows and a clot can form. If the hole is too big, you add pressure to help that clamp. After the bleed stops, vessels open up (inflammation) to bring oxygen, food, and white cells. Then repair, new vessels, collagen, maybe a scar. The body cannot send extra blood until the bleed is stopped. Extra blood too soon = they bleed out.

Closed injuries: contusion / bruise = bleeding under the skin; the skin is not cut. Ecchymosis = the color of a bruise (blue/purple → green/yellow). Hematoma = a bigger pool of blood in tissue or in a body space (example: inside the skull). More blood, more swelling. Bruises often do not show yet on scene. Face and scalp swell a lot because they have many vessels. A bruise is bleeding, but it is almost never a 5-second killer by itself.

Crush injury = a heavy force on the body. How bad = how hard × how long. Tissue past the squeeze gets no blood. Cells go acid, then they die. Crush syndrome = crush lasting 4+ hours. Dead cells dump their insides into the trapped part. Big problem is potassium (K+ lives inside cells). Also acid, cell junk, and muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis / myoglobin). That junk clogs the kidneys. While the weight is still on, the poison stays trapped. When they lift the weight, potassium floods the body. Two killers: heart stops (heart hates extra K+) and kidneys fail. What you do: tourniquet the limb BEFORE they lift the weight. Trap the junk until the hospital can give fluids and drugs. Worse for the arm. Better for the heart and kidneys. Do not loosen a tourniquet unless a doctor says so. A bleed tourniquet left on 4+ hours can CREATE this problem. Get them in before that if you can.

Compartment syndrome: crush / tourniquet = squeeze from the outside. Compartment = squeeze from the inside. Hurt tissue swells. Skin only stretches so far, then it crushes the vessels and nerves inside — an internal tourniquet. Bad enough → no pulse past it. Takes time. Book later says about 6 to 12 hours. A break 10 minutes ago is not compartment syndrome. A kid who broke an arm at recess and sat at home for hours might be. A bit more common in kids.

Open wound types: abrasion = friction scrape (road rash). Open, but not an immediate killer. On the forehead it can be a clue the brain is hurt. Laceration = jagged cut. Incision = smooth cut (like a scalpel). Avulsion = tissue torn into a flap or ripped clean off — a chunk of tissue. Amputation = a body part is off (finger, hand, arm). Puncture = force on a small spot. Chest/belly: little hole outside, bad bleed inside.

Avulsion care: if it is still a flap, lay it back where it belongs and bandage. Do not wash dirt out of the hole. Dirt may be plugging the bleed. Hospital cleans before they sew. Surface dirt you can brush off.

Amputation care: still try direct pressure first if it might work (finger, small child arm). Big adult arm or leg: go straight to a tourniquet. Pressure will not stop that. Bring the part if you can: wrap in sterile dressing → put in plastic → keep cool. Do not delay the ambulance hunting for the part. Life over limb. Fire or police can bring it later.

Chest and upper-belly holes: a hole into the chest lets the diaphragm suck air in the wrong door. That drops the lung faster. Air should only enter down the windpipe. Gauze lets air through. Use plastic (occlusive / chest seal / non-porous). Put one from the sternal notch down to the belly button, front and back. The diaphragm moves every breath. A high belly hole can still be a chest hole. Bullet in and out: seal both holes. This dressing is about air, not usually a big outside bleed. Assume the lung is leaking too. After you plug the wall, pressure can build and squeeze the heart (tension pneumothorax). Tape 3 sides so the dressing can burp like Tupperware. Store-bought vents do the same job.

Closed-injury care: RICE means rest, ice, compression, elevation. Fine for an isolated sprained ankle. Not what you do for a critical multi-trauma. Those patients get shock care and go.

Every bandage is for at least one of three jobs: stop more injury, stop more infection, stop bleeding. Learn the short moist list. Everything else is usually dry sterile. Moist: anything that should be inside the body and is now outside (guts, later eyes). Plastic / occlusive: hole that can suck air into the chest (and the same plastic layer over a wet evisceration dressing to hold heat). Do not push organs or a dislocated joint back in.
