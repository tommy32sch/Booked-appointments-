Toxicology part 1 — detailed

This recap is the first half of toxicology: how you spot a poison call, the four routes in, Poison Control, charcoal as a concept, alcohol, opioids with naloxone (Narcan), and sedatives. Stimulants, food poisoning, and plant poisoning come later.

The most important first step is to recognize this is a toxicology event. Step 2 is to try to name the substance. How they look depends on what they took. Most poisons cannot be pulled out in the field. Do airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs) and go to the hospital. Opioids are the special one. You have Narcan for those. Give oxygen and ABCs before Narcan. Same idea as oxygen before epinephrine (Epi).

Learn the words. A poison is any substance whose chemical action can damage the body or stop it from working right. A toxin is a poison made by bacteria, animals, or plants. Substance abuse is using a substance the wrong way to get a wanted effect. Overdose is a toxic dose. It can just mean they took the next pill too soon. A toxidrome is the pattern a drug class makes. Heroin and fentanyl look the same. Potentiate means one drug makes another drug stronger, like alcohol plus a sleeping pill. Tolerance means they need more and more to get the same effect. Miosis means pinpoint / very small pupils. That is a classic opioid clue. An adsorbent binds a poison in the gut so the body does not absorb it. Charcoal is that kind of drug. Over-the-counter (OTC) drugs often have a wide safety window. Tightly controlled drugs: the helpful dose and the deadly dose are close.

There is no one overdose look. Stimulants such as meth push everything up: blood pressure, breathing, heart rate, energy. Depressants such as heroin, alcohol, and benzodiazepines push everything down: blood pressure, breathing, heart rate, mental status. Breathing going down is the life threat for depressants.

Scene clues: needles, syringes, drug stuff, empty alcohol bottles, pill bottles, labels, pill pieces in vomit, chemical leaking from a truck, or a drug-lab look. Safe senses for hazmat: sight and sound. You can see a leak or hear a hiss from far away. Not safe: smell (if you smell it, you already breathed it), touch, and never taste. Your safety first. A gas leak may not be safe for you. A pill overdose is usually safer for you than a gas leak.

History questions to write down: what was it; when did they take it or get exposed; how much and over how long; anything to eat or drink before or after; any antidote already given (cops often give Narcan before you arrive); how much do they weigh. Twelve hours ago is already absorbed. Five minutes ago may still be in the stomach. Symptoms may not have started yet. Bring bottles, labels, and workplace chemical sheets (MSDS = material safety data sheet) if you can. Tell the hospital even if you do not know what it was. Some bottles now have a QR code. The MSDS first-aid section often says flush 20 minutes and go to the hospital.

Four routes in, same as other drugs: inhale, absorb through skin, swallow, inject. Injection is usually the fastest life threat. It goes straight into the blood. Inhalation can also get bad fast because it hits airway and breathing. About 80% of poisonings are swallowed: pills, alcohol, plants, food. Kids: usually an accident. Adults: often on purpose. First job: stop the problem from getting worse. Then try to make it better.

Inhale: get them out of the bad air into fresh air. You cannot wash out the lungs. Give high-flow oxygen instead. High-flow oxygen means 10–15 L/min on a non-rebreather (NRB) or bag-valve mask (BVM). The device still depends on rate, rhythm, and quality. Rapid transport.

Absorb (chemical on skin): do not get it on you. Take off clothes. Powder: brush off first, then flush what is left. Liquid: go straight to flush. Flush 15–20 minutes. Five minutes is wrong even if the rest of the answer looks right. If it needs a special hazmat suit, that is hazmat’s job. If nobody knows what it is, wash it off. Most things are not water-reactive.

Swallow (ingestion): ABCs plus rapid transport. Call Poison Control.

Inject: intravenous (IV) drugs or venom from a bite or sting. You cannot dilute it or take it out in the field. ABCs plus transport. Narcan only if you think it is an opioid.

Poison Control phone: 1-800-222-1222. They are the poison experts. They can ID a bottle label or a pill by shape and numbers. They are not medical control. If they tell you to do something you are not already allowed to do: hang up, call medical control, tell the doctor what they said. Many agencies say: if Poison Control tells you to do it and it is in your scope, just do it.

Activated charcoal: know it exists. It is not in Arizona EMT scope. Rare. It binds poison in the gut so the small intestine cannot absorb it. Then they poop it out. It works best soon after they swallowed pills. After 20–30 minutes it may already be too late. They must be awake and able to protect the airway. You cannot force them to drink it. Dose concept: 1 gram per kilogram (1 kg = 2.2 lb). Protocols often stop around 50 grams. Shake it first. It is a suspension. Vomiting is very common. That is a problem, not the goal. Do not give charcoal for alkali, cyanide, ethanol, iron, lithium, heavy metals, or anything that burned the throat. Do not make people vomit on purpose. The old vomit drug was syrup of ipecac. Extra vomit means extra risk of stuff going into the lungs.

Tolerance: the user needs more and more to feel the same thing. Dangerous setup: a heavy user gets clean, loses tolerance, then takes the old amount. Worst overdoses are often a relapse, not “they took more today.”

Alcohol is a central nervous system (CNS) depressant. Everything down. Breathing down is the life threat. Vomit plus poor breathing is an airway problem. Alcohol makes other drugs stronger (potentiates). A sleeping pill plus alcohol can knock them out hard. You cannot reverse it. ABCs and transport.

Delirium tremens (DTs): a heavy long-time drinker who stops suddenly. The body was used to alcohol being there. Agitation, restlessness, fever, sweating, shakes, confusion, delusions, hallucinations, seizures. Treat the symptoms. ABCs first. Treat seizures like any other seizure: protect them, suction if needed, breathe for them if needed. Most seizures last under 5 minutes. Long ones need paramedics or the emergency room.

Opiate means from the poppy. Opioid means made in a lab. Same receptors. Same look. Heroin, fentanyl, morphine, Vicodin, and oxycodone all look the same. Breathing is slow and shallow and can stop. That is what kills them. Heart and blood pressure go down. Mental status goes down. They can go unresponsive. Pupils are pinpoint (miosis). Classic clue, not a guarantee. Gut: nausea and vomiting. Reversing it can also make them vomit.

Narcan (naloxone) only works on opioids. It does nothing for alcohol, benzodiazepines, or barbiturates. There is no single test dose. 2 mg and 4 mg can both be right. Timing matters more than which number. Intranasal (IN) is preferred. No needle. Safer around IV drug users. It works even if they are not breathing. It is not an inhaler. It soaks through the nose into the blood. Very safe. If they are out and it might be opioids, try it. If 1–2 doses do nothing, it is probably not opioids. Cops often give it before you arrive. Write that down. When they wake up they may fight or vomit. That is the high wearing off, not a drug side effect.

Oxygen before Narcan. ABCs always first. No oxygen is what kills them right now. These patients are usually in respiratory failure → BVM, then spray Narcan between breaths. Do not stand there waiting for Narcan while they get no air. If you expect them to wake up fast, a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) may be smarter than an oropharyngeal airway (OPA). OPA plus gag reflex back can mean vomit. You can still spray Narcan in the other nostril if an NPA is in one side.

Barbiturates have “barb” in the name (phenobarbital, pentobarbital). Benzodiazepines end in -pam or -lam (diazepam, lorazepam, midazolam). Valium, Xanax, and Ativan are benzos. Both are CNS depressants. Everything down. Looks a lot like alcohol and opioids. Benzos in public are anxiety pills. In EMS and the hospital they are drugs that stop seizures. Pinpoint pupils are less classic than with opioids. Breathing down is the immediate life threat. Suction. BVM if needed. Narcan will do nothing. Different receptors. If Narcan twice does nothing, think “not an opioid,” or mixed drugs.

Practice

Do this: name stimulant up versus depressant down. You did it right if breathing down was the depressant life threat.

Do this: list the four routes and mark inject as the fastest life threat. You did it right if swallow was about 80%.

Do this: call Poison Control, then hang up for an out-of-scope order and call medical control. You did it right if Poison Control stayed the poison experts, not medical control.

Do this: bag first, then Narcan between breaths. You did it right if you did not wait with empty lungs.

Do this: refuse Narcan on a barbiturate or benzodiazepine name. You did it right if “barb” and -pam/-lam stayed depressants with no Narcan.

Do this: name DTs as a heavy drinker who stops cold turkey. You did it right if ABCs and seizure care came before any reverse attempt.

Do this: say charcoal is an adsorbent at 1 g/kg, awake with an airway, not Arizona EMT scope. You did it right if ipecac and forced vomit were refused.
