Trauma overview (Ch 23–25) — key

Index of suspicion = how worried you are about unseen injuries inside. Those inside problems need surgery. There is no operating room in the ambulance. Do not waste time wrapping every broken bone while they bleed inside or the brain dies. Biggest driver of survival: get them to a trauma center fast. Platinum 10 minutes = from arrival on scene to wheels rolling.

Chapter 23

Behavioral health is a light review here. Scene safety first. Always. Do not start with “this is just psych.” Do a full medical check. It could be low oxygen, low blood sugar, or a head injury. Ask people who know them: how is this compared with their normal? Nobody who knows them is there → treat it as new and bad. Then ABCs and transport. Restraints were already taught.

Chapter 25

Mechanism of injury (MOI) is what physically happened. Nature of illness (NOI) is a medical problem. Most common trauma calls: car crashes and falls — not shootings and stabbings. Bruising often does not show yet on scene. Do not wait for bruises. Pain plus a real MOI is enough. Fast heart rate or fast breathing means the body is reacting. Go.

Kinetic energy idea: ½ mass × speed². A little more speed matters more than a little more weight (except a huge mass change). Main trauma types: blunt, penetrating, and burns. Barotrauma is a small separate group.

Three collisions in a car crash: (1) vehicle hits and stops; (2) body hits the inside; (3) organs keep going and smash inside. Collision 3 is the life threat — aorta shear, kidney / renal vessels, gut vessels. You cannot fix that in the field. Surgery. Tell the hospital: how smashed, where they sat, intrusion WHERE. Passenger-compartment intrusion is worse than trunk or engine crush zones. Bent steering wheel → chest. Damaged lower dash → knee. Starred windshield → head. Indirect: knee hits dash → force up the leg → hip can shatter. Coup-contrecoup = brain ping-pongs inside the skull. Significant crash: death in the car, severe intrusion into where people sit, severe rear damage, rollover / spin, ejected. Also ask: seatbelt?

Motorcycle thrown forward: classic bilateral femur breaks. Look at gear and helmet. Car vs person: adult more likely over the car; child more likely under (bumper heights).

Falls: grade by height (20 feet magic number; or more than 2–3× the person’s height for kids), surface, and what hit first. Older bones break easier even at standing height.

Penetrating energy: low (stab), medium (handgun), high (rifle). Knife: assume the worst. Bullet path inside is unknown; cavitation hurts nearby tissue. Leave impaled objects in unless they block CPR with no pulse, block the airway, or stick you immovable. Impaled in the back with no pulse must come out so you can lay flat for CPR. Fire can cut long rebar shorter; the object stays in; stabilize it.

Blast: primary = pressure wave / hollow spaces / lungs (eardrums pop easy but are not the killer; air bubbles in blood act like clots). Secondary = shrapnel. Tertiary = thrown. Quaternary = everything else. You cannot fix air bubbles in the field.

Order on scene: gloves → scene safety → what happened → how many → extra help → think spine → XABCs → board → trauma center. X = huge outside bleed BEFORE airway. You do not carry blood. Platinum 10 is for a real trauma concern. Helicopter landing zone: minimum 60×60 feet; better 100×100. More than one patient: do not leave the one you already have. Mass-casualty triage rules come later.
