Respiratory and infection overview

Key notes. Saturday 2:46. 12-question recap.

Fever or hot skin: think infection. Then ask if it is septic shock: infection plus low blood pressure. They may have a fast pulse (tachycardia), fast breathing (tachypnea), altered mental status, and poor skin perfusion. You do not carry antibiotics. High-flow oxygen, lie them flat if they can, keep warm, rapid transport. Gloves, mask, eye protection. N95 if the risk is high.

Dyspnea is difficulty breathing. Apnea is not breathing. Tachypnea is fast breathing. Hypoxia is low oxygen at the tissues.

Upper airway: nose, mouth, pharynx, larynx. Lower: trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli. Residual volume is the air left after a max exhale. It keeps alveoli open. Atelectasis is collapsed alveoli.

Stridor, snoring, and gurgling are upper sounds you can hear without a stethoscope. Stridor is high-pitched on inspiration and means upper swelling (croup, epiglottitis, anaphylaxis, inhalation burns). Snoring is the tongue. Gurgling is fluid.

Wheezes, rhonchi, and crackles need a stethoscope. Wheeze is musical on exhalation: lower constriction (asthma, bronchiolitis, RSV, bronchitis). Rhonchi are low rattles: mucus in larger airways, often pneumonia. Crackles are pops: fluid in alveoli (pneumonia, pulmonary edema). Localized abnormal sound, especially one lobe: think pneumonia. Diffuse bilateral wheezing: think asthma, bronchiolitis, or RSV.

Croup: usually under 6, viral, low-grade fever, slow onset, seal-bark cough, stridor. Kids have a narrow cone upper airway, so a little swelling is a big problem. Humidified oxygen if you have it. Otherwise still give oxygen. Keep them calm. Position of comfort.

Epiglottitis: bacterial (Haemophilus influenzae type B). High fever, rapid onset, stridor, tripod, drooling, sniffing position. The epiglottis can swell shut. Do not agitate. Do not put an oropharyngeal airway (OPA) in or suction the back of the throat. Humidified oxygen if you have it. Upright. Rapid transport. Be ready to bag if they fail.

RSV / bronchiolitis: infants and young kids, very contagious. Fever, cough, mucus, wheezing. Suction the nose (they are nose-breathers). Oxygen as needed.

Pneumonia: viral or bacterial. Fever, productive cough (green, yellow, or rust sputum), localized abnormal sounds. Aspiration can be both sides. Watch for septic shock.

Pertussis: coughing spells then a whoop on inspiration. Oxygen and suction as needed.

Flu: fever, cough, muscle aches. No unique field clue. Mask the patient. N95 on you if high risk.

Tuberculosis: fever, night sweats, coughing blood (hemoptysis). N95/HEPA on you. Surgical mask on them.

Most dyspnea: sit them up. Shock with no breathing problem: supine. You do not treat the bug. Support, protect yourself, spot severity, go.

Practice

  • Do this: name stridor vs wheeze. You did it right if stridor is upper without a stethoscope and wheeze is lower with one.
  • Do this: split croup from epiglottitis on one kid drill. You did it right if seal-bark plus low slow fever is croup, and drooling plus high rapid fever is epiglottitis.
  • Do this: refuse an OPA on an epiglottitis drill. You did it right if you kept them calm and left the throat alone.
  • Do this: suction an infant RSV nose. You did it right if the bulb went to the nose.
  • Do this: pick localized rhonchi plus colored sputum as pneumonia. You did it right if you did not call it diffuse asthma.
  • Do this: say the TB triad and your mask. You did it right if fever, night sweats, hemoptysis, and N95 on you.
  • Do this: call septic shock from infection plus low pressure. You did it right if you started high-flow oxygen, warmth, and a rapid transport.
  • Do this: give dry oxygen when you have no humidifier. You did it right if you still gave oxygen.
