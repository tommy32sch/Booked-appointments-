# Respiratory emergencies overview — key

Pulmonary edema is fluid in the alveoli. Congestive heart failure (CHF) is the usual cause. Crackles (wet, bubbling; some say rales). They may have paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND): they get short of breath lying down at night, so they sleep upright. Keep them sitting up.

If they are working hard but still breathing adequately, start a non-rebreather. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) pushes air in and they exhale against pressure, which shoves fluid from the alveoli back into the blood. Do not use CPAP if blood pressure is low or they are in shock, unconscious, nauseated, or have significant chest trauma. Watch pressure and mental status the whole time.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is chronic bronchitis plus emphysema. Many live with high carbon dioxide and breathe on hypoxic drive. Short transports: still give oxygen. Long transports: lower flow or medical direction, and aim for their usual saturation if you know it.

Chronic bronchitis (blue bloater): mucus, wheeze, low oxygen, right-sided heart failure with jugular venous distention (JVD) and pedal edema. Emphysema (pink puffer): lost elasticity, barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing.

Asthma: lower-airway constriction and mucus, wheeze mostly on exhalation. Albuterol is a beta-2 agonist. It also hits beta-1, so heart rate and shakiness go up. That is not shock. Status asthmaticus does not respond well. Get advanced life support (ALS).

Mild allergy is one system (often hives). Monitor and transport. No epinephrine. Anaphylaxis is two or more systems. Epinephrine intramuscular, oxygen first then move fast to epi. Scrape a bee stinger with a straight edge. Do not use tweezers. The sac can keep injecting for about 20 minutes.

Spontaneous pneumothorax: air leak without trauma, often a tall thin male smoker, sudden sharp pleuritic pain, maybe decreased sounds on that side. Oxygen, upright, watch for tension.

Pleural effusion is fluid around the lung, not in the alveoli, so no crackles. Decreased sounds over the fluid. Oxygen, sit up, go.

Pulmonary embolism (PE): clot from the legs to the lung. Sudden dyspnea, often normal sounds, saturation stays low on a non-rebreather because air gets in but blood does not. Rapid transport.

Panic hyperventilation: they blow off too much carbon dioxide. Tingling, carpopedal spasms. Coach slow breathing. Do not use a paper bag. Compensatory hyperventilation (diabetic ketoacidosis) is helping. Do not just slow those breaths.

Carbon monoxide: colorless. Pulse oximetry lies. High-flow oxygen. Get them out.

PASTE: progression, associated chest pain, sputum, talking tiredness, exercise tolerance. Pink frothy sputum is pulmonary edema.

## Practice
Do this: sit a CHF/PND patient up. You did it right if you never forced them flat.

Do this: list four CPAP nos. You did it right if low pressure, unconscious, vomiting, and chest trauma were on the list.

Do this: give oxygen to a hypoxic COPD patient on a short run. You did it right if you did not withhold it for hypoxic drive.

Do this: name albuterol’s shake and fast pulse as a drug effect. You did it right if you did not call it shock.

Do this: hold epi on hives-only. You did it right if you said one system, monitor and go.

Do this: scrape a stinger with a straight edge. You did it right if you never pinched it with tweezers.

Do this: pick PE when saturation stays low on a non-rebreather with normal sounds. You did it right if you said the clot blocks blood, not air.

Do this: say the pulse ox can lie in carbon monoxide. You did it right if you still put them on high-flow oxygen.
