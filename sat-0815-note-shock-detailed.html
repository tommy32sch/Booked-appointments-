Understanding types of shock

Detailed notes. Sat Aug 29 8:15.

This talk is the longer shock class after the short perfusion clip. Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion: cells short on oxygen and nutrients, not clearing waste.

Stages

Compensated: fight-or-flight is still winning. Epinephrine raises heart rate and squeeze (beta-1), clamps peripheral vessels (alpha), and opens airways (beta-2). Blood pressure can still be normal. You still see a fast pulse, fast breathing, and cool pale clammy skin from that clamp and shunt.

Decompensated: those moves fail. Systolic pressure tanks. Vital organs lose flow. Arrest risk is high.

Adult minimum systolic for perfusion is 90 mmHg. Nitro thinking uses 100, but the general floor here is 90. Pediatric minimum systolic is 2 × age in years + 70. A 4-year-old is 78. Under that is hypotension / decompensated shock for that child.

Pulse pressure is systolic minus diastolic. In many shock states systolic falls faster than diastolic, so a narrowing pulse pressure (under about 25) can mark worsening shock.

Baroreceptors in the aorta and carotids sample pressure every beat. A drop fires the brain, then epinephrine from the adrenals.

The body shunts: it closes precapillary and post-capillary sphincters in less important beds. Skin goes cool and pale. Gut digestion turns off. Blood goes to brain, heart, lungs, and big skeletal muscle. That clamp is why early shock can still show a normal pressure.

Poor perfusion means anaerobic metabolism: less ATP, more lactic acid, local acidosis. Tissue turns toxic and will die if you do not fix flow.

Classic pattern

Blood pressure trending down (may still be normal early). Heart rate up. Breathing rate up. Cool, pale, clammy skin. Dropping pressure, rising heart rate and breathing, cool pale clammy = shock until proven otherwise. Then add one or two extras to name the type.

Universal EMT care

High-flow oxygen 10–15 L/min. Non-rebreather if rate and tidal volume are adequate. Bag-valve mask with oxygen if breathing is inadequate. Pick the device only from rate, rhythm, and quality.

Supine if they tolerate it, to help venous return. Keep warm with blankets even in a warm room: shock cuts cell metabolism and they get internally cold. Rapid transport. Recognize early, do the basics, load and go.

Nitro and CPAP both drop pressure. If they are already hypotensive or in shock, those are contraindicated.

Cardiogenic (pump problem)

The heart muscle is weak or damaged. Cardiac output is down even if volume and vessels are okay. Most common cause: a big myocardial infarction now or in the past. Left ventricle fails, blood backs LV to LA to pulmonary veins to lung capillaries. Fluid into alveoli: crackles (rales), maybe pink frothy sputum.

Shock plus crushing/pressure chest pain, maybe a heart history, plus crackles. High-flow oxygen, supine if tolerated, keep warm, rapid transport. Aspirin if you suspect active MI, they can chew, no allergy; blood pressure does not matter for aspirin. No nitro if they are in shock. CPAP can help pulmonary edema if pressure is still adequate; stop or do not start if systolic falls below protocol (often about 90).

Obstructive (pump blocked from outside)

The heart is okay. Something outside stops filling or pumping.

Cardiac tamponade: blood or fluid in the pericardial sac squeezes the heart so the right atrium cannot fill. Often chest trauma. Beck’s triad: narrowing pulse pressure, JVD, muffled heart tones. Trauma + shock + JVD + equal breath sounds. You cannot drain the sac. Oxygen, supine, warm, rapid transport, early hospital alert.

Tension pneumothorax: a hole leaks air into the pleural space. Pressure collapses that lung and shoves the heart and great veins. Trauma (penetrating or rib puncture) is typical. Shock plus absent breath sounds on one side, often JVD, late tracheal deviation away. Tamponade and tension can share trauma, shock, and JVD. Lungs split them: equal and clear → tamponade; one side gone → tension. EMT: oxygen, warm, rapid transport. Paramedic may needle decompress.

Pulmonary embolism: a traveling clot (often a DVT) lodges in pulmonary arteries. Air can sit in alveoli with no blood under it, so no gas exchange. A big saddle PE can block both lungs and arrest them fast. Medical, sudden severe dyspnea, sharp localized chest pain, hypoxia that does not improve with high-flow oxygen. EMT: oxygen, position of comfort (often semi-Fowler if they tolerate it), rapid transport. Hospital may use clot-busters, which can bleed elsewhere.

Practice

  • Do this: write compensated vs decompensated without looking. You did it right if compensated still holds systolic with a fast pulse and cool pale clammy skin.
  • Do this: run 2 × age + 70 for a 6-year-old. You did it right if you got 82.
  • Do this: compute pulse pressure from 110/90. You did it right if you said 20 and called that narrow.
  • Do this: auscultate both sides on a classmate and say tamponade vs tension if they were in shock with JVD. You did it right if equal sounds is tamponade and a silent side is tension.
  • Do this: list Beck’s triad on paper. You did it right if narrowing pulse pressure, JVD, and muffled tones are all there.
  • Do this: pick NRB vs BVM from two made-up breathing lines. You did it right if the device followed rate and tidal volume only.
  • Do this: cover them and say the heat reason. You did it right if you said less cell metabolism, internal hypothermia.
  • Do this: say nitro/CPAP in a hypotensive shock patient. You did it right if you held both.
  • Do this: name cardiogenic from chest pain plus crackles plus the classic shock pattern. You did it right if you said cardiogenic / big MI.
  • Do this: name PE from sudden dyspnea plus a sat that will not rise on oxygen. You did it right if you said pulmonary embolism.
