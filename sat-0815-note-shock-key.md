# Understanding types of shock — key

Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion. Cells do not get enough oxygen and nutrients and they do not clear waste.

Compensated shock: the body is still winning. Epinephrine holds blood pressure. Heart rate and breathing go up. Skin is cool, pale, and clammy. Blood pressure can still look okay.

Decompensated shock: that hold fails. Systolic blood pressure drops. Vital organs lose perfusion. Arrest risk is high.

Adult floor for systolic perfusion: ≥ 90 mmHg. (Nitro thinking uses ≥ 100.) Pedi minimum systolic: 2 × age in years + 70. A 4-year-old is 78.

Pulse pressure = systolic minus diastolic. Narrowing pulse pressure (under about 25) can mean shock is getting worse.

Classic shock pattern: blood pressure trending down, heart rate up, breathing rate up, cool pale clammy skin. Then add one or two extra findings to name the type.

## Universal care
High-flow oxygen 10–15 L/min. Non-rebreather if rate and tidal volume are adequate. Bag-valve mask with oxygen if breathing is inadequate. Device choice is from respiratory rate, rhythm, and quality.

Lie them flat (supine) if they can, to help venous return. Keep them warm (blankets). Rapid transport. Load and go after ABCs.

Do not give nitro or CPAP if they are already hypotensive / in shock. Both drop blood pressure more.

## Types
Cardiogenic: the pump is weak. Most common cause is a big myocardial infarction (MI). Left ventricle fails, blood backs into the lungs: crackles, maybe pink frothy sputum. Pattern: shock plus chest pain and crackles. Aspirin if you suspect active MI and they can chew (blood pressure does not matter for aspirin). No nitro if hypotensive. CPAP for pulmonary edema only if pressure is still adequate (stop if systolic falls, often under 90).

Obstructive: the heart is okay but something outside blocks filling or pumping. Three from this recap: cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive pulmonary embolism.

Cardiac tamponade: fluid in the pericardial sac squeezes the heart. Beck’s triad: narrowing pulse pressure, jugular vein distension (JVD), muffled heart tones. Chest trauma + shock + JVD + equal clear breath sounds. You cannot drain the sac. Oxygen, supine, warm, rapid transport, early alert.

Tension pneumothorax: air trapped in the pleural space collapses the lung and pushes the heart. Shock + JVD + absent breath sounds on one side ± tracheal deviation away (late). Same trauma + shock + JVD as tamponade; lungs tell them apart. EMT: oxygen, warm, rapid transport. Paramedic may needle decompress.

Pulmonary embolism: a clot (often from a DVT) blocks a pulmonary artery. Sudden severe dyspnea, sharp localized chest pain, hypoxia that does not improve with oxygen. Medical, not trauma. EMT: oxygen, position of comfort, rapid transport.

## Practice
Do this: say compensated vs decompensated in one line each. You did it right if compensated still holds pressure with a fast pulse and cool pale clammy skin, and decompensated has a falling systolic.

Do this: compute a 4-year-old’s minimum systolic. You did it right if you got 78 (2 × 4 + 70).

Do this: name the classic four-part shock pattern. You did it right if you said pressure down, heart rate up, breathing up, cool pale clammy.

Do this: pick NRB vs BVM from a breathing description. You did it right if adequate rate and volume get a non-rebreather at 10–15 L, and inadequate breathing gets a bag with oxygen.

Do this: list Beck’s triad. You did it right if you said narrowing pulse pressure, JVD, and muffled heart tones.

Do this: contrast tamponade vs tension by breath sounds. You did it right if equal/clear is tamponade and one-sided absent is tension.

Do this: name PE from sudden dyspnea plus oxygen that does not bring the saturation up. You did it right if you said pulmonary embolism / clot in the lung.

Do this: wrap a blanket and say why. You did it right if you said shock cuts cell metabolism so they get internally cold.

Do this: say when nitro and CPAP are out. You did it right if you said already hypotensive / in shock.
