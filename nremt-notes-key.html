Patient assessment overview — key

Roadmap for every patient: walk-in to handoff.

Flow NREMT

Scene size-up

Primary — find and treat life threats

History — OPQRST and SAMPLE

Secondary — physical exam + full vitals

Field impression and interventions

Reassessment

Scene (before you touch them) NREMT

  • BSI / PPE
  • Scene safe for you, partner, patient, bystanders
  • MOI vs NOI
  • Number of patients
  • Extra resources (fire, police, ALS, more units)
  • Call law early if you suspect abuse/violence or the scene is escalating

Primary (every patient) NREMT

Goal: ABCs + transport priority.

  • General impression: sick vs not, bleeding, distress, trauma vs medical, age, position
  • AVPU. If Alert, check person, place, time, event
  • Alert and oriented ×4 → expressed consent. Confused / V / P / U → implied consent
  • Airway open and clear. No stethoscope: snoring = tongue; gurgling = fluid → suction; stridor = upper swelling
  • Breathing: adult 12–20. Chest rise, work of breathing. Lower sounds with a stethoscope: wheeze = bronchoconstriction (often exhalation); crackles = alveolar fluid; rhonchi = mucus/congestion
  • Circulation: adult pulse 60–100; skin CTC; cap refill especially kids; stop major bleeding as soon as you see it
  • Rapid scan if critical trauma
  • Load-and-go vs more time on scene

History (ABCs handled, they can talk) NREMT

OPQRST: onset, provocation/palliation, quality, region/radiation, severity 0–10, time.

SAMPLE: signs/symptoms, allergies, medications, past history, last oral intake, events.

Alcohol/drugs: calm, not judging. You may re-ask once, then document “Patient denies alcohol or drug use.” Most EMT treatments do not change on undisclosed use. Reasonable decisions with the facts you had.

Abuse: mandatory report on suspicion. Not defamation to report suspicion. Defamation is stating guilt as fact. Write what you see and what they said.

Pregnancy: any childbearing-age female with lower abdominal pain — “Any chance you’re pregnant?” and last menstrual period. Ask bowel/bladder and N/V/D with belly complaints. STI/sexual history only if it matters clinically.

Chart: objective, verifiable, true. Quotes, not guilt.

Secondary NREMT

  • Focused exam: responsive medical who can point at the problem
  • Detailed head-to-toe: unresponsive, significant MOI, or you suspect hidden/multiple injuries
  • Look, feel, listen. DCAP-BTLS on each region. Compare sides.

DCAP-BTLS: deformities, contusions, abrasions, punctures/penetrations, burns, tenderness, lacerations, swelling.

Numbers NREMT

  • Adult RR 12–20; kids faster
  • Adult pulse 60–100; kids higher
  • Adult systolic 90–120. <90 hypotension/shock. 120–130 hypertension range. Systolic is your emergency focus
  • Ped systolic min (<10 yr) = 2 × age + 70. Age 10 = 90, then adult cutoff
  • GCS: E 1–4, V 1–5, M 1–6. Total 3–15. 15 alert. 3 nothing
  • PERRL. Pinpoint often opioids. Dilated often stimulants or brain injury. Note unequal, blown, sluggish
  • SpO₂ 94–99%. CO can read falsely high. It does not count how many red cells you have
  • Glucose 80–120. <80 low. Check altered, known diabetic, stroke vs drunk vs sugar

Impression, treat, reassess NREMT

Field impression is what you think is going on, not a hospital diagnosis.

ABCs and life threats before medications. Inadequate breathing → ventilate before the drug.

Reassess mental status/GCS, ABCs, vitals, response to treatment. Unstable ~5 min. Stable ~15 min.

Handoff: scene + complaint, primary/life threats, OPQRST/SAMPLE hits, exam + vitals/trends, GCS/pupils, treatments and effect.

Practice

  • Do this: Recite the six-step flow without looking. You did it right if you said scene, primary, history, secondary, impression/interventions, reassessment — in that order.
  • Do this: Name the five scene tasks plus when you call law. You did it right if you have BSI, safety, MOI/NOI, number, resources, and law for abuse or an escalating scene.
  • Do this: Pair upper-airway sounds you hear without a stethoscope. You did it right if snoring is tongue, gurgling is fluid and suction, stridor is swelling.
  • Do this: Give adult RR, pulse, systolic band, and the peds hypotension formula. You did it right if 12–20, 60–100, 90–120, and 2×age+70 under age 10.
  • Do this: Score a fully alert patient and a no-response patient on GCS. You did it right if 15 (4+5+6) and 3.
  • Do this: Pick focused vs detailed for a talking belly-pain patient vs an ejection. You did it right if focused for the first and detailed for the second.
  • Do this: Write one chart line for a bruise and one for a quote. You did it right if the bruise is what you see and the quote is in their words, not “he is an abuser.”
  • Do this: Choose ventilate vs give a drug when breathing is inadequate. You did it right if you ventilate first.

Reassessment — key

Reassessment is the fifth part of patient assessment. Transport is not the end of the job.

How often NREMT

  • Unstable / critical: every 5 minutes. By the time you finish, it is almost time to start again
  • Stable: every 15 minutes

What you recheck NREMT

  • Airway still open and protected
  • Breathing: rate, depth, quality, work of breathing
  • Circulation: pulse, skin, major bleeding
  • Mental status: LOC, orientation, how they interact
  • Full vital sets, not one number

One set is a snapshot. Two or more sets show trends. BP dropping with HR and RR rising can mean they are sliding toward shock. Flat numbers can mean they are at least not clearly worse or better.

Big change = start over NREMT

Any significant change (better or worse) resets you to primary: mental status → airway → breathing → circulation.

They were talking and go unresponsive: restart that order now.

You get a pulse back during CPR: stop compressions, then reassess MS, airway, breathing, circulation. Do not assume ROSC means they are breathing well. You may still need to ventilate.

If you did it, you re-check it NREMT

Ask: is this still working, and is it still safe?

  • Tourniquet: bleeding still controlled, still in place, not too loose or too tight
  • Splint: swelling can make a good splint too tight. Distal PMS, cap refill, wiggle fingers/toes, new numbness/tingling/severe pain
  • Chest pain meds (nitro, aspirin where appropriate): “Your pain was 8/10; what is it now?” 8 → 5 is progress
  • Low sugar: recheck glucose after oral glucose or other treatment
  • Low oxygen: recheck SpO₂, respiratory status, work of breathing after O₂
  • Restraints: still secure, not cutting circulation. Distal CSM, cap refill, warmth, color, they can feel and move fingers and toes

Meds on the exam vs the street NREMT

On National Registry-style questions, assume meds are online orders unless the stem already gives you orders or a protocol that authorizes the drug. Safe default: contact medical control for permission before you give it.

If the stem says you have orders or protocol authorization, do not pick “call medical control first.”

Street world is often standing (offline) orders. Test world is online unless told otherwise.

Sequence is the answer NREMT

Right pieces in the wrong order is a wrong answer.

Default test order: contact medical control → administer → reassess.

Do not pick “give the drug, then call, then reassess.”

Why this exists NREMT

You are building a field impression and picking treatments. Reassessment, ABCs, and vital trends answer: what is going on, and what are you doing about it?

Practice

  • Do this: Say the two clocks out loud. You did it right if unstable is 5 minutes and stable is 15.
  • Do this: List what one full reassess pass includes. You did it right if you have mental status, ABCs, a new full vital set, and every intervention you already did.
  • Do this: Read two BP/HR/RR sets that are sliding. You did it right if you called the second set a trend toward shock, not a snapshot.
  • Do this: Act ROSC after CPR. You did it right if you stopped compressions and rechecked mental status, airway, breathing, and circulation — and you said they may still need ventilations.
  • Do this: Recheck a splint after swelling. You did it right if you named distal pulse, motor, sensation, cap refill, and new numbness or severe pain.
  • Do this: Ask a chest-pain pain score after nitro. You did it right if you compared now to the first number (8 to 5 is improvement).
  • Do this: Pick the exam med sequence when the stem does not give orders. You did it right if you contact medical control, then give the drug, then reassess.
  • Do this: Cross out an option that has the right three steps in the wrong order. You did it right if you treated that option as wrong.

Respiratory physiology and assessment — key

After scene safety, massive bleed, and they are alive, airway is next. See a life threat, treat a life threat — that includes a catastrophic airway.

Three words (this order) NREMT

Ventilation: air moving in and out. Ask “is air moving?” Rate, rhythm, quality, chest rise.

Respiration: gas exchange (O₂ / CO₂). Alveoli ↔ lung capillaries, later blood ↔ cells.

Oxygenation: O₂ on hemoglobin. You estimate it with pulse ox.

Looks sick but ventilation is fine: think respiration first (fluid, pneumonia, COPD), then oxygenation (sat, hemoglobin), then circulation (shock, heart).

Upper vs lower NREMT

Upper (above the larynx): nose, nasopharynx, oropharynx, epiglottis, larynx / cords. Warm, filter, humidify. Failures: tongue, swelling (allergy/infection), trauma, blood/vomit.

Lower (below the larynx): trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Job is gas exchange.

Phrenic / C3–4–5 NREMT

“C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive.” Injury above C3–5: diaphragm out, they cannot breathe. Injury below C5: may be paralyzed but can still breathe if the phrenic nerve is intact.

Pressure NREMT

Normal inhale is active: diaphragm and intercostals contract → cavity bigger → negative pressure inside the chest → air is pulled in. Exhale is passive: muscles relax, cavity shrinks, air goes out.

When you bag: you push air in (positive pressure). Risks: air in the stomach and vomit; over-squeeze can pop a lung.

Volumes (EMT level) NREMT

Tidal volume = one normal breath. You judge it by chest rise, not a number.

Inspiratory / expiratory reserve = extra you could force in or out.

Residual volume = air left in the alveoli after a full exhale. Keeps them open so they do not collapse between breaths.

Drive to breathe NREMT

Primary: high CO₂ (central chemoreceptors → brainstem → phrenic → diaphragm).

Backup: low O₂ (hypoxic drive). Matters later in severe COPD, when CO₂ is always high and stops being a useful signal.

Adequate vs inadequate (adult) NREMT

Adequate: 12–20/min, regular, good chest rise, clear equal sounds.

Can still be adequate if the rate is a little high (example 22) but volume is good, sounds are clear, they are alert.

Inadequate: under 12 or over 20 with poor volume or irregular rhythm; little or no rise; severe retractions / accessory muscles; agonal, apnea, or chaotic; cyanosis, poor LOC, poor sat.

Patterns NREMT

Agonal: rare, gasping, spastic — arrest. Not breathing. CPR + ventilations.

Cheyne–Stokes: shallow/slow → deeper/faster → slow → pause → repeats. Often brain injury. Bad.

Biot’s / ataxic: no pattern at all. Severe brainstem.

Kussmaul: deep and rapid. Metabolic acidosis (DKA, aspirin OD). The body is compensating — do not slam it down by over-bagging.

Chest NREMT

Pneumothorax: air in the chest but not inside the lung (collapsed lung). Trauma, rib break, puncture, or over-aggressive bag.

Hemothorax: blood in the chest.

Hemopneumothorax: both.

One-sided badness: the other lung may be doing all the work.

Pulse ox NREMT

% of hemoglobin that is saturated. Target 94–99%.

Give O₂ for sat under 94, difficulty breathing, or shock.

Lies or misleads: anemia, low volume, cold hands, nail polish, bright light. Carbon monoxide: sat can look 100% while the seats are full of CO, not O₂.

Capnography (concept) NREMT

Good end-tidal CO₂ means ventilation, gas exchange, circulation, and cells making CO₂ are all working.

Shark-fin waveform: air is hard to get out — asthma / COPD.

Primary check NREMT

Look for massive bleed, severe distress, cyanosis, altered LOC, face/neck/chest trauma.

Talking clearly → airway is likely open. Snoring, gurgling, stridor, silence, blood/vomit/foreign body → fix it.

Trauma worry → jaw thrust. No trauma → head-tilt chin-lift. Suction if gurgling/fluids. OPA/NPA when they fit.

Count rate 30 sec × 2 (adult 12–20). Rhythm and special patterns. Rise, shallow vs normal, accessory muscles, retractions, tripod, nasal flare, pursed lips.

Listen if you can: equal and clear vs diminished, crackles, wheeze, absent.

Skin: color, temp, wet vs dry. Pulse ox if it does not delay a killer.

Oxygen vs bag NREMT

Not breathing or only agonal: extra help / AED. If no pulse, CPR. Bag with oxygen.

Breathing but inadequate: assist with BVM + O₂. Open + adjunct. Seal, right mask. Slow gentle squeezes — just visible rise. Over-squeeze → pneumothorax, stomach air, vomit. Rate per protocol (often about 10–12/min for an adult with a pulse). Recheck rise, skin, mind, sat.

Breathing adequately but sick or hypoxic (dyspnea, sat <94%, shock, chest pain, distress): keep the airway, give supplemental O₂ (device per protocol), watch rate/rhythm/quality, sat trend, mental status.

Red flags (move / ALS) NREMT

Suspected pneumo or hemo (short of breath + one-sided silent lung ± trauma). Severe distress you cannot fix with basics. Head/neck trauma with a weird pattern. Kussmaul plus DKA or poisoning signs. Anyone you must keep bagging.

Difficulty-breathing pass NREMT

Primary first. Then onset (sudden vs slow), what makes it better/worse (flat, work), tight vs burning vs cannot get air in vs out, associated (pain, cough, fever, wheeze, swelling, trauma), past (asthma, COPD, CHF, recent illness, diabetes), meds (inhalers, home O₂, diuretics, insulin, blood thinners).

Look: tripod, full sentences?, chest symmetry/trauma, sounds, edema, JVD, cyanosis. Treat O₂ or BVM. Help with their prescribed inhaler if protocol allows.

Practice

  • Do this: Name the order after safety. You did it right if massive bleed and “are they alive” come before airway, and a catastrophic airway is still “see it, treat it.”
  • Do this: Say ventilation vs respiration vs oxygenation in one breath each. You did it right if ventilation is air moving, respiration is gas exchange, oxygenation is O₂ on hemoglobin (pulse ox).
  • Do this: They look terrible but chest rise is good. You did it right if you work respiration first (fluid / pneumonia / COPD), then sat/hemoglobin, then shock/heart — not “bag harder.”
  • Do this: Point C3–4–5. You did it right if above that level means the diaphragm is out, and below C5 they may still breathe.
  • Do this: Mime a normal breath, then a bag squeeze. You did it right if inhale is negative (cavity bigger, air pulled in) and a bag is positive (you push; stomach air and popped lung if you over-squeeze).
  • Do this: Judge tidal volume without a number. You did it right if you used visible chest rise, and residual volume is the leftover that keeps alveoli open.
  • Do this: Recite the adult adequate box. You did it right if 12–20, regular, good rise, clear equal sounds — and 22 with good volume and an alert patient can still be adequate.
  • Do this: Name agonal vs Cheyne–Stokes vs Biot’s vs Kussmaul. You did it right if agonal is arrest (CPR + bag), Cheyne–Stokes is the wax-wane-pause (brain), Biot’s is chaos (brainstem), Kussmaul is deep-fast (acidosis — do not slam it).
  • Do this: Read a sat of 100% on a house-fire patient. You did it right if you did not trust it as proof of oxygen — CO can fill the seats.
  • Do this: Decide bag vs mask O₂. You did it right if no breath / agonal / poor volume gets a bag, and a breathing-but-hypoxic or shocked patient gets supplemental O₂ with the airway held.
  • Do this: Bag an adult who still has a pulse. You did it right if the squeeze only makes visible rise, you did not blast the stomach, and the rate is in the local ~10–12 range.

Airway and suction — key

Airway before breathing. If air cannot reach the lungs, you cannot assess or fix breathing.

Patent or not NREMT

Conscious and talking or crying → airway is patent. Say “airway check” and move to breathing.

Unconscious → assume they are not protecting the airway. Muscles relax, the tongue falls back, it can fully obstruct. Open and clear before you judge breathing.

Position NREMT

Patient supine if you can. You at the top of the head.

Conscious people often sit up to protect their own airway.

Trapped and you cannot lay them supine: urgent/extrication move. You may give up ideal spine protection to treat an immediate airway or breathing life threat.

Two openers NREMT

Head-tilt–chin-lift: no spinal concern. One hand on forehead, one under the bony chin. Tilt to pull the tongue off the back of the throat. Easier, more effective, easier to hold. Example: elderly found unconscious in bed; witnessed collapse on a couch with no fall.

Jaw thrust: suspected or possible spine, or you are not sure — err cautious. Fingers behind the angle of the jaw, thumbs on the cheekbones. Lift the jaw forward (underbite) while the head and neck stay in line. Harder to hold; if you let go, the tongue drops and the obstruction returns. Built to protect C-spine while you open the airway.

Four EMT airway steps (this lesson) NREMT

Open (tilt-chin or jaw thrust)

Assess — open the mouth, look for blood/vomit/secretions/foreign material/teeth, listen for gurgling. Do not overcomplicate the mouth open; cross-finger is mostly textbook

Clear — suction only if you see fluid/blood/vomit/secretions or you hear gurgling. If the stem never mentions fluid or gurgling, do not pick suction

Maintain — keep the tongue from coming back (adjuncts, position, more suction, ventilate as needed)

Gear NREMT

  • Battery portable unit: usual on scene
  • Wall unit in the ambulance/hospital
  • Hand-powered backup: tiring; stuff can fall back if you pause

Rigid Yankauer: go-to oral suction. Bigger opening for vomit, blood, chunks.

Flexible French/whistle tip: smaller, clogs easier. Trach tubes or down an ET tube — not a mouth full of vomit.

Start-of-shift check: power/battery/charging, tubing and canister on, vacuum. Book adequate vacuum is greater than 300 mmHg. Gauge, light, or glove-finger test.

Tubing, catheter, canister: single patient, disposable, biohazard trash. Wipe the machine body. Restock and recharge.

Suction steps NREMT

Open the airway first. Supine, you at the head. Mouth open. Look and listen.

If indicated: unit on, confirm suction.

Measure: corner of mouth to earlobe, or corner of mouth to angle of the jaw. That is max depth.

Insert with the vent hole uncovered (no suction on the way in). Only as far as you can see / measured depth. Not down the trachea.

Cover the vent, suction while you withdraw with gentle circles. Do not suction while you advance.

Time: until clear, max 10 seconds. Same limit for adults, children, infants.

Between passes: suction sterile water through the tubing to clear clots.

Safety NREMT

Aspiration (blood/vomit/secretions into the lungs) raises mortality about 30–70% on top of the original problem.

No suction ready: log roll to the side so gravity drains.

Dyspnea: sit them upright. That is often the fastest first move.

Catheter keeps clogging: stop the failed loop. Roll to the side, drain, suction, scoop with the catheter — not your finger.

Cycle if they are still dirty: suction up to 10 seconds, ventilate about 2 minutes, repeat. You may push leftover material into the lungs. No oxygen is 100% mortality. That is worse than a 30–70% extra risk. Least-bad choice: oxygenate after you have suctioned as far as the time limit allows.

Once the airway is clear, say “airway check” and go to breathing: rate, rhythm, quality, oxygen or ventilations.

Practice

  • Do this: Point at a talking patient, then an unconscious one. You did it right if talking is “airway check, go to breathing,” and unconscious is assume not protected, open and clear first.
  • Do this: Pick tilt-chin vs jaw thrust for bed-found medical vs a wreck. You did it right if no-spine medical is tilt-chin and possible spine (or unsure) is jaw thrust.
  • Do this: Name the only two suction triggers. You did it right if you see fluid/blood/vomit/secretions or you hear gurgling — and you refused suction when the stem has neither.
  • Do this: Measure a rigid catheter on your own face. You did it right if it is corner of mouth to earlobe or angle of jaw, and you do not bury it in the trachea.
  • Do this: Mime insert vs withdraw. You did it right if the vent is open on the way in and you cover it and circle only on the way out, 10 seconds max for every age.
  • Do this: Choose portable vs wall vs hand, and rigid vs flexible. You did it right if scene is battery portable, oral vomit is rigid, trach/ET is flexible.
  • Do this: Vomit and no suction in your hand. You did it right if you log-roll to the side for gravity while a partner grabs the unit.
  • Do this: Still dirty after 10 seconds. You did it right if you ventilate about 2 minutes next, because no oxygen is 100% mortality.

OPA, NPA, and oxygen — key

Primary assessment is immediate life threats: airway, need for CPR, major bleeding. Primary plus treat/transport is about two-thirds of the test.

Unconscious, no bystanders, no medical ID, no med list: skip SAMPLE/OPQRST. Go to secondary / physical exam.

Adjuncts (maintain step) NREMT

Goal: keep the tongue from blocking the airway.

OPA: rigid, mouth. NPA: flexible, nose. Both stop just past the base of the tongue so air can still pass if the tongue falls back.

OPA NREMT

Use only if fully unresponsive (AVPU = U): not awake, no verbal, no pain, no gag.

Gag on insert → they still have a gag. Pull it now. Expect vomit.

Do not use if conscious, semi-conscious, or any gag.

When they are truly out and have no gag, OPA first: it holds the tongue off the back of the throat better than NPA.

Size: corner of mouth to earlobe, or corner of mouth to angle of the jaw.

Open the airway first (tilt-chin or jaw thrust). Do not shove it straight in from the front — that rams the tongue down.

Two legal insert methods: upside down then 180° rotate, or sideways then 90° rotate. Pull it out along the curve.

Cardiac arrest / CPR: muscles and tongue are floppy. OPA is fine if unresponsive and no gag.

NPA NREMT

Use when they need a path but still have a gag — semi-conscious or even alert. Also if unresponsive but gagging. Usually does not trigger gag the way an OPA does.

Hard stop: any head or face trauma, especially suspected basilar skull fracture. The tube can go through a broken skull base into the brain. Positive-pressure ventilation can then blow air into the cranial vault.

Head/face trauma: mouth OPA is OK if no gag. Nose NPA is never OK.

Unresponsive: try OPA. If they gag, pull it. NPA only if no head/face trauma.

Gag plus head trauma: no OPA, no NPA. You hold the airway by hand and fight the tongue until ALS can put in a higher airway.

Size: nose to earlobe, or nose to angle of the jaw.

Water-based medical lube. Bevel toward the septum. Gentle, straight in; it follows the curve and ends past the tongue base. Single-use, then trash.

Recovery position NREMT

Unconscious, not injured (no suspected spine), breathing well on their own → roll on the side so the airway stays open and vomit/secretions can drain.

Deeply unconscious in EMS often needs a bag — then they stay supine.

When you give oxygen NREMT

Give O₂ if SpO₂ < 94%, or they look like respiratory distress (dyspnea, hard work of breathing), or they look like shock.

Target for most emergency patients: 94–99%.

Treat the patient, not the number. Distress with a “normal” sat still gets oxygen.

If they might benefit and they look sick, do not withhold.

Caution over time: extra O₂ can tighten vessels and can worsen stroke or MI. Free oxygen with no hemoglobin left to bind it irritates tissue (toxicity). COPD is extra sensitive — more on that later. If they are clearly compromised, still give O₂.

Hemoglobin = stadium seats. Full house → extra O₂ has nowhere to sit and causes trouble.

Tanks and regulators NREMT

D tank: small portable, scene / gurney. H tank: tall (~5 ft) mounted in the ambulance.

~2,000 psi (car tire ~30). Always secured, strapped, laid down, or mounted. Never let it fall. A broken valve turns it into a missile that can go through a wall.

Pin index: unique hole pattern so only the right gas and regulator mate. Oxygen tanks are green/silver. Stops filling a tank with the wrong gas (nitrous, CO₂, helium).

Regulator drops ~2,000 psi to about 40–70 psi. Flow meter sets L/min.

Bourdon gauge: works in any position — portable tanks you lay down. Pressure-compensated (vertical tube): must stay upright — usually wall-mounted.

Non-rebreather NREMT

Preferred high-concentration mask at EMT level.

Breathing adequately (rate and tidal volume OK) but hypoxic (SpO₂ < 94%), cyanotic, or short of breath / distress. Ventilation is fine; they need more O₂ than 21% room air.

Flow: 10–15 L/min (book/test). Commonly 12–15. Test default if they need O₂ and are breathing well: NRB 10–15 unless the stem names another device.

Tank is 100%. They get about 90–95% because the mask leaks room air.

Setup NREMT

Confirm O₂ tank (green/silver). Secure it. Pins match. Open, check gauge (full ≈ 2,000). Set L/min. Fit the mask; leak is why it is not 100%. Watch sat, color, effort, mental status. Titrate toward 94–99%. Worsening: recheck airway, need for BVM / ALS, transport priority.

Practice

  • Do this: Recite when you skip SAMPLE. You did it right if unconscious plus no bystanders / ID / med list means skip history and go to the physical exam.
  • Do this: Say who gets an OPA. You did it right if only AVPU = U with no gag, and gag on insert means pull it now and expect vomit.
  • Do this: Measure an OPA and an NPA on your face. You did it right if OPA is mouth-corner to ear or jaw angle, and NPA is nose to ear or jaw angle.
  • Do this: Mime both OPA inserts. You did it right if you open the airway first, then 180° from upside-down or 90° from the side — never a straight shove that drives the tongue down.
  • Do this: Say the NPA hard stop. You did it right if any head or face trauma (especially basilar skull) is no NPA, even if they need an adjunct.
  • Do this: Gag plus facial trauma — pick your tools. You did it right if you use neither adjunct and you hold the jaw/tongue by hand.
  • Do this: Name three reasons to give O₂. You did it right if sat under 94, distress (even with a “good” number), or shock — target 94–99%.
  • Do this: Set up an NRB on a breathing, hypoxic patient. You did it right if 10–15 L/min and you did not pick a cannula just because they look “pretty okay.”
  • Do this: Point at D vs H, Bourdon vs upright tube, and a loose tank. You did it right if D is portable, H is the big mounted tank, Bourdon works lying down, compensated must stand, and an unsecured tank is a missile at ~2,000 psi.

Oxygen devices and airway — key

Almost every call here is two questions: is the airway open and clear, and are they breathing adequately.

If the airway is not open: tilt-chin or jaw thrust, suction, OPA/NPA, treat choking.

If they are breathing adequately: supplemental O₂ (test default is a non-rebreather). If they are not breathing enough or not at all: bag them. Severe work with fluid in the lungs but still breathing on their own: think CPAP if they meet the rules.

Non-rebreather NREMT

Flow 10–15 L/min. About 90–95% O₂. Flaps let exhaled air out and stop them from rebreathing it.

Conscious or unconscious does not matter. They just need adequate spontaneous breathing.

Start high: too little O₂ kills now; too much is a later problem.

Test world: adequate breathing plus distress, sat under 94%, or shock → NRB 10–15, not a cannula.

Nasal cannula NREMT

1–6 L/min. About 24–44% (1 L ≈ 24%, just above 21% room air; 6 L ≈ 44%).

Real world: mild hypoxia or a small bump. Registry: rarely the right pick.

Over 6 L dries and burns the nose. You usually run 2–4.

Mild sat around 92% and they look comfortable, minor SOB, or a home-O₂ user who does not need high flow.

Other masks NREMT

Partial rebreather = NRB with the side flaps off. Still 10–15 L/min on the face. About 80–90%. Rare. Sometimes mentioned for panic/hyperventilation.

Venturi: color adapters with a printed flow and exact % (24, 28, 32…). Long-term controlled O₂. EMS rarely carries it. You may see it in a facility.

Trach mask over a stoma: treat like a face mask — 10–15 L/min if you are using it as high flow.

Humidified O₂: bubble through sterile water. Comfort for long-term. Portable tanks often do not have it. Need O₂ now → give dry.

BVM NREMT

Apnea, or inadequate rate/rhythm/quality (too slow, too fast, shallow, poor rise).

10–15 L/min. Tight seal → close to 100%. Adult, child, infant bags. Same flow; only the rate changes.

Adult: 1 breath every 6 seconds = 10/min. Child and infant: 1 every 2–3 seconds = 20–30/min.

Gentle ~1-second squeeze until you see chest rise. Count out loud so you do not go too fast. If they still take weak breaths, try to squeeze with their inhale.

Too hard / too fast: air in the stomach → vomit and aspiration. Extra pressure in the chest squeezes the heart and great vessels → blood pressure drops. You can push them toward arrest.

CPAP NREMT

Constant pressure while they breathe. Back-pressure in the alveoli helps shove fluid out of the way (pulmonary edema). Like sticking your head out a car window at 70 mph.

They must be alert, follow commands, and protect the airway. Severe work, usually wet lungs, still breathing on their own. NRB is not enough and they are still fighting.

Do not use if: apneic or hypoventilating (they need a bag); cannot follow commands / unconscious; cannot protect the airway; systolic under 90 (it drops BP more); trauma, especially chest/face or suspected pneumothorax; they cannot tolerate the mask; vomiting, GI bleed, nausea; shock with low BP.

Choking NREMT

Mild: they can cough, speak, move air. Hands off. Encourage cough. You may add NRB so the air they move is richer.

Severe and conscious: no cough, no speech, turning blue, universal sign.

Adult/child (updated AHA): 5 back blows, 5 abdominal thrusts, repeat until it comes out or they go unresponsive.

Infant: 5 back blows (face down on your forearm, head supported), 5 chest thrusts (same spot as infant CPR). Alternate until resolved or unresponsive.

They collapse: supine, start CPR. Compressions move blood and help pop the object. Pulse or not — if they drop from choking, compress.

Other pearls NREMT

Stable dentures stay — they help the face hold a mask. Loose plates come out. Braces stay unless they block the path.

Face bleeds a lot and swells fast. Suction blood often. Swelling can close the airway. No NPA if you suspect facial trauma. No CPAP in trauma. External bleed: direct pressure first.

When you give O₂ NREMT

Adequately breathing plus: sat under 94%, any difficulty or labored breathing (even with a “normal” sat), or shock (cool pale clammy, altered, weak/rapid pulse).

Comfortable, no shock, sat in range → you probably do not need O₂.

Flow you run NREMT

Airway open? Tilt-chin (no trauma) or jaw thrust (possible spine). Look. Suction up to 10 seconds, then re-oxygenate.

OPA if fully out, no gag. NPA if they need a path but gag and have no face/head trauma. Stable dentures stay; loose ones go.

Rate, rhythm, quality. Adult 12–20. Chest rise. SpO₂. Work of breathing.

Adequate + need O₂ → NRB 10–15 (test). Inadequate or apnea → BVM 10–15 at the rates above. Wet, alert, SBP ≥ 90, no trauma/vomit/pneumo → CPAP. Any CPAP no → NRB or BVM.

Reassess airway, breathing, sat, mind, BP. Watch a rising belly and a falling BP.

Numbers NREMT

Room air 21%. NRB 10–15 → ~90–95%. NC 1–6 → ~24–44%. BVM 10–15. Adult bag 1/6 sec = 10/min. Child/infant 1/2–3 sec = 20–30/min. CPAP off if SBP < 90. Give O₂ if sat < 94% or distress or shock.

Practice

  • Do this: Name the two first questions. You did it right if they are “airway open and clear?” and “breathing adequate?”
  • Do this: Pick a device for adequate breathing plus sat 88% or distress. You did it right if you said NRB 10–15, not a cannula, on the test.
  • Do this: Recite NC numbers. You did it right if 1–6 L/min, ~24–44%, and you usually stay 2–4 because 6 burns.
  • Do this: Bag an adult vs a toddler. You did it right if both get 10–15 L/min, adult is 1 every 6 seconds, child/infant is 1 every 2–3 seconds, and the squeeze only makes visible rise.
  • Do this: Say two BVM harms. You did it right if stomach air/vomit and extra chest pressure dropping blood pressure.
  • Do this: Green-light CPAP. You did it right if alert, follows commands, still breathing, wet lungs / hard work, SBP at least 90, no trauma, no vomit.
  • Do this: Kill CPAP. You did it right if apnea, no commands, SBP under 90, trauma/pneumo, or they cannot protect the airway.
  • Do this: Mild vs severe choke. You did it right if talking/coughing is encourage cough (± NRB), and silent/blue adult-child is 5 back / 5 abdominal until out or down.
  • Do this: Infant choke, then they go limp. You did it right if 5 back / 5 chest while awake, then supine CPR when they drop.
  • Do this: Face smash plus blood. You did it right if you suction, skip NPA, skip CPAP, leave stable dentures, pull loose ones.
