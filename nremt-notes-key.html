Patient assessment overview — key

Roadmap for every patient: walk-in to handoff.

Flow NREMT

  • Scene size-up
  • Primary — find and treat life threats
  • History — OPQRST and SAMPLE
  • Secondary — physical exam + full vitals
  • Field impression and interventions
  • Reassessment

Scene (before you touch them) NREMT

  • BSI / PPE
  • Scene safe for you, partner, patient, bystanders
  • MOI vs NOI
  • Number of patients
  • Extra resources (fire, police, ALS, more units)
  • Call law early if you suspect abuse/violence or the scene is escalating

Primary (every patient) NREMT

Goal: ABCs + transport priority.

  • General impression: sick vs not, bleeding, distress, trauma vs medical, age, position
  • AVPU. If Alert, check person, place, time, event
  • Alert and oriented ×4 → expressed consent. Confused / V / P / U → implied consent
  • Airway open and clear. No stethoscope: snoring = tongue; gurgling = fluid → suction; stridor = upper swelling
  • Breathing: adult 12–20. Chest rise, work of breathing. Lower sounds with a stethoscope: wheeze = bronchoconstriction (often exhalation); crackles = alveolar fluid; rhonchi = mucus/congestion
  • Circulation: adult pulse 60–100; skin CTC; cap refill especially kids; stop major bleeding as soon as you see it
  • Rapid scan if critical trauma
  • Load-and-go vs more time on scene

History (ABCs handled, they can talk) NREMT

OPQRST: onset, provocation/palliation, quality, region/radiation, severity 0–10, time.

SAMPLE: signs/symptoms, allergies, medications, past history, last oral intake, events.

Alcohol/drugs: calm, not judging. You may re-ask once, then document “Patient denies alcohol or drug use.” Most EMT treatments do not change on undisclosed use. Reasonable decisions with the facts you had.

Abuse: mandatory report on suspicion. Not defamation to report suspicion. Defamation is stating guilt as fact. Write what you see and what they said.

Pregnancy: any childbearing-age female with lower abdominal pain — “Any chance you’re pregnant?” and last menstrual period. Ask bowel/bladder and N/V/D with belly complaints. STI/sexual history only if it matters clinically.

Chart: objective, verifiable, true. Quotes, not guilt.

Secondary NREMT

  • Focused exam: responsive medical who can point at the problem
  • Detailed head-to-toe: unresponsive, significant MOI, or you suspect hidden/multiple injuries
  • Look, feel, listen. DCAP-BTLS on each region. Compare sides.

DCAP-BTLS: deformities, contusions, abrasions, punctures/penetrations, burns, tenderness, lacerations, swelling.

Numbers NREMT

  • Adult RR 12–20; kids faster
  • Adult pulse 60–100; kids higher
  • Adult systolic 90–120. <90 hypotension/shock. 120–130 hypertension range. Systolic is your emergency focus
  • Ped systolic min (<10 yr) = 2 × age + 70. Age 10 = 90, then adult cutoff
  • GCS: E 1–4, V 1–5, M 1–6. Total 3–15. 15 alert. 3 nothing
  • PERRL. Pinpoint often opioids. Dilated often stimulants or brain injury. Note unequal, blown, sluggish
  • SpO₂ 94–99%. CO can read falsely high. It does not count how many red cells you have
  • Glucose 80–120. <80 low. Check altered, known diabetic, stroke vs drunk vs sugar

Impression, treat, reassess NREMT

Field impression is what you think is going on, not a hospital diagnosis.

ABCs and life threats before medications. Inadequate breathing → ventilate before the drug.

Reassess mental status/GCS, ABCs, vitals, response to treatment. Unstable ~5 min. Stable ~15 min.

Handoff: scene + complaint, primary/life threats, OPQRST/SAMPLE hits, exam + vitals/trends, GCS/pupils, treatments and effect.

Practice

  • Do this: Recite the six-step flow without looking. You did it right if you said scene, primary, history, secondary, impression/interventions, reassessment — in that order.
  • Do this: Name the five scene tasks plus when you call law. You did it right if you have BSI, safety, MOI/NOI, number, resources, and law for abuse or an escalating scene.
  • Do this: Pair upper-airway sounds you hear without a stethoscope. You did it right if snoring is tongue, gurgling is fluid and suction, stridor is swelling.
  • Do this: Give adult RR, pulse, systolic band, and the peds hypotension formula. You did it right if 12–20, 60–100, 90–120, and 2×age+70 under age 10.
  • Do this: Score a fully alert patient and a no-response patient on GCS. You did it right if 15 (4+5+6) and 3.
  • Do this: Pick focused vs detailed for a talking belly-pain patient vs an ejection. You did it right if focused for the first and detailed for the second.
  • Do this: Write one chart line for a bruise and one for a quote. You did it right if the bruise is what you see and the quote is in their words, not “he is an abuser.”
  • Do this: Choose ventilate vs give a drug when breathing is inadequate. You did it right if you ventilate first.

Reassessment — key

Reassessment is the fifth part of patient assessment. Transport is not the end of the job.

How often NREMT

  • Unstable / critical: every 5 minutes. By the time you finish, it is almost time to start again
  • Stable: every 15 minutes

What you recheck NREMT

  • Airway still open and protected
  • Breathing: rate, depth, quality, work of breathing
  • Circulation: pulse, skin, major bleeding
  • Mental status: LOC, orientation, how they interact
  • Full vital sets, not one number

One set is a snapshot. Two or more sets show trends. BP dropping with HR and RR rising can mean they are sliding toward shock. Flat numbers can mean they are at least not clearly worse or better.

Big change = start over NREMT

Any significant change (better or worse) resets you to primary: mental status → airway → breathing → circulation.

They were talking and go unresponsive: restart that order now.

You get a pulse back during CPR: stop compressions, then reassess MS, airway, breathing, circulation. Do not assume ROSC means they are breathing well. You may still need to ventilate.

If you did it, you re-check it NREMT

Ask: is this still working, and is it still safe?

  • Tourniquet: bleeding still controlled, still in place, not too loose or too tight
  • Splint: swelling can make a good splint too tight. Distal PMS, cap refill, wiggle fingers/toes, new numbness/tingling/severe pain
  • Chest pain meds (nitro, aspirin where appropriate): “Your pain was 8/10; what is it now?” 8 → 5 is progress
  • Low sugar: recheck glucose after oral glucose or other treatment
  • Low oxygen: recheck SpO₂, respiratory status, work of breathing after O₂
  • Restraints: still secure, not cutting circulation. Distal CSM, cap refill, warmth, color, they can feel and move fingers and toes

Meds on the exam vs the street NREMT

On National Registry-style questions, assume meds are online orders unless the stem already gives you orders or a protocol that authorizes the drug. Safe default: contact medical control for permission before you give it.

If the stem says you have orders or protocol authorization, do not pick “call medical control first.”

Street world is often standing (offline) orders. Test world is online unless told otherwise.

Sequence is the answer NREMT

Right pieces in the wrong order is a wrong answer.

Default test order: contact medical control → administer → reassess.

Do not pick “give the drug, then call, then reassess.”

Why this exists NREMT

You are building a field impression and picking treatments. Reassessment, ABCs, and vital trends answer: what is going on, and what are you doing about it?

Practice

  • Do this: Say the two clocks out loud. You did it right if unstable is 5 minutes and stable is 15.
  • Do this: List what one full reassess pass includes. You did it right if you have mental status, ABCs, a new full vital set, and every intervention you already did.
  • Do this: Read two BP/HR/RR sets that are sliding. You did it right if you called the second set a trend toward shock, not a snapshot.
  • Do this: Act ROSC after CPR. You did it right if you stopped compressions and rechecked mental status, airway, breathing, and circulation — and you said they may still need ventilations.
  • Do this: Recheck a splint after swelling. You did it right if you named distal pulse, motor, sensation, cap refill, and new numbness or severe pain.
  • Do this: Ask a chest-pain pain score after nitro. You did it right if you compared now to the first number (8 to 5 is improvement).
  • Do this: Pick the exam med sequence when the stem does not give orders. You did it right if you contact medical control, then give the drug, then reassess.
  • Do this: Cross out an option that has the right three steps in the wrong order. You did it right if you treated that option as wrong.

Airway and suction — key

Airway before breathing. If air cannot reach the lungs, you cannot assess or fix breathing.

Patent or not NREMT

Conscious and talking or crying → airway is patent. Say “airway check” and move to breathing.

Unconscious → assume they are not protecting the airway. Muscles relax, the tongue falls back, it can fully obstruct. Open and clear before you judge breathing.

Position NREMT

Patient supine if you can. You at the top of the head.

Conscious people often sit up to protect their own airway.

Trapped and you cannot lay them supine: urgent/extrication move. You may give up ideal spine protection to treat an immediate airway or breathing life threat.

Two openers NREMT

Head-tilt–chin-lift: no spinal concern. One hand on forehead, one under the bony chin. Tilt to pull the tongue off the back of the throat. Easier, more effective, easier to hold. Example: elderly found unconscious in bed; witnessed collapse on a couch with no fall.

Jaw thrust: suspected or possible spine, or you are not sure — err cautious. Fingers behind the angle of the jaw, thumbs on the cheekbones. Lift the jaw forward (underbite) while the head and neck stay in line. Harder to hold; if you let go, the tongue drops and the obstruction returns. Built to protect C-spine while you open the airway.

Four EMT airway steps (this lesson) NREMT

  • Open (tilt-chin or jaw thrust)
  • Assess — open the mouth, look for blood/vomit/secretions/foreign material/teeth, listen for gurgling. Do not overcomplicate the mouth open; cross-finger is mostly textbook
  • Clear — suction only if you see fluid/blood/vomit/secretions or you hear gurgling. If the stem never mentions fluid or gurgling, do not pick suction
  • Maintain — keep the tongue from coming back (adjuncts, position, more suction, ventilate as needed)

Gear NREMT

  • Battery portable unit: usual on scene
  • Wall unit in the ambulance/hospital
  • Hand-powered backup: tiring; stuff can fall back if you pause

Rigid Yankauer: go-to oral suction. Bigger opening for vomit, blood, chunks.

Flexible French/whistle tip: smaller, clogs easier. Trach tubes or down an ET tube — not a mouth full of vomit.

Start-of-shift check: power/battery/charging, tubing and canister on, vacuum. Book adequate vacuum is greater than 300 mmHg. Gauge, light, or glove-finger test.

Tubing, catheter, canister: single patient, disposable, biohazard trash. Wipe the machine body. Restock and recharge.

Suction steps NREMT

Open the airway first. Supine, you at the head. Mouth open. Look and listen.

If indicated: unit on, confirm suction.

Measure: corner of mouth to earlobe, or corner of mouth to angle of the jaw. That is max depth.

Insert with the vent hole uncovered (no suction on the way in). Only as far as you can see / measured depth. Not down the trachea.

Cover the vent, suction while you withdraw with gentle circles. Do not suction while you advance.

Time: until clear, max 10 seconds. Same limit for adults, children, infants.

Between passes: suction sterile water through the tubing to clear clots.

Safety NREMT

Aspiration (blood/vomit/secretions into the lungs) raises mortality about 30–70% on top of the original problem.

No suction ready: log roll to the side so gravity drains.

Dyspnea: sit them upright. That is often the fastest first move.

Catheter keeps clogging: stop the failed loop. Roll to the side, drain, suction, scoop with the catheter — not your finger.

Cycle if they are still dirty: suction up to 10 seconds, ventilate about 2 minutes, repeat. You may push leftover material into the lungs. No oxygen is 100% mortality. That is worse than a 30–70% extra risk. Least-bad choice: oxygenate after you have suctioned as far as the time limit allows.

Once the airway is clear, say “airway check” and go to breathing: rate, rhythm, quality, oxygen or ventilations.

Practice

  • Do this: Point at a talking patient, then an unconscious one. You did it right if talking is “airway check, go to breathing,” and unconscious is assume not protected, open and clear first.
  • Do this: Pick tilt-chin vs jaw thrust for bed-found medical vs a wreck. You did it right if no-spine medical is tilt-chin and possible spine (or unsure) is jaw thrust.
  • Do this: Name the only two suction triggers. You did it right if you see fluid/blood/vomit/secretions or you hear gurgling — and you refused suction when the stem has neither.
  • Do this: Measure a rigid catheter on your own face. You did it right if it is corner of mouth to earlobe or angle of jaw, and you do not bury it in the trachea.
  • Do this: Mime insert vs withdraw. You did it right if the vent is open on the way in and you cover it and circle only on the way out, 10 seconds max for every age.
  • Do this: Choose portable vs wall vs hand, and rigid vs flexible. You did it right if scene is battery portable, oral vomit is rigid, trach/ET is flexible.
  • Do this: Vomit and no suction in your hand. You did it right if you log-roll to the side for gravity while a partner grabs the unit.
  • Do this: Still dirty after 10 seconds. You did it right if you ventilate about 2 minutes next, because no oxygen is 100% mortality.
