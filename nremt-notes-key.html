Patient assessment overview — key

Roadmap for every patient: walk-in to handoff.

Flow NREMT

1. Scene size-up

2. Primary — find and treat life threats

3. History — OPQRST and SAMPLE

4. Secondary — physical exam + full vitals

5. Field impression and interventions

6. Reassessment

Scene (before you touch them) NREMT

  • BSI / PPE
  • Scene safe for you, partner, patient, bystanders
  • MOI vs NOI
  • Number of patients
  • Extra resources (fire, police, ALS, more units)
  • Call law early if you suspect abuse/violence or the scene is escalating

Primary (every patient) NREMT

Goal: ABCs + transport priority.

  • General impression: sick vs not, bleeding, distress, trauma vs medical, age, position
  • AVPU. If Alert, check person, place, time, event
  • Alert and oriented ×4 → expressed consent. Confused / V / P / U → implied consent
  • Airway open and clear. No stethoscope: snoring = tongue; gurgling = fluid → suction; stridor = upper swelling
  • Breathing: adult 12–20. Chest rise, work of breathing. Lower sounds with a stethoscope: wheeze = bronchoconstriction (often exhalation); crackles = alveolar fluid; rhonchi = mucus/congestion
  • Circulation: adult pulse 60–100; skin CTC; cap refill especially kids; stop major bleeding as soon as you see it
  • Rapid scan if critical trauma
  • Load-and-go vs more time on scene

History (ABCs handled, they can talk) NREMT

OPQRST: onset, provocation/palliation, quality, region/radiation, severity 0–10, time.

SAMPLE: signs/symptoms, allergies, medications, past history, last oral intake, events.

Alcohol/drugs: calm, not judging. You may re-ask once, then document “Patient denies alcohol or drug use.” Most EMT treatments do not change on undisclosed use. Reasonable decisions with the facts you had.

Abuse: mandatory report on suspicion. Not defamation to report suspicion. Defamation is stating guilt as fact. Write what you see and what they said.

Pregnancy: any childbearing-age female with lower abdominal pain — “Any chance you’re pregnant?” and last menstrual period. Ask bowel/bladder and N/V/D with belly complaints. STI/sexual history only if it matters clinically.

Chart: objective, verifiable, true. Quotes, not guilt.

Secondary NREMT

  • Focused exam: responsive medical who can point at the problem
  • Detailed head-to-toe: unresponsive, significant MOI, or you suspect hidden/multiple injuries
  • Look, feel, listen. DCAP-BTLS on each region. Compare sides.

DCAP-BTLS: deformities, contusions, abrasions, punctures/penetrations, burns, tenderness, lacerations, swelling.

Numbers NREMT

  • Adult RR 12–20; kids faster
  • Adult pulse 60–100; kids higher
  • Adult systolic 90–120. <90 hypotension/shock. 120–130 hypertension range. Systolic is your emergency focus
  • Ped systolic min (<10 yr) = 2 × age + 70. Age 10 = 90, then adult cutoff
  • GCS: E 1–4, V 1–5, M 1–6. Total 3–15. 15 alert. 3 nothing
  • PERRL. Pinpoint often opioids. Dilated often stimulants or brain injury. Note unequal, blown, sluggish
  • SpO₂ 94–99%. CO can read falsely high. It does not count how many red cells you have
  • Glucose 80–120. <80 low. Check altered, known diabetic, stroke vs drunk vs sugar

Impression, treat, reassess NREMT

Field impression is what you think is going on, not a hospital diagnosis.

ABCs and life threats before medications. Inadequate breathing → ventilate before the drug.

Reassess mental status/GCS, ABCs, vitals, response to treatment. Unstable ~5 min. Stable ~15 min.

Handoff: scene + complaint, primary/life threats, OPQRST/SAMPLE hits, exam + vitals/trends, GCS/pupils, treatments and effect.

Practice

  • Do this: Recite the six-step flow without looking. You did it right if you said scene, primary, history, secondary, impression/interventions, reassessment — in that order.
  • Do this: Name the five scene tasks plus when you call law. You did it right if you have BSI, safety, MOI/NOI, number, resources, and law for abuse or an escalating scene.
  • Do this: Pair upper-airway sounds you hear without a stethoscope. You did it right if snoring is tongue, gurgling is fluid and suction, stridor is swelling.
  • Do this: Give adult RR, pulse, systolic band, and the peds hypotension formula. You did it right if 12–20, 60–100, 90–120, and 2×age+70 under age 10.
  • Do this: Score a fully alert patient and a no-response patient on GCS. You did it right if 15 (4+5+6) and 3.
  • Do this: Pick focused vs detailed for a talking belly-pain patient vs an ejection. You did it right if focused for the first and detailed for the second.
  • Do this: Write one chart line for a bruise and one for a quote. You did it right if the bruise is what you see and the quote is in their words, not “he is an abuser.”
  • Do this: Choose ventilate vs give a drug when breathing is inadequate. You did it right if you ventilate first.

Reassessment — key

Reassessment is the fifth part of patient assessment. Transport is not the end of the job.

How often NREMT

  • Unstable / critical: every 5 minutes. By the time you finish, it is almost time to start again
  • Stable: every 15 minutes

What you recheck NREMT

  • Airway still open and protected
  • Breathing: rate, depth, quality, work of breathing
  • Circulation: pulse, skin, major bleeding
  • Mental status: LOC, orientation, how they interact
  • Full vital sets, not one number

One set is a snapshot. Two or more sets show trends. BP dropping with HR and RR rising can mean they are sliding toward shock. Flat numbers can mean they are at least not clearly worse or better.

Big change = start over NREMT

Any significant change (better or worse) resets you to primary: mental status → airway → breathing → circulation.

They were talking and go unresponsive: restart that order now.

You get a pulse back during CPR: stop compressions, then reassess MS, airway, breathing, circulation. Do not assume ROSC means they are breathing well. You may still need to ventilate.

If you did it, you re-check it NREMT

Ask: is this still working, and is it still safe?

  • Tourniquet: bleeding still controlled, still in place, not too loose or too tight
  • Splint: swelling can make a good splint too tight. Distal PMS, cap refill, wiggle fingers/toes, new numbness/tingling/severe pain
  • Chest pain meds (nitro, aspirin where appropriate): “Your pain was 8/10; what is it now?” 8 → 5 is progress
  • Low sugar: recheck glucose after oral glucose or other treatment
  • Low oxygen: recheck SpO₂, respiratory status, work of breathing after O₂
  • Restraints: still secure, not cutting circulation. Distal CSM, cap refill, warmth, color, they can feel and move fingers and toes

Meds on the exam vs the street NREMT

On National Registry-style questions, assume meds are online orders unless the stem already gives you orders or a protocol that authorizes the drug. Safe default: contact medical control for permission before you give it.

If the stem says you have orders or protocol authorization, do not pick “call medical control first.”

Street world is often standing (offline) orders. Test world is online unless told otherwise.

Sequence is the answer NREMT

Right pieces in the wrong order is a wrong answer.

Default test order: contact medical control → administer → reassess.

Do not pick “give the drug, then call, then reassess.”

Why this exists NREMT

You are building a field impression and picking treatments. Reassessment, ABCs, and vital trends answer: what is going on, and what are you doing about it?

Practice

  • Do this: Say the two clocks out loud. You did it right if unstable is 5 minutes and stable is 15.
  • Do this: List what one full reassess pass includes. You did it right if you have mental status, ABCs, a new full vital set, and every intervention you already did.
  • Do this: Read two BP/HR/RR sets that are sliding. You did it right if you called the second set a trend toward shock, not a snapshot.
  • Do this: Act ROSC after CPR. You did it right if you stopped compressions and rechecked mental status, airway, breathing, and circulation — and you said they may still need ventilations.
  • Do this: Recheck a splint after swelling. You did it right if you named distal pulse, motor, sensation, cap refill, and new numbness or severe pain.
  • Do this: Ask a chest-pain pain score after nitro. You did it right if you compared now to the first number (8 to 5 is improvement).
  • Do this: Pick the exam med sequence when the stem does not give orders. You did it right if you contact medical control, then give the drug, then reassess.
  • Do this: Cross out an option that has the right three steps in the wrong order. You did it right if you treated that option as wrong.

Respiratory physiology and assessment — key

After scene safety, massive bleed, and they are alive, airway is next. See a life threat, treat a life threat — that includes a catastrophic airway.

Three words (this order) NREMT

Ventilation: air moving in and out. Ask “is air moving?” Rate, rhythm, quality, chest rise.

Respiration: gas exchange (O₂ / CO₂). Alveoli ↔ lung capillaries, later blood ↔ cells.

Oxygenation: O₂ on hemoglobin. You estimate it with pulse ox.

Looks sick but ventilation is fine: think respiration first (fluid, pneumonia, COPD), then oxygenation (sat, hemoglobin), then circulation (shock, heart).

Upper vs lower NREMT

Upper (above the larynx): nose, nasopharynx, oropharynx, epiglottis, larynx / cords. Warm, filter, humidify. Failures: tongue, swelling (allergy/infection), trauma, blood/vomit.

Lower (below the larynx): trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Job is gas exchange.

Phrenic / C3–4–5 NREMT

“C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive.” Injury above C3–5: diaphragm out, they cannot breathe. Injury below C5: may be paralyzed but can still breathe if the phrenic nerve is intact.

Pressure NREMT

Normal inhale is active: diaphragm and intercostals contract → cavity bigger → negative pressure inside the chest → air is pulled in. Exhale is passive: muscles relax, cavity shrinks, air goes out.

When you bag: you push air in (positive pressure). Risks: air in the stomach and vomit; over-squeeze can pop a lung.

Volumes (EMT level) NREMT

Tidal volume = one normal breath. You judge it by chest rise, not a number.

Inspiratory / expiratory reserve = extra you could force in or out.

Residual volume = air left in the alveoli after a full exhale. Keeps them open so they do not collapse between breaths.

Drive to breathe NREMT

Primary: high CO₂ (central chemoreceptors → brainstem → phrenic → diaphragm).

Backup: low O₂ (hypoxic drive). Matters later in severe COPD, when CO₂ is always high and stops being a useful signal.

Adequate vs inadequate (adult) NREMT

Adequate: 12–20/min, regular, good chest rise, clear equal sounds.

Can still be adequate if the rate is a little high (example 22) but volume is good, sounds are clear, they are alert.

Inadequate: under 12 or over 20 with poor volume or irregular rhythm; little or no rise; severe retractions / accessory muscles; agonal, apnea, or chaotic; cyanosis, poor LOC, poor sat.

Patterns NREMT

Agonal: rare, gasping, spastic — arrest. Not breathing. CPR + ventilations.

Cheyne–Stokes: shallow/slow → deeper/faster → slow → pause → repeats. Often brain injury. Bad.

Biot’s / ataxic: no pattern at all. Severe brainstem.

Kussmaul: deep and rapid. Metabolic acidosis (DKA, aspirin OD). The body is compensating — do not slam it down by over-bagging.

Chest NREMT

Pneumothorax: air in the chest but not inside the lung (collapsed lung). Trauma, rib break, puncture, or over-aggressive bag.

Hemothorax: blood in the chest.

Hemopneumothorax: both.

One-sided badness: the other lung may be doing all the work.

Pulse ox NREMT

% of hemoglobin that is saturated. Target 94–99%.

Give O₂ for sat under 94, difficulty breathing, or shock.

Lies or misleads: anemia, low volume, cold hands, nail polish, bright light. Carbon monoxide: sat can look 100% while the seats are full of CO, not O₂.

Capnography (concept) NREMT

Good end-tidal CO₂ means ventilation, gas exchange, circulation, and cells making CO₂ are all working.

Shark-fin waveform: air is hard to get out — asthma / COPD.

Primary check NREMT

Look for massive bleed, severe distress, cyanosis, altered LOC, face/neck/chest trauma.

Talking clearly → airway is likely open. Snoring, gurgling, stridor, silence, blood/vomit/foreign body → fix it.

Trauma worry → jaw thrust. No trauma → head-tilt chin-lift. Suction if gurgling/fluids. OPA/NPA when they fit.

Count rate 30 sec × 2 (adult 12–20). Rhythm and special patterns. Rise, shallow vs normal, accessory muscles, retractions, tripod, nasal flare, pursed lips.

Listen if you can: equal and clear vs diminished, crackles, wheeze, absent.

Skin: color, temp, wet vs dry. Pulse ox if it does not delay a killer.

Oxygen vs bag NREMT

Not breathing or only agonal: extra help / AED. If no pulse, CPR. Bag with oxygen.

Breathing but inadequate: assist with BVM + O₂. Open + adjunct. Seal, right mask. Slow gentle squeezes — just visible rise. Over-squeeze → pneumothorax, stomach air, vomit. Rate per protocol (often about 10–12/min for an adult with a pulse). Recheck rise, skin, mind, sat.

Breathing adequately but sick or hypoxic (dyspnea, sat <94%, shock, chest pain, distress): keep the airway, give supplemental O₂ (device per protocol), watch rate/rhythm/quality, sat trend, mental status.

Red flags (move / ALS) NREMT

Suspected pneumo or hemo (short of breath + one-sided silent lung ± trauma). Severe distress you cannot fix with basics. Head/neck trauma with a weird pattern. Kussmaul plus DKA or poisoning signs. Anyone you must keep bagging.

Difficulty-breathing pass NREMT

Primary first. Then onset (sudden vs slow), what makes it better/worse (flat, work), tight vs burning vs cannot get air in vs out, associated (pain, cough, fever, wheeze, swelling, trauma), past (asthma, COPD, CHF, recent illness, diabetes), meds (inhalers, home O₂, diuretics, insulin, blood thinners).

Look: tripod, full sentences?, chest symmetry/trauma, sounds, edema, JVD, cyanosis. Treat O₂ or BVM. Help with their prescribed inhaler if protocol allows.

Practice

  • Do this: Name the order after safety. You did it right if massive bleed and “are they alive” come before airway, and a catastrophic airway is still “see it, treat it.”
  • Do this: Say ventilation vs respiration vs oxygenation in one breath each. You did it right if ventilation is air moving, respiration is gas exchange, oxygenation is O₂ on hemoglobin (pulse ox).
  • Do this: They look terrible but chest rise is good. You did it right if you work respiration first (fluid / pneumonia / COPD), then sat/hemoglobin, then shock/heart — not “bag harder.”
  • Do this: Point C3–4–5. You did it right if above that level means the diaphragm is out, and below C5 they may still breathe.
  • Do this: Mime a normal breath, then a bag squeeze. You did it right if inhale is negative (cavity bigger, air pulled in) and a bag is positive (you push; stomach air and popped lung if you over-squeeze).
  • Do this: Judge tidal volume without a number. You did it right if you used visible chest rise, and residual volume is the leftover that keeps alveoli open.
  • Do this: Recite the adult adequate box. You did it right if 12–20, regular, good rise, clear equal sounds — and 22 with good volume and an alert patient can still be adequate.
  • Do this: Name agonal vs Cheyne–Stokes vs Biot’s vs Kussmaul. You did it right if agonal is arrest (CPR + bag), Cheyne–Stokes is the wax-wane-pause (brain), Biot’s is chaos (brainstem), Kussmaul is deep-fast (acidosis — do not slam it).
  • Do this: Read a sat of 100% on a house-fire patient. You did it right if you did not trust it as proof of oxygen — CO can fill the seats.
  • Do this: Decide bag vs mask O₂. You did it right if no breath / agonal / poor volume gets a bag, and a breathing-but-hypoxic or shocked patient gets supplemental O₂ with the airway held.
  • Do this: Bag an adult who still has a pulse. You did it right if the squeeze only makes visible rise, you did not blast the stomach, and the rate is in the local ~10–12 range.

Airway and suction — key

Airway before breathing. If air cannot reach the lungs, you cannot assess or fix breathing.

Patent or not NREMT

Conscious and talking or crying → airway is patent. Say “airway check” and move to breathing.

Unconscious → assume they are not protecting the airway. Muscles relax, the tongue falls back, it can fully obstruct. Open and clear before you judge breathing.

Position NREMT

Patient supine if you can. You at the top of the head.

Conscious people often sit up to protect their own airway.

Trapped and you cannot lay them supine: urgent/extrication move. You may give up ideal spine protection to treat an immediate airway or breathing life threat.

Two openers NREMT

Head-tilt–chin-lift: no spinal concern. One hand on forehead, one under the bony chin. Tilt to pull the tongue off the back of the throat. Easier, more effective, easier to hold. Example: elderly found unconscious in bed; witnessed collapse on a couch with no fall.

Jaw thrust: suspected or possible spine, or you are not sure — err cautious. Fingers behind the angle of the jaw, thumbs on the cheekbones. Lift the jaw forward (underbite) while the head and neck stay in line. Harder to hold; if you let go, the tongue drops and the obstruction returns. Built to protect C-spine while you open the airway.

Four EMT airway steps (this lesson) NREMT

1. Open (tilt-chin or jaw thrust)

2. Assess — open the mouth, look for blood/vomit/secretions/foreign material/teeth, listen for gurgling. Do not overcomplicate the mouth open; cross-finger is mostly textbook

3. Clear — suction only if you see fluid/blood/vomit/secretions or you hear gurgling. If the stem never mentions fluid or gurgling, do not pick suction

4. Maintain — keep the tongue from coming back (adjuncts, position, more suction, ventilate as needed)

Gear NREMT

  • Battery portable unit: usual on scene
  • Wall unit in the ambulance/hospital
  • Hand-powered backup: tiring; stuff can fall back if you pause

Rigid Yankauer: go-to oral suction. Bigger opening for vomit, blood, chunks.

Flexible French/whistle tip: smaller, clogs easier. Trach tubes or down an ET tube — not a mouth full of vomit.

Start-of-shift check: power/battery/charging, tubing and canister on, vacuum. Book adequate vacuum is greater than 300 mmHg. Gauge, light, or glove-finger test.

Tubing, catheter, canister: single patient, disposable, biohazard trash. Wipe the machine body. Restock and recharge.

Suction steps NREMT

Open the airway first. Supine, you at the head. Mouth open. Look and listen.

If indicated: unit on, confirm suction.

Measure: corner of mouth to earlobe, or corner of mouth to angle of the jaw. That is max depth.

Insert with the vent hole uncovered (no suction on the way in). Only as far as you can see / measured depth. Not down the trachea.

Cover the vent, suction while you withdraw with gentle circles. Do not suction while you advance.

Time: until clear, max 10 seconds. Same limit for adults, children, infants.

Between passes: suction sterile water through the tubing to clear clots.

Safety NREMT

Aspiration (blood/vomit/secretions into the lungs) raises mortality about 30–70% on top of the original problem.

No suction ready: log roll to the side so gravity drains.

Dyspnea: sit them upright. That is often the fastest first move.

Catheter keeps clogging: stop the failed loop. Roll to the side, drain, suction, scoop with the catheter — not your finger.

Cycle if they are still dirty: suction up to 10 seconds, ventilate about 2 minutes, repeat. You may push leftover material into the lungs. No oxygen is 100% mortality. That is worse than a 30–70% extra risk. Least-bad choice: oxygenate after you have suctioned as far as the time limit allows.

Once the airway is clear, say “airway check” and go to breathing: rate, rhythm, quality, oxygen or ventilations.

Practice

  • Do this: Point at a talking patient, then an unconscious one. You did it right if talking is “airway check, go to breathing,” and unconscious is assume not protected, open and clear first.
  • Do this: Pick tilt-chin vs jaw thrust for bed-found medical vs a wreck. You did it right if no-spine medical is tilt-chin and possible spine (or unsure) is jaw thrust.
  • Do this: Name the only two suction triggers. You did it right if you see fluid/blood/vomit/secretions or you hear gurgling — and you refused suction when the stem has neither.
  • Do this: Measure a rigid catheter on your own face. You did it right if it is corner of mouth to earlobe or angle of jaw, and you do not bury it in the trachea.
  • Do this: Mime insert vs withdraw. You did it right if the vent is open on the way in and you cover it and circle only on the way out, 10 seconds max for every age.
  • Do this: Choose portable vs wall vs hand, and rigid vs flexible. You did it right if scene is battery portable, oral vomit is rigid, trach/ET is flexible.
  • Do this: Vomit and no suction in your hand. You did it right if you log-roll to the side for gravity while a partner grabs the unit.
  • Do this: Still dirty after 10 seconds. You did it right if you ventilate about 2 minutes next, because no oxygen is 100% mortality.

OPA, NPA, and oxygen — key

Primary assessment is immediate life threats: airway, need for CPR, major bleeding. Primary plus treat/transport is about two-thirds of the test.

Unconscious, no bystanders, no medical ID, no med list: skip SAMPLE/OPQRST. Go to secondary / physical exam.

Adjuncts (maintain step) NREMT

Goal: keep the tongue from blocking the airway.

OPA: rigid, mouth. NPA: flexible, nose. Both stop just past the base of the tongue so air can still pass if the tongue falls back.

OPA NREMT

Use only if fully unresponsive (AVPU = U): not awake, no verbal, no pain, no gag.

Gag on insert → they still have a gag. Pull it now. Expect vomit.

Do not use if conscious, semi-conscious, or any gag.

When they are truly out and have no gag, OPA first: it holds the tongue off the back of the throat better than NPA.

Size: corner of mouth to earlobe, or corner of mouth to angle of the jaw.

Open the airway first (tilt-chin or jaw thrust). Do not shove it straight in from the front — that rams the tongue down.

Two legal insert methods: upside down then 180° rotate, or sideways then 90° rotate. Pull it out along the curve.

Cardiac arrest / CPR: muscles and tongue are floppy. OPA is fine if unresponsive and no gag.

NPA NREMT

Use when they need a path but still have a gag — semi-conscious or even alert. Also if unresponsive but gagging. Usually does not trigger gag the way an OPA does.

Hard stop: any head or face trauma, especially suspected basilar skull fracture. The tube can go through a broken skull base into the brain. Positive-pressure ventilation can then blow air into the cranial vault.

Head/face trauma: mouth OPA is OK if no gag. Nose NPA is never OK.

Unresponsive: try OPA. If they gag, pull it. NPA only if no head/face trauma.

Gag plus head trauma: no OPA, no NPA. You hold the airway by hand and fight the tongue until ALS can put in a higher airway.

Size: nose to earlobe, or nose to angle of the jaw.

Water-based medical lube. Bevel toward the septum. Gentle, straight in; it follows the curve and ends past the tongue base. Single-use, then trash.

Recovery position NREMT

Unconscious, not injured (no suspected spine), breathing well on their own → roll on the side so the airway stays open and vomit/secretions can drain.

Deeply unconscious in EMS often needs a bag — then they stay supine.

When you give oxygen NREMT

Give O₂ if SpO₂ < 94%, or they look like respiratory distress (dyspnea, hard work of breathing), or they look like shock.

Target for most emergency patients: 94–99%.

Treat the patient, not the number. Distress with a “normal” sat still gets oxygen.

If they might benefit and they look sick, do not withhold.

Caution over time: extra O₂ can tighten vessels and can worsen stroke or MI. Free oxygen with no hemoglobin left to bind it irritates tissue (toxicity). COPD is extra sensitive — more on that later. If they are clearly compromised, still give O₂.

Hemoglobin = stadium seats. Full house → extra O₂ has nowhere to sit and causes trouble.

Tanks and regulators NREMT

D tank: small portable, scene / gurney. H tank: tall (~5 ft) mounted in the ambulance.

~2,000 psi (car tire ~30). Always secured, strapped, laid down, or mounted. Never let it fall. A broken valve turns it into a missile that can go through a wall.

Pin index: unique hole pattern so only the right gas and regulator mate. Oxygen tanks are green/silver. Stops filling a tank with the wrong gas (nitrous, CO₂, helium).

Regulator drops ~2,000 psi to about 40–70 psi. Flow meter sets L/min.

Bourdon gauge: works in any position — portable tanks you lay down. Pressure-compensated (vertical tube): must stay upright — usually wall-mounted.

Non-rebreather NREMT

Preferred high-concentration mask at EMT level.

Breathing adequately (rate and tidal volume OK) but hypoxic (SpO₂ < 94%), cyanotic, or short of breath / distress. Ventilation is fine; they need more O₂ than 21% room air.

Flow: 10–15 L/min (book/test). Commonly 12–15. Test default if they need O₂ and are breathing well: NRB 10–15 unless the stem names another device.

Tank is 100%. They get about 90–95% because the mask leaks room air.

Setup NREMT

Confirm O₂ tank (green/silver). Secure it. Pins match. Open, check gauge (full ≈ 2,000). Set L/min. Fit the mask; leak is why it is not 100%. Watch sat, color, effort, mental status. Titrate toward 94–99%. Worsening: recheck airway, need for BVM / ALS, transport priority.

Practice

  • Do this: Recite when you skip SAMPLE. You did it right if unconscious plus no bystanders / ID / med list means skip history and go to the physical exam.
  • Do this: Say who gets an OPA. You did it right if only AVPU = U with no gag, and gag on insert means pull it now and expect vomit.
  • Do this: Measure an OPA and an NPA on your face. You did it right if OPA is mouth-corner to ear or jaw angle, and NPA is nose to ear or jaw angle.
  • Do this: Mime both OPA inserts. You did it right if you open the airway first, then 180° from upside-down or 90° from the side — never a straight shove that drives the tongue down.
  • Do this: Say the NPA hard stop. You did it right if any head or face trauma (especially basilar skull) is no NPA, even if they need an adjunct.
  • Do this: Gag plus facial trauma — pick your tools. You did it right if you use neither adjunct and you hold the jaw/tongue by hand.
  • Do this: Name three reasons to give O₂. You did it right if sat under 94, distress (even with a “good” number), or shock — target 94–99%.
  • Do this: Set up an NRB on a breathing, hypoxic patient. You did it right if 10–15 L/min and you did not pick a cannula just because they look “pretty okay.”
  • Do this: Point at D vs H, Bourdon vs upright tube, and a loose tank. You did it right if D is portable, H is the big mounted tank, Bourdon works lying down, compensated must stand, and an unsecured tank is a missile at ~2,000 psi.

Oxygen devices and airway — key

Almost every call here is two questions: is the airway open and clear, and are they breathing adequately.

If the airway is not open: tilt-chin or jaw thrust, suction, OPA/NPA, treat choking.

If they are breathing adequately: supplemental O₂ (test default is a non-rebreather). If they are not breathing enough or not at all: bag them. Severe work with fluid in the lungs but still breathing on their own: think CPAP if they meet the rules.

Non-rebreather NREMT

Flow 10–15 L/min. About 90–95% O₂. Flaps let exhaled air out and stop them from rebreathing it.

Conscious or unconscious does not matter. They just need adequate spontaneous breathing.

Test world: adequate breathing plus distress, sat under 94%, or shock → NRB 10–15, not a cannula.

Nasal cannula NREMT

1–6 L/min. About 24–44% (1 L ≈ 24%, just above 21% room air; 6 L ≈ 44%).

Real world: mild hypoxia or a small bump. Registry: rarely the right pick.

Over 6 L dries and burns the nose. You usually run 2–4.

Mild sat around 92% and they look comfortable, minor SOB, or a home-O₂ user who does not need high flow.

Other masks NREMT

Partial rebreather = NRB with the side flaps off. Still 10–15 L/min on the face. About 80–90%. Rare. Sometimes mentioned for panic/hyperventilation.

Venturi: color adapters with a printed flow and exact % (24, 28, 32…). Long-term controlled O₂. EMS rarely carries it. You may see it in a facility.

Trach mask over a stoma: treat like a face mask — 10–15 L/min if you are using it as high flow.

Humidified O₂: bubble through sterile water. Comfort for long-term. Portable tanks often do not have it. Need O₂ now → give dry.

BVM NREMT

Apnea, or inadequate rate/rhythm/quality (too slow, too fast, shallow, poor rise).

10–15 L/min. Tight seal → close to 100%. Adult, child, infant bags. Same flow; only the rate changes.

Adult: 1 breath every 6 seconds = 10/min. Child and infant: 1 every 2–3 seconds = 20–30/min.

Gentle ~1-second squeeze until you see chest rise. Count out loud so you do not go too fast. If they still take weak breaths, try to squeeze with their inhale.

Too hard / too fast: air in the stomach → vomit and aspiration. Extra pressure in the chest squeezes the heart and great vessels → blood pressure drops. You can push them toward arrest.

CPAP NREMT

Constant pressure while they breathe. Back-pressure in the alveoli helps shove fluid out of the way (pulmonary edema). Like sticking your head out a car window at 70 mph.

They must be alert, follow commands, and protect the airway. Severe work, usually wet lungs, still breathing on their own. NRB is not enough and they are still fighting.

Do not use if: apneic or hypoventilating (they need a bag); cannot follow commands / unconscious; cannot protect the airway; systolic under 90 (it drops BP more); trauma, especially chest/face or suspected pneumothorax; they cannot tolerate the mask; vomiting, GI bleed, nausea; shock with low BP.

Choking NREMT

Mild: they can cough, speak, move air. Hands off. Encourage cough. You may add NRB so the air they move is richer.

Severe and conscious: no cough, no speech, turning blue, universal sign.

Adult/child (updated AHA): 5 back blows, 5 abdominal thrusts, repeat until it comes out or they go unresponsive.

Infant: 5 back blows (face down on your forearm, head supported), 5 chest thrusts (same spot as infant CPR). Alternate until resolved or unresponsive.

They collapse: supine, start CPR. Compressions move blood and help pop the object. Pulse or not — if they drop from choking, compress.

Other pearls NREMT

Stable dentures stay — they help the face hold a mask. Loose plates come out. Braces stay unless they block the path.

Face bleeds a lot and swells fast. Suction blood often. Swelling can close the airway. No NPA if you suspect facial trauma. No CPAP in trauma. External bleed: direct pressure first.

When you give O₂ NREMT

Adequately breathing plus: sat under 94%, any difficulty or labored breathing (even with a “normal” sat), or shock (cool pale clammy, altered, weak/rapid pulse).

Comfortable, no shock, sat in range → you probably do not need O₂.

Flow you run NREMT

1. Airway open? Tilt-chin (no trauma) or jaw thrust (possible spine). Look. Suction up to 10 seconds, then re-oxygenate.

2. OPA if fully out, no gag. NPA if they need a path but gag and have no face/head trauma. Stable dentures stay; loose ones go.

3. Rate, rhythm, quality. Adult 12–20. Chest rise. SpO₂. Work of breathing.

4. Adequate + need O₂ → NRB 10–15 (test). Inadequate or apnea → BVM 10–15 at the rates above. Wet, alert, SBP ≥ 90, no trauma/vomit/pneumo → CPAP. Any CPAP no → NRB or BVM.

5. Reassess airway, breathing, sat, mind, BP. Watch a rising belly and a falling BP.

Numbers NREMT

Room air 21%. NRB 10–15 → ~90–95%. NC 1–6 → ~24–44%. BVM 10–15. Adult bag 1/6 sec = 10/min. Child/infant 1/2–3 sec = 20–30/min. CPAP off if SBP < 90. Give O₂ if sat < 94% or distress or shock.

Circulatory intro — key

Pump (heart) + pipes (arteries, veins, capillaries) + fluid (blood). You need all three for pressure, O₂/glucose to cells, and CO₂/waste out. Any one fails → perfusion fails → shock.

Two loops NREMT

Pulmonary: right ventricle → lungs → left atrium. Gas exchange in the lungs: pick up O₂, drop off CO₂.

Systemic: left ventricle → body → right atrium. Deliver O₂ and nutrients; pick up CO₂ and waste.

Breathing problems plus heart issues: think how the two loops interact.

Chambers NREMT

Right atrium: deoxygenated blood from the body.

Right ventricle: deoxygenated blood to the lungs.

Left atrium: oxygenated blood from the lungs.

Left ventricle: oxygenated blood to the body.

Left ventricle wall is thicker/stronger — it pumps to the whole body. Right ventricle is thinner/weaker — it only pumps to the lungs.

Left-sided pump failure → backup into the lungs → shortness of breath, crackles, CHF picture.

Right-sided pump failure → backup into the body → JVD, peripheral edema (later chapters; use the logic now).

Flow path NREMT

Body → right heart → lungs → left heart → body.

Body → right atrium via SVC (head/upper body) and IVC (lower body) → tricuspid → right ventricle → pulmonic valve → pulmonary arteries (deoxygenated, to lungs) → alveoli (O₂ in, CO₂ out) → pulmonary veins (oxygenated) → left atrium → mitral/bicuspid → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → head, arms, trunk, legs.

Valve order: T–P–M–A (tricuspid, pulmonic, mitral, aortic).

Valves and sounds NREMT

AV: tricuspid (right), mitral/bicuspid (left).

Semilunar: pulmonic (RV → lungs), aortic (LV → body).

Open for forward flow; close to stop backflow. Lub-dub is valves snapping shut.

Mitral valve prolapse: valve does not close correctly; some backflow during contraction.

Muscle and electricity NREMT

Cardiac muscle can generate its own impulses. The brain can speed or slow the rate, but each beat does not need a brain signal. Unconscious does not automatically mean cardiac arrest — the heart can keep beating.

Gas exchange (two places) NREMT

Lungs (blood ↔ alveoli): O₂ into blood, CO₂ out to be exhaled.

Tissues (blood ↔ cells): O₂ into cells, CO₂ into blood.

Pump, pipes, fluid, or lungs fail → cells lose O₂ → shock.

Numbers NREMT

Adult resting HR 60–100. Tachycardia >100. Bradycardia <60.

Stroke volume: blood per beat. You do not measure it in the field.

Cardiac output: blood in one minute. CO = SV × HR.

Like breathing: tidal volume = air per breath; minute volume = tidal volume × rate.

HR or SV too low → CO falls → poor perfusion: altered mental status, weak pulses, cool/clammy skin, low BP.

Very high HR can drop SV (less filling time) and also drop CO.

Field checklist NREMT

Pump: rate, rhythm, quality. Poor CO: AMS, weak/absent peripheral pulses, cool pale clammy, low BP especially with tachycardia. Left clues = lung symptoms. Right clues = JVD, edema, weight gain.

Pipes: skin color/temp/moisture, cap refill (especially peds), peripheral vs central pulses. SOB plus poor circulation → think pump affecting the lungs. Failed valve → blood goes backward; use T–P–M–A to reason where.

Fluid: suspect hypovolemia with bleeding, vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration — tachycardia, low BP, cool pale clammy, thirst, weakness, dizziness. Normal perfusion: warm skin, normal mental status, strong pulses, normal BP.

Oxygenation: rate/effort, lung sounds (clear vs crackles/wheezes), SpO₂ if you have it. Left failure → fluid in lungs → crackles, SOB, possible CHF. Tissue hypoxia clues: AMS, cyanosis (late), cool pale, shock.

Anchors NREMT

Pump–pipes–fluid. Pulmonary = heart ↔ lungs. Systemic = heart ↔ body.

Left ventricle stronger (body). Right weaker (lungs).

Left fail → lungs. Right fail → body.

Circulatory fundamentals — key

Pump (heart) + pipes (vessels) + fluid (blood) = perfusion. You use this to read shock, vitals, and why meds or disease change BP and LOC.

Cardiac output NREMT

CO = SV × HR. Example: 80 mL × 70 bpm = 5.6 L/min.

Adult blood volume ~5–6 L. At rest you circulate essentially the whole volume every minute.

Drop SV (bleed, poor squeeze) or drop HR too low → CO falls → BP falls → poor perfusion → altered LOC.

Aorta NREMT

All left-ventricle output hits the aorta first. Aorta rupture: most or all blood volume can leave in about a minute — crash BP, they pass out, then arrest.

High suspicion: severe trauma or aneurysm rupture (sudden severe pain, shock, fast crash). Rapid transport.

Blood thinners NREMT

Anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs) block clots from forming or growing. In trauma or internal bleed they block normal clotting — bleed is worse and harder to stop.

Always ask: “Are you on blood thinners?” Minor injury + anticoagulants is still high risk.

Preload NREMT

Preload = venous return (blood coming back to the heart).

Starling / rubber band: more stretch → stronger squeeze → more out. Less in → weaker squeeze → less out.

Low preload: hemorrhage, severe dehydration, third-spacing. Result: less pumped, lower BP, less brain blood → syncope / AMS.

Shock from blood loss: “Preload is low → CO is low → brain is short on oxygen.” Look for tachycardia, weak pulses, cool/clammy, low BP, AMS.

Afterload NREMT

Afterload = systemic vascular resistance (how tight the arteries are).

Vasoconstriction (tiny straw) → BP up. Vasodilation (fire hose) → BP down.

Body compensates low BP with vasoconstriction (cool, pale, clammy). Anaphylaxis = massive vasodilation → BP crash. EpiPen vasoconstricts → BP and perfusion up.

Anaphylaxis and epi NREMT

Widespread vasodilation → afterload drops → BP crashes. Epinephrine: strong vasoconstriction (plus bronchodilation). Epi is life-saving because it reverses the dangerous vasodilation.

Electricity NREMT

Automaticity: heart cells fire their own impulses.

SA node = primary pacemaker (normal sinus). Path: SA fires → atria squeeze → AV node delays so ventricles fill → ventricles squeeze.

Pacemaker when the native system is unreliable. Electrical activity is what makes the mechanical pump you feel as a pulse.

Brain and syncope NREMT

Brain is picky about oxygen, sugar (glucose), and temperature.

Low preload/CO → less blood/O₂ to brain → they black out. Carotid squeeze (choke-out) does the same.

When they pass out: did the brain lose oxygen, sugar, or perfusion?

Arteries vs veins NREMT

Artery = away from the heart. Vein = back to the heart. Direction, not oxygen content.

Usual: arteries oxygenated, veins deoxygenated.

Exceptions: pulmonary arteries = deoxygenated RV → lungs. Umbilical arteries = deoxygenated baby → placenta. Umbilical vein = oxygenated placenta → baby.

Coronaries and MI NREMT

Coronary arteries run on the heart surface and feed the myocardium. RCA → right side. LCA → left side.

MI: plaque/clot clogs a coronary. Muscle loses O₂ (and sugar), cannot make energy, cells start to die. Chest pain, SOB, diaphoresis, nausea. Time is muscle.

Field prompts NREMT

Pump: CO = SV × HR. Rate, pulse quality, MI picture.

Fluid/preload: bleed (including occult/aorta), blood thinners, tachy, falling BP, cool pale clammy, delayed cap refill, weak pulses, dizziness/syncope/AMS.

Pipes/afterload: pale cool clammy = constriction (compensating). Flushed warm + hypotension = dilation (anaphylaxis, sepsis). Epi constricts.

Brain: LOC (AVPU, A&O). Syncope = O₂, sugar, or flow.

Electrical: SA primary; AV delay for fill. Note implanted pacers. Palpitations, skips, sudden dizziness/syncope.

Special vessels: pulmonary arteries (deox to lungs), coronaries (heart muscle; block = MI), carotids (brain; squeeze/block = syncope / stroke-like).

Circulatory + abdomen — key

Hepatic arteries → liver. Liver detoxifies blood, holds a large share of total blood volume (≈40%). Trauma to the liver is a major internal-bleed risk.

Renal arteries → kidneys. Kidneys filter the blood and decide what stays in the bloodstream vs what leaves as urine. On a test, “filters the blood” = kidneys.

Mesenteric arteries → intestines via the mesentery. The mesentery suspends the intestines and carries vessels to intestinal tissue.

Blood cells and the spleen NREMT

RBCs last ≈120 days. Bone marrow inside bone makes them.

Spleen (LUQ, solid, very vascular) removes old/damaged RBCs. Injury = major internal bleed. If the stem says “filters blood” for waste, think kidneys. If it is old RBC removal, think spleen.

Path and gas exchange NREMT

Left ventricle → aorta → arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins → vena cavae → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle.

Capillaries are the only place in the circulatory system where gas exchange occurs. O₂: blood → body cells. CO₂: cells → blood.

Alveoli + lung capillaries: O₂ in / CO₂ out. Gases move by diffusion (high → low).

Pulmonary arteries are the exception: deoxygenated blood, right ventricle → lungs.

Systemic veins carry deoxygenated blood back. SVC: head/upper body → right atrium. IVC: lower body → right atrium.

Arteries vs veins NREMT

Arteries: high pressure, usually oxygenated (except pulmonary arteries). You feel pulses in arteries. Arterial bleed: bright red, spurts with the heartbeat, rapidly life-threatening.

Veins: low pressure, usually deoxygenated (except pulmonary veins). You start IVs in veins (safer, less pressure). Venous bleed: darker red, flows/oozes.

Pulses and BP NREMT

A pulse is the pressure wave when the left ventricle contracts. You press an artery against a firm structure. Too much pressure can stop the pulse; too little and you will not feel it. Use fingers, not the thumb.

Carotid (neck): unresponsive adult life check.

Radial (wrist, thumb side): responsive adult.

Femoral (groin): severe shock/trauma.

Brachial (inside upper arm): infants and BP.

Dorsalis pedis (top of foot) and posterior tibial (behind medial ankle): distal circulation after a leg injury.

Why you check: dead vs alive / CPR, rate and quality, distal flow beyond a fracture or splint. Left vs right usually does not matter unless there is a local injury.

Systolic (top): pressure when the left ventricle contracts and ejects. Diastolic (bottom): pressure when the heart relaxes and refills. Flow is pulsatile, not a garden hose.

Abdomen NREMT

Solid organs (high blood content): liver, spleen, kidneys, pancreas. Injury risk = severe internal bleeding.

Hollow organs (can leak contents): stomach, intestines, bladder, gallbladder. Injury risk = leakage → peritonitis / severe infection.

Quadrants from the patient’s perspective, centered on the navel: RUQ, LUQ, RLQ, LLQ. Spleen = LUQ. Liver mostly RUQ.

Trauma + quadrant pain: which organs live there, and is the risk bleed or leak?

Perfusion basics — key

Blood is not just red fluid. RBCs (erythrocytes) carry hemoglobin and oxygen (delivery trucks). Bleeding costs oxygen-carrying capacity, not only volume. IV fluids help pressure/volume; they do not carry oxygen — only blood does. WBCs (leukocytes) fight infection (not your acute trauma focus). Platelets clump to clot. Plasma also hauls glucose, hormones, nutrients, CO₂, lactic acid, and other waste.

Pump – pipes – fluid NREMT

Pump = heart. Pipes = vessels. Fluid = blood volume.

Any one fails → BP drops → less perfusion → cells lose oxygen.

Systolic (top): pressure when the left ventricle contracts and sends blood into the body. That is the number you use in emergencies.

Diastolic (bottom): resting pressure while the heart fills. Much less important prehospital.

Adult target: systolic ≥ 90 mmHg. Under 90 is hypotension and a shock flag, plus poor-perfusion signs (ALOC, cool/clammy, delayed cap refill, tachycardia).

One vital set is a snapshot. Two or more is a trend (better, worse, same). Document at least two sets.

Unstable / critical: reassess every 5 minutes (BP, HR, RR, SpO₂, mental status).

Stable: every 15 minutes.

Perfusion and shock NREMT

Perfusion = good stuff in (O₂, nutrients) and trash out (CO₂, acids, junk).

Poor perfusion makes cells doubly unhappy: not enough fuel, too much waste (lactic acid if they go anaerobic). Oxygen delivery is the more urgent talk.

Hypoperfusion = shock. Same word in EMS.

Every shock type is a pump, pipe, and/or fluid fail.

Pump: heart too weak or squeezed — massive MI, CHF, tension pneumothorax, pericardial tamponade.

Pipes: vasodilation (too wide) — fire hose on a kitchen sink. Distributive: sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic.

Fluid: not enough in the tank — hemorrhage, GI bleed, severe dehydration.

“Significant” blood loss is often ~500–1000 mL, but speed and the patient matter. Volume loss matters; how fast it comes off decides whether they collapse. Slow problems: the body can compensate. Fast, big problems: decompensation.

Compensation NREMT

Fight-or-flight (sympathetic): faster HR, vasoconstriction to raise BP, faster RR for O₂ in / CO₂ out.

Compensated shock: shock signs with a still-okay BP.

Decompensated shock: hypotension plus clear poor perfusion.

Same idea as fever: slow climb to 105 °F can be survivable; a sudden jump to 102 °F can seize a kid.

One RBC path (know cold) NREMT

Right atrium (deox via SVC/IVC) → tricuspid → right ventricle → pulmonic valve → pulmonary arteries → pulmonary capillaries at alveoli (CO₂ out, O₂ in) → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral/bicuspid → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body.

Left-side fail → backup LV → LA → pulmonary veins → lung capillaries → pulmonary edema, SOB, crackles.

Right-side fail → backup RV → RA → SVC/IVC → JVD, peripheral edema.

Endocrine and nervous overview — key

Brain is the control center. It sends commands to other systems, especially endocrine.

Adrenal glands and epinephrine NREMT

Adrenal glands: small, triangle-shaped, sit on top of the kidneys. Ad-renal and epi-nephrine both mean above the kidneys.

They make adrenaline = epinephrine. The epinephrine in an EpiPen is the same adrenaline your body makes in fight or flight.

Receptors (must memorize) NREMT

Alpha → vasoconstriction → ↑ blood pressure.

Beta-1 (“1 heart”) → ↑ heart rate and ↑ contractility (squeezes harder).

Beta-2 (“2 lungs”) → bronchodilation → bronchi/bronchioles open → easier airflow.

Epinephrine hits all three: alpha, beta-1, and beta-2. That combination is classic fight or flight — key for shock and anaphylaxis.

Other meds NREMT

Albuterol: beta-2 agonist → bronchodilation. Used for asthma (bronchoconstriction).

Beta blockers (for hypertension): block beta-1 → lower HR and contractility → lower BP.

Baroreceptors and chemoreceptors NREMT

Baroreceptors = pressure receptors in the aorta and carotid arteries. They check blood pressure every heartbeat. When BP drops, they alert the brain → sympathetic / fight or flight → epinephrine release.

Chemoreceptors = chemistry sensors. They monitor O₂, CO₂, pH, sugar, and help the brain adjust breathing and circulation.

Regardless of why BP is low (bleeding, heart failure, anaphylaxis, massive vasodilation), the first body response is the same:

- Alpha: vasoconstriction → ↑ BP

- Beta-1: ↑ HR and contractility → better cardiac output

- Beta-2: bronchodilation → more oxygen in

Universal shock presentation you will learn: cool, pale, clammy, tachycardic, possibly low BP (late sign).

Sympathetic vs parasympathetic NREMT

Both are autonomic (involuntary).

Sympathetic (fight or flight): ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, bronchodilation, vasoconstriction; decreased blood flow to GI tract and reproductive system.

Parasympathetic (rest and digest / feed and breed): brings the body back down after stress; slows HR; restores blood flow to GI and reproductive systems; promotes digestion.

Nervous system splits NREMT

CNS = brain + spinal cord.

PNS = all other nerves. Motor nerves send signals out to muscles (movement). Sensory nerves send information in to the brain (data input).

PNS splits:

- Somatic: voluntary control of skeletal muscles

- Autonomic: involuntary control of heart, smooth muscle, glands — sympathetic and parasympathetic

Brain (what matters to you) NREMT

Brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla): breathing, heart rate, blood pressure. Most critical for life; deeply protected.

Cerebrum: largest; personality, judgment, language, memory, sensory processing.

- Frontal: personality, judgment, motor control, speech production (Broca’s area)

- Parietal: sensation and spatial awareness

- Occipital: vision

- Temporal: hearing, understanding words, some memory

Cerebellum: coordination and balance; less tested at EMT level.

Stroke: signs match the area that lost blood flow (left frontal / Broca → speech problems). Your job: recognize stroke, use a stroke scale, rapid transport. Hospital CT decides ischemic vs hemorrhagic treatment.

Body systems overview — key

Cerebrum: speech, learning, conscious thought, sight, hearing, memory of events. Altered mental status, confusion, slurred speech, not oriented → think cerebrum.

Cerebellum: balance, muscle coordination, posture, “muscle memory.” Issues → ataxia, poor walking, uncoordinated movement.

Brainstem: involuntary life functions — heart rate, breathing, temperature, digestion, sleep–wake, consciousness level. Damage is immediately life-threatening (apnea, abnormal respirations, loss of consciousness).

Hypothalamus: body temperature (and other autonomic work). Brain injury or illness can cause fever or temp dysregulation.

RAS: gatekeeper of consciousness. Filters background sensory info. Trauma, stroke, hypoxia, toxins → altered or lost consciousness.

Spinal cord NREMT

Electrical highway. Motor: brain → cord → muscles → movement. Sensory: body → cord → brain → sensation.

Cord injury: paralysis below the level (motor blocked) and numbness/loss of feeling below (sensory blocked). Protect the spine. Check motor AND sensory below the suspected level.

Autonomic NREMT

Sympathetic (fight or flight): ↑ HR, ↑ RR, dilated pupils, pale/cool/clammy, ↓ digestion.

Parasympathetic (rest and digest): ↓ HR, relaxed/normal state, digestion active.

Use this to read shock, stress, and medical vitals.

Homeostasis (adults this recap) NREMT

HR 60–100. RR 12–20. Blood glucose ~80–120 mg/dL. SpO₂ 94–99% on room air.

You judge how far from these ranges they are and how fast they are drifting.

Neck vessels NREMT

Carotids: oxygenated blood to the brain; pulse check in unresponsive adults.

Jugulars: deoxygenated blood from brain back to the heart. External jugulars are superficial.

JVD (bulging): blood backup toward the heart — right-sided heart problems or blocked venous return. Think right heart failure, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, other obstructive issues. Inspect semi-upright.

Collapsed/flat neck veins (groove): low volume — hemorrhage, severe dehydration.

Skin and burns NREMT

Epidermis: outer tough layer. Dermis: nerves, vessels, hair follicles. Then subcutaneous fat, muscle, bone.

Superficial (1st): epidermis only — red, painful, no blisters.

Partial thickness (2nd): epidermis + part of dermis — blisters, very painful.

Full thickness (3rd): all layers — white, charred, or leathery; little pain in the worst area (nerves gone).

Paper-cut epidermis may not hurt much. Dermis hurts a lot — that is where the nerve endings live.

CSF NREMT

Surrounds brain and spinal cord. Insulation, shock absorption, protection (crash-barrel / amniotic-fluid idea). After head trauma, watch for CSF leak from ears or nose.

GI NREMT

Small intestine: nutrient absorption. Sections (nice-to-know): duodenum, jejunum, ileum.

Peristalsis: smooth-muscle waves that move food. No heart-like pump.

Hollow organ rupture (appendix): contents spill → peritonitis. Can paralyze peristalsis → severe bloating, distension, pain, backup. Hospital may use an NG tube. Field clues: sudden pain change, rigid/guarding abdomen, distension, very ill. RLQ that generalizes → think appendicitis/rupture.

Pancreas and liver NREMT

Pancreas: deep mid–upper abdomen, straddles RUQ/LUQ. Epigastric pain radiating to the back. Enzymes for starches and proteins. Insulin ↓ BG. Glucagon ↑ BG. Failures = diabetes, hypo, hyper.

Liver: large solid organ, RUQ. Detoxifies blood. Makes clotting factors (liver disease → bleed easier). Blood-saturated — major internal bleed in abdominal trauma. Watch shock after RUQ blunt trauma.

Sat 11:27 Review of human body systems — key

Clotting factors are puzzle pieces. If even one is missing, the body cannot form an effective clot. Hemophilia is an inherited missing-factor disorder (Hemophilia A is factor VIII). You do not need the number on scene. Minor trauma can mean serious, prolonged bleeding. Direct pressure still matters, but bleeding may restart when you let go. You will not carry factor replacement. Ask about a bleeding disorder and factor meds, hold pressure, and move them early.

Liver: makes bile (fat digestion), stores glucose as glycogen and dumps it back when blood sugar drops, detoxifies blood, and makes clotting factors. Gallbladder stores bile and releases it into the small intestine after fatty meals. After gallbladder removal they lose on-demand storage; fatty meals can cause nausea, vomiting, gas, bloating, indigestion. Right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain can be liver or gallbladder. Pain after fatty meals plus nausea/vomiting and RUQ tenderness → think biliary.

Stomach: acid and enzymes do the bulk of digestion. Small intestine: nutrient absorption; also gets bile from the liver and enzymes from the pancreas. Large intestine (colon): water absorption and stool; without that you get chronic diarrhea. Appendix sits near the start of the colon in the right lower quadrant (RLQ). Appendicitis classically is RLQ pain. For belly calls you sort solid versus hollow, quadrant, and function.

Lymphatic system is the sewer: waste, toxins, damaged cells, bacteria. Nodes swell when they are full of waste and broken-down bacteria (infection). No heart-like pump; movement, muscle work, and breathing move lymph. Swollen tender neck nodes support infection. They are not usually the life threat.

Endocrine uses hormones in the blood. Hypothalamus bridges nervous (electrical) and endocrine (hormonal). Pituitary is the master gland: hypothalamus → pituitary → other glands. Adrenals sit on the kidneys and release epinephrine (adrenaline) for fight-or-flight, shock, and stress. Pancreas makes insulin so cells can use glucose. Type 1: no insulin. Type 2: insulin resistance. Thyroid (neck) controls metabolism; hypothyroidism is low metabolism, weight gain, fatigue — usually history, not a 911 emergency. Four parathyroids on the thyroid regulate calcium; calcium is needed for muscle contraction, especially the heart. You treat the effects of too much or too little hormone, not the hormone itself.

Kidneys filter blood and decide keep versus pee. They control fluid balance, blood pressure (through volume), and pH (water and some solutes; lungs handle carbon dioxide). One vessel in; cleaned blood back out; urine down the ureters to the bladder. Too much water → more urine. Dehydrated → less urine. Extra sugar beyond storage can spill into urine (uncontrolled diabetes). Kidneys plus lungs regulate pH.

After airway, breathing, and circulation (ABC) life threats, treat quality-of-life injuries: hands, feet, face, genitals. They may not kill, but they can wreck function, fertility, vision, or appearance. Female reproductive care is a major topic: pregnancy, labor, newborn, postpartum.

Cells need oxygen plus glucose to make energy (ATP), heat, carbon dioxide, and water. With enough oxygen they use aerobic metabolism (plenty of energy; wastes are carbon dioxide and water). Shock is hypoperfusion: not enough blood flow and oxygen to tissues. Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism: less energy, lactic acid, acidosis, organ failure, and hypothermia. Shock is cellular energy failure, not just low blood pressure. You restore perfusion, give oxygen, and keep them warm with blankets no matter the outdoor temperature.

Sat 1:01 EMT equipment and patient movement — key

This chapter is equipment (gurneys, backboards, bariatric gear) and movement (lifting, carrying, rolling, log roll). Tests hit when to use or skip a device, safe moves for the patient and your back, spinal motion restriction (SMR), and when you call medical control.

Wheeled ambulance stretcher = gurney. Manual or powered. Powered is heavier (about 40–145 lb) but cuts lifting strain. Very top-heavy with a patient on it. Head end is usually heavier. Load headfirst so they face the back windows. Always lock it into the ambulance floor bracket.

Do not take a gurney on stairs, off-road or rough ground (dirt, desert, fields), or angled slopes that can tip (driveway edges, curbs at an angle). Use at least two of you (front and back) for turns, ramps, and slopes.

Supine (flat on the back) is preferred for shock — helps perfusion and blood pressure. Position of comfort is when no set medical position is required: sitting, semi-Fowler, or side-lying if it is safe and protocol allows.

Universal shock treatment: high-flow oxygen, keep them warm, lay them supine, rapid transport. “O2 + warm + supine + go fast.”

After a call, strip disposable sheets, disinfect, re-sheet. Some agencies bleach and reuse washable linen.

Even “walking wounded” ride on the gurney, upright if needed but strapped. If they crash mid-transport, you already have them on the stretcher.

Backboard = long spine board, about 6–7 ft, usually plastic. It is a full-body splint for the spinal column. Often left at the hospital; agencies stencil their name so they can collect, disinfect, and reuse. More holes = better strap placement.

Strap order: X strap over chest/torso, strap over hips, one or two over the legs, head last with blocks and tape/straps.

Old SMR: almost any big trauma got boarded “just in case.” Current SMR is selective: symptoms, neuro status, mechanism, mental status / reliability.

Strongly consider SMR/backboard: conscious trauma with neck or back pain, numbness, tingling, weakness, or paralysis; unconscious with a significant mechanism (bad motor-vehicle crash, high fall) who cannot report pain; poor historian (intoxicated, head-injured, unreliable) — they wince when you press the spine but deny pain → treat as possible spinal injury and board.

If they are wider than the board, ask whether boarding helps or harms. Forcing them on can worsen alignment or safety — use other stabilization, call medical control, and use a bariatric gurney for transport when you have one.

Combative or claustrophobic: explain and reassure first. Head injury or severe agitation may need paramedic sedation per protocol. If they cannot tolerate the board or it is unsafe, call medical control — mechanism, symptoms, behavior — and ask for orders (modify SMR, off the board, sedate, or restrain). That keeps you in the standard of care.

EMS backs get hurt a lot. Powered gurneys exist to prevent that. Lift with your legs, not your back. Shoulders over pelvis — no twisting while you lift. Load close. Feet shoulder-width. Bend at hips and knees, not the waist. Do not lean out and lift far from your center.

Power grip: palms up, hands about 10 inches apart, full wrap, fingers at a similar angle. Stronger and safer than spread fingers or an overhand grab.

Roll when you can (gurney or other wheels). Ask a conscious patient their weight; estimate if they are unconscious. Too heavy or awkward → request more people or lifting devices.

Log roll moves a suspected spine patient from the ground onto a board without twisting. Book four-person: one at the head holds manual c-spine (cervical spine) and commands the count; three along the side at shoulders/chest, hips, and legs do the roll. Collar first if you can. Roll as one unit onto the side, slide the board, roll back onto the board. Secure torso, hips, legs, then head last while the head holder stays until it is secured. This is a tested team skill station. The head person leads.

State rules say what must be on every ambulance (gurney, splints, and the rest). Agencies add optional gear by cost, preference, and medical director. Stryker (yellow) and Ferno (red) are field names, not the test.

Sat 1:32 EMS Patient Moving and Equipment Guide — key

Protect the patient (airway, breathing, circulation, spine when it fits) and protect your crew. Do not be a hero. Call for more people when the weight or the space is risky.

Two-person crews at the example agency handle about 250 lb. Above that you call for help — fire, extra unit, or facility staff. A lift assist from dispatch is extra people, not a second ambulance. At a hospital, nursing home, or care facility, ask staff to help you move.

Bariatric (very large) gurneys are wider, not just stronger. A standard gurney may already hold about 750 lb. Width is the problem. Bariatric gurneys may go to about 1200 lb. Keep the gurney as low as you can. It is top-heavy with a large patient. Lower is more stable.

Two loading setups: a lift tray (gurney rolls onto a rear metal tray that lifts like a truck gate, then slides in) or a ramp plus powered winch (ramp slides out, cable pulls the gurney up while you stabilize and guide). Bariatric units are common on planned interfacility jobs when weight is known. They are rare on time-critical 911 work because they can take 30+ minutes. You weigh wait-for-gear versus move-now with what you have.

Diamond carry on a backboard uses four people: head, feet, one each side. If one slips, three still hold. Two-person carry is riskier — short distance and few people only.

Stair chair: conscious, awake, no suspected spine injury, cannot walk the stairs, no usable elevator. Not for unconscious or spine-concern patients. Unconscious or spine concern on stairs: backboard, strongest at head and feet, feet first down. A backboard can be just a carry tool even when you are not treating a spine.

One team leader calls the count: ready, then 1-2-3. That stops one person lifting while others wait, or others lowering while you still hold. On a backboard the person holding the head is the leader and calls every move.

Emergency move: seconds, scene or patient will kill them (car or building on fire, pulseless in a burning car). Get them out now even if the spine is not fully protected. Urgent move: serious but not seconds — about one minute. You still usually skip full scene immobilization; more controlled than a drag. Non-urgent: scene and patient stable, breathing okay, not in shock. Plan, least lifting, fewest moves, crew safety and comfort.

Portable folding stretcher: light, wilderness or hard-to-reach; often special rescue, not every unit. Flexible (Reeves-type): bendy plastic for confined space, trench, or crevice; you can hoist them while supported. Seated extrication device (short backboard/vest): seated in a vehicle with possible spine injury, conscious, breathing okay, scene stable. Rarely used. A crash bad enough for spine damage is often urgent — skip it and go rapid extrication and backboard.

Vacuum mattress: giant beanbag you vacuum down so it matches their shape. Good for elderly kyphosis (severe spinal curve) when a flat hard board may worsen their baseline. Often not on the truck. Ask if flat on a board helps or harms. If unsure, pad the head and neck with towels or blankets and call medical control.

Basket (Stokes) stretcher: backboard with raised sides for technical rope and water rescue, mountain or trail hoist. Sides add lateral support.

Scoop stretcher: splits in half (sometimes four pieces). Slide under and reconnect. Ideal for elderly suspected hip fracture or anyone you do not want to roll. Scoop with little hip movement, then to a backboard or gurney. Without a scoop you may have to roll and pull the injured side — painful but sometimes the only option. Elderly hip fractures have about 50% 12-month mortality from immobility (clots, strokes, infections, deconditioning), not just the break.

Neonate is birth to 1 month. An isolette is a heated protective box that mounts on a standard gurney with the mattress off. It keeps the newborn warm. Air medical teams (flight nurse and flight medic) may fly the baby by airplane, then you meet on the tarmac, load the isolette, and drive to a children’s hospital or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). You provide the vehicle and gurney. You still write a patient care report (PCR). Clinical decisions stay with the specialty team.

Match the tool: backboard for unconscious, spine concern, stairs, rapid extrication. Stair chair for conscious, no spine, stairs only. Scoop for hip / do not roll. Basket, flexible, or portable for technical, confined, or wilderness. Vacuum mattress for curved spines if you have one.

  • Do this: Name why a bariatric gurney is different and where you set the height. You did it right if wider (capacity about 1200 lb vs about 750 lb on a standard) and as low as possible because it is top-heavy.
  • Do this: Pick stair chair versus backboard for stairs. You did it right if conscious, no spine, no elevator is chair, and unconscious or spine concern is board, feet first, strongest at the ends.
  • Do this: Label a car-on-fire pull, a one-minute serious move, and a stable planned carry. You did it right if emergency, urgent, and non-urgent, and you said the first two skip full scene immobilization.
  • Do this: Name scoop versus vacuum mattress versus Stokes basket. You did it right if scoop is split-and-slide for hip/no-roll, vacuum is beanbag for kyphosis, and basket is raised sides for rope or water.

Sat 2:01 EMS patient positioning and safety — key

Shock: supine (flat on the back). Do not elevate the legs. Trendelenburg is out.

Pregnant ≥20 weeks: left lateral. Flat on the back lets the uterus, amniotic fluid, and fetus squeeze the aorta and inferior vena cava (IVC) against the spine. That cuts circulation and drops blood pressure — supine hypotensive syndrome. Left side offloads more than the right. In the ambulance, left side also faces the bench so you can watch face, breathing, and color.

Unresponsive, breathing well, no suspected spine injury: recovery position (on the side). Protects from tongue falling back and from secretions or vomit. Most unresponsive patients cannot breathe well and need to stay supine for airway work and a bag-valve mask (BVM).

Seizure: post-ictal and breathing well → recovery position. Not breathing well or you need airway support → supine. Position follows airway and breathing.

Respiratory distress / dyspnea: Fowler (sitting up) or High Fowler (very upright). Semi-Fowler is partly reclined. Sit them up if they tolerate it and it is not contraindicated (spine concern, shock that needs supine).

No strong medical reason for a set position: position of comfort. Ask if they want to sit up or lie back more.

Prone (face down): never transport an unconscious patient prone. A conscious, stable person who asks for prone is rare — only if fully awake, breathing well, and you can watch airway and breathing the whole time. Still not preferred. Avoid prone whenever airway or mental status is a worry.

Restraints: only if they are actively trying to harm themselves or others. Before you call it behavioral, check hypoxia (low oxygen), hypoglycemia (low blood sugar), head injury, and other medical or trauma causes.

How to restrain: ideal team is five people — one per extremity plus one at the head. Law enforcement often helps. Supine only. Prone restraint is prohibited (positional asphyxia; you cannot watch the airway). After you restrain, keep watching airway, breathing, circulation (ABCs) and mental status. They are still your patient.

Document the situation, what you did, and how they did afterward. Restrain without writing the combativeness and it can look like you kidnapped them.

Standard Precautions / body substance isolation (BSI): treat every patient as infectious. Wear the right personal protective equipment (PPE). After each call, discard disposables and clean and disinfect even if it looks clean. Clean like the next patient is a newborn or your grandmother.

Lifting: ask “Are we strong enough?” and “Do we need more help?” If the team is at the limit, get help — do not wait for someone to admit it. Poor judgment can drop the patient or hurt you or your partner.

Manual gurney: the head end is heavier and lifts higher because of the wheels. Put your stronger (often taller) person at the head.

Water / technical rescue: special teams often use a basket (Stokes) stretcher. A backboard floats up under a patient in water and can sit inside a basket for a hoist. Scoop stretcher: hip fracture or anyone you do not want to roll — scoop from both sides. Local kits vary.

Exam-level device jobs: stair chair for confined spaces or weak walkers; gurney for standard transport; backboard mainly for extrication and some trauma; scoop for pelvic/hip injury with little movement.

First fix immediately life-threatening problems. Then supportive care, comfort, and safe transport. When it is no longer emergent, stay on comfort, safety, documentation, and professionalism.

  • Do this: Place a pregnant patient past 20 weeks and name the syndrome. You did it right if left lateral to prevent supine hypotensive syndrome (uterus on aorta and IVC).
  • Do this: Place unresponsive-but-breathing with no spine concern, then a seizure who is not breathing well. You did it right if recovery position, then supine for airway.
  • Do this: Place dyspnea vs position of comfort vs prone. You did it right if Fowler/High Fowler if tolerated, comfort if no medical reason, and never prone if unconscious.
  • Do this: Say when you may restrain, how many people, and which position. You did it right if active self-harm or harm to others, five people, supine only, then watch ABCs.
  • Do this: Name the three restraint documentation pieces. You did it right if situation, intervention, and outcome — without the why it can look like kidnapping.
  • Do this: Frame a lift and a manual gurney. You did it right if “Are we strong enough?” and strongest at the head (heavier, higher).
  • Do this: Match scoop, backboard in water, and stair chair. You did it right if scoop for hip/no-roll, backboard floats under then can go in a basket, stair chair for tight spaces or weak walkers.

Sat 2:47 EMT Patient Assessment Fundamentals — key

Five parts, every call: scene size-up → primary assessment → history taking → secondary assessment (physical exam) → ongoing reassessment. Primary is the biggest National Registry chunk.

Scene size-up happens before you touch anyone. Body substance isolation / personal protective equipment (BSI / PPE): gloves at least, eyes/face as needed. Then scene safety — you, crew, bystanders, patient. A safe scene can turn unsafe. Call police, fire, utility, or hazardous materials (hazmat) to make it safe. Do not rush in.

Mechanism of injury (MOI) = trauma: what hit the body. Predicts hidden injuries (brain, chest, abdomen). Nature of illness (NOI) = medical problem (chest pain, shortness of breath). Real calls can be both (heart attack then crash; seizure then fall).

Count patients. Many people with the same complaint → think environment / hazmat (gas, carbon monoxide (CO), chemicals). Do not become patient #9.

Ask early for extra help: more ambulances, fire, hazmat, rescue, advanced life support (ALS) / paramedics, air medical, bariatric, utilities. On tests you are a basic emergency medical technician (EMT) on an ambulance. You request ALS. You do not assume they are already there.

Consider cervical-spine (c-spine) based on MOI. Think: backboard with the gurney?

Skip BSI or scene safety on a skills sheet = automatic fail.

Field impression = your best professional guess from history, signs, symptoms, vitals, exam. You do not diagnose. Universal care first (not breathing → ventilate; massive bleed → stop it). Specific care after the impression (suspected heart attack / myocardial infarction (MI) → aspirin, maybe nitro per protocol; overdose → Narcan; anaphylaxis → EpiPen).

Signs = objective (you and a partner can both see or measure). Symptoms = what they tell you. Pain 8/10 is still a symptom.

Primary = find and treat life threats now. Flow: general impression → level of consciousness → airway → breathing → circulation → rapid exam if trauma or sick-looking → priority and transport. Find a life threat, treat it, then move on.

General impression starts the moment you see them. Maybe dead? Check a pulse now. Sick or not sick is only a starting guess.

Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive (AVPU). Pain: sternal rub, trapezius pinch, nail-bed pressure. If Alert, check alert and oriented times four (A&O x4): person, place, time, event. A&O x4 and acting reasonably usually has capacity. Not fully oriented or confused → altered mental status → often implied consent. Use their baseline.

Patent airway = open and clear. Talking or crying = patent. Open with head-tilt chin-lift if no spine concern; jaw thrust if trauma. Gurgling → suction. Snoring → tongue. High-pitched → partial block. Fix airway before anything else.

Breathing only after airway is open. Adult worry: rate > 28 (tachypnea) or < 8 (bradypnea). Slow, especially < 8 → ventilate. Fast needs more workup. Irregular chaotic breathing can mean brainstem / head injury. Check depth (tidal volume / chest rise), work of breathing, pulse oximetry (SpO₂) goal 94–99% in context. Hypoventilation or arrest → ventilate. Distressed but still ventilating → oxygen per protocol.

Pulse: conscious adult/child → radial. Unconscious adult/child → carotid. Infant (<1 year) → brachial. Weak or absent radial while they are alive → likely low blood pressure (BP). Pale, cool, clammy → often shock. Capillary refill especially in kids; normal ≤ 2 seconds. Treat shock, severe external bleeding, and no pulse now.

More patients than resources → triage. Count, sort by severity, treat critical people who can still survive. Mass casualty incident (MCI): shorter notes; you cannot care for everyone at once.

  • Do this: Separate MOI from NOI and name a both-medical-and-trauma call. You did it right if MOI is what hit the body and NOI is the illness, plus MI→crash or seizure→fall.
  • Do this: Say when you request ALS on a test. You did it right if you are a basic EMT and you request ALS instead of assuming paramedics are already there.
  • Do this: Contrast a field impression with a diagnosis, then universal vs specific care. You did it right if unofficial guess after clues, doctors diagnose, and ventilate/bleed-control come before aspirin/Narcan/Epi.
  • Do this: Call 8/10 pain a sign or a symptom. You did it right if symptom — it is still what they report.
  • Do this: Walk primary assessment order and the life-threat rule. You did it right if impression, AVPU, airway, breathing, circulation, rapid exam if needed, priority/transport — treat the threat before moving on.

EMT pharmacology fundamentals — key

You do not need every drug on earth. You master a short EMT list and give them only when state scope and your medical director’s protocols allow it. The medical director can say no even if the state says you may.

For each drug lock indication, contraindication, dose, route, and major side effects. Test world: you have the EMT list and you call medical control when required.

Words NREMT

Pharmacology: science of drugs used to prevent or treat disease, relieve pain, and manage symptoms.

Pharmacodynamics: what the drug does to the body — how it hits receptors and makes a therapeutic effect.

Agonist: binds and stimulates the normal response. Epinephrine is an alpha agonist (vasoconstriction), beta-1 agonist (↑ heart rate and contractility — 1 heart), and beta-2 agonist (bronchodilation — 2 lungs).

Antagonist: binds and blocks, often the opposite effect. Naloxone (Narcan) is a narcotic receptor antagonist — reverses opioid overdose.

Pharmacokinetics is the life cycle: onset (admin → starts working — drives when you reassess), peak (max effect), duration (how long it lasts), elimination (out of the system). Route matters: intravenous (IV) is immediate; by mouth (PO) pill is 20–30 min.

Indication: reason to give or do it. Contraindication: reason not to. For EMT meds you treat contraindications as absolute unless a doctor overrides. Side effects are unintended — headache can be mild; hypotension can be dangerous.

Generic name is not owned, usually lowercase (naloxone, epinephrine). Brand is owned and capitalized (Narcan, EpiPen). National Registry wants generic.

Routes and forms NREMT

Enteral goes through the gut. PO swallow is slow (20–30 min). Sublingual (SL) under the tongue is very vascular and fast — nitroglycerin. Rectal (PR) is not an EMT route in this system.

Parenteral is anything not through the gut. IV is immediate (paramedic). Intraosseous (IO) into bone marrow acts like IV (critical, e.g. arrest). Inhalation: metered-dose inhaler (MDI), nebulized albuterol. Intranasal (IN): naloxone, rapid. Intramuscular (IM): EpiPen, lateral thigh, moderate/fast. Subcutaneous (SC): slower, no current EMT meds. Transdermal patches: too slow — not EMT emergency drugs.

Forms you see: tablets (aspirin), capsules (not much you carry), solutions (EpiPen fluid), suspensions (old charcoal — mostly gone), MDI (same dose each spray), gels (oral glucose).

Five rights NREMT

Right patient (their prescribed med, or your box drug on the approved list and allowed). Right medication. Right dose (know fixed EMT doses cold). Right route. Right time (expiration; hold time on an EpiPen; how often you may repeat nitro).

Document name, dose, route, time, and how they responded.

Oral glucose NREMT

Sugar gel for hypoglycemia (blood sugar < 80 mg/dL) if they are conscious and can protect the airway (follow commands, swallow). One tube PO / buccal. Tubes often 15 g (range 15–25 g) — say “one tube.” Recheck sugar.

Do not give if they are unconscious or cannot protect the airway — aspiration. Many arrivals are already out → contraindicated; paramedics/ED use IV dextrose. Test answer is oral glucose, not juice.

Aspirin (ASA) NREMT

Stops platelets from clumping so a clot does not grow. It does not dissolve clots.

Indication: chest pain you think is cardiac / heart attack (acute coronary syndrome), not “any chest pain.”

Allergy = do not give unless a doctor orders otherwise. Other relative issues (liver, bleeding, some asthma) → call medical control. Avoid in kids (Reye’s) — this is mostly an adult MI tool.

Tablets 81 mg. Adult 162–324 mg PO, chewed then swallowed. Test: 160–325 mg PO, chewed. If they already took some, subtract and often top off to 324 mg with medical direction.

Nitroglycerin NREMT

Vasodilator. Widens vessels including coronaries. More blood and oxygen to heart muscle. Pain should drop if ischemia is the cause.

0.4 mg SL tablet or spray. Up to 3 doses, 5 minutes apart. Keep it under the tongue — do not swallow right away. Indication: suspected cardiac chest pain and it is prescribed or standing orders.

No nitro if systolic blood pressure (BP) < 100 (you need triple-digit systolic), sexual-enhancement meds in the last 24 hours (Viagra, Cialis, Levitra; generics often end in -fil — combined vasodilators crash BP), or head injury.

Side effects: severe headache (common), hypotension (most dangerous), burn under the tongue, nausea.

Check BP before every dose. Repeat only if SBP ≥ 100 and pain is still there, every 5 min, max 3. Stop if BP < 100 or pain is gone. Give one, then move — do not sit on scene for all three. Wear gloves — nitro absorbs through your skin.

Epinephrine NREMT

Sympathomimetic — fight-or-flight. Alpha: squeeze vessels, raise BP. Beta-1: ↑ HR and squeeze. Beta-2: open airways.

Indication is anaphylaxis, not any allergy. Anaphylaxis = allergic reaction in ≥ 2 systems (skin: hives/itch/swelling; respiratory: wheeze, dyspnea, airway swell; circulatory: low BP, shock). Hives + wheeze = anaphylaxis. Hives only = not.

Adult auto-injector 0.3 mg IM lateral thigh. Junior 0.15 mg IM. Blue to the sky, orange to the thigh, click, hold ~3 seconds. Can go through one layer of clothes.

Test world: must be prescribed to the patient unless your protocols let you use agency stock. Do not delay true anaphylaxis.

Numbers NREMT

Glucose: < 80 mg/dL, 1 tube, awake + airway. Aspirin: 81 mg tabs, 162–324 (test 160–325) chewed PO. Nitro: 0.4 mg SL, q5 min, max 3, SBP ≥ 100. Epi: 0.3 mg adult / 0.15 mg peds IM thigh, hold ~3 sec. PO pill ~20–30 min. IV immediate.

  • Do this: Define pharmacodynamics in one line. You did it right if you said what the drug does to the body / receptors → therapeutic effect.

Sun 4:46 EMT medication and protocols — key

You review core EMT drugs, when you give them, when you hold them, and what happens if you step outside scope. Once a drug is in, you cannot take it back.

Epinephrine (epi) NREMT

Only for anaphylaxis — not a mild allergy and not a simple asthma attack. You can justify epi if you can call it anaphylaxis. If you cannot, do not hunt epi orders.

Look for airway trouble (face / tongue / throat swell, wheeze in an allergic story), shock (low blood pressure (BP), poor perfusion), or more than one body system (skin + breathing + circulation).

What it does: alpha receptors squeeze vessels → BP up. Beta-1 raises heart rate and squeeze. Beta-2 opens airways (bronchodilation).

Hold or be very careful: very high BP (epi raises BP → stroke / bleed risk; true anaphylaxis is usually low, not high BP); hypothermia (that heart is extra sensitive — even rough movement can arrest; epi can push them into arrest); myocardial infarction (MI) / known heart attack (epi makes the heart work harder); simple asthma (use albuterol, not epi).

Naloxone (Narcan) NREMT

Works only on opiates / opioids (heroin, fentanyl, morphine, oxycodone, Vicodin). It will not reverse alcohol, benzodiazepines (Valium, Xanax), or other non-opioid sedatives.

It is an antagonist: similar shape, binds the same receptors, blocks instead of firing them. It reverses respiratory depression, central nervous system (CNS) depression, and low BP from opioids.

Intranasal (IN) is most common — spray in the nose, absorbs through nasal mucosa into blood. It is not a lung drug. It works even if they are not breathing. Intramuscular (IM) is usually lateral thigh, like an EpiPen. Intravenous (IV) is paramedic in most systems.

No single standard EMT dose to memorize — it varies by route and packaging. Follow the auto-injector or prefilled IN device and protocol.

You want breathing rate and depth better, then mental status better. Side effects: nausea, vomiting, they can wake agitated or combative. Scene safety as they come up. Prefer IN when you can so you skip needles (HIV, hepatitis C risk in IV drug users).

Field picture: low BP, low heart rate (HR), slow / shallow or no breaths, down mental status. Pinpoint pupils are classic but not always there. A “trial” that works points to opioid; no change means other cause or a heavy load that needs another dose. Very safe if no opioid is on board.

Albuterol and inhaled delivery NREMT

Albuterol is for asthma / bronchoconstriction / bronchospasm. Beta-2 agonist → bronchodilation. Less whole-body punch than epi, but the right tool for asthma.

Metered-dose inhaler (MDI): usually their prescribed inhaler. You assist if protocol allows. Timing is the whole game — spray as they inhale so it reaches lung. Spray off-time coats the mouth and does nothing. Kids: spacer / aerochamber holds a cloud so they do not need perfect timing.

Small-volume nebulizer (SVN): more often what EMS carries. Run 6 liters per minute (L/min) oxygen through the cup with liquid albuterol. Continuous mist. They breathe again and again — higher, deeper dose than one MDI puff.

Oral glucose NREMT

For suspected hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) when mental status is down but they can still swallow and protect the airway.

Do not give if they are unconscious or cannot protect the airway / follow commands / swallow. Thick gel in the lungs is an airway emergency (aspiration).

Your EMT list and scope NREMT

This recap’s EMT list: aspirin for suspected cardiac chest pain (per protocol); nitroglycerin for cardiac chest pain (usually assist their prescription); oral glucose if the airway is protected; epinephrine for anaphylaxis; naloxone for opioid overdose; albuterol / bronchodilator for asthma (MDI or nebulizer).

You are limited to that list. Over-the-counter drugs (Tylenol, Benadryl) are generally not yours unless they are written into your protocols. Paramedics and nurses have broader lists by setting and state law.

Paramedics: bigger list than you, still state-limited. IV, more meds, intubation (a skill most nurses do not have), intraosseous (IO) bone access.

Nurses: generally higher hospital scope than paramedics, broader med authority, skill set depends on unit.

Paramedics in the emergency room (ER): hospital-by-hospital. Some let them work near full scope (IVs, push meds, assessments). Some park them on basic tech work. Class example: a system had to add Tylenol to the hospital paramedic list because they could give fentanyl but not Tylenol as the list was written.

Errors and legal NREMT

You cannot un-give a drug. Harm or death can follow. Career and legal hit too.

If you mess up: take care of the patient first (watch, treat problems, transport). Call medical control now — factual, objective, honest (situation, drug, dose, timing, what happened). Document the same way. Expect follow-up with your agency / medical director — how it happened, how to stop it. Harm or death escalates fast.

Red lines: a drug outside your legal scope (EMT giving a paramedic-only med); something state and protocol clearly forbid. Consequences: discipline or termination, report to the state EMS office, suspend or pull your card, legal action if the patient is hurt.
